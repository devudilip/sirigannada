# Badami Chalukya: verification pass for the ಇಮ್ಮಡಿ ವಿಕ್ರಮಾದಿತ್ಯ story (Vikramaditya II, r. c. 733 to c. 744/746)

Date of pass: 2026-10-15. Companion to `badami-chalukya.md` (§1, §2.1 items 6–8, §2.2, §3 Story C, §5
items 13–17) and to `badami-chalukya-verified.md` (the Pulikeshi pass, whose content is not repeated).
Every item records the edition actually opened, the URL, a verbatim quotation, and a tier
(`inscription` / `scholarship` / `legend` / UNVERIFIED). Nothing here is from memory.

vol. I pt. II, 1896), Indian Antiquary VII (1878) and X (1881), Epigraphia Indica V (1898–99) and

introduction). OCR noise is corrected only where the reading is certain; doubtful spots are marked.

## 0. Corrections to the earlier files, up front

1. **Vakkaleri plates are EI V no. 22** (Kielhorn, pp. 200–205), not no. 25. Kielhorn does not
   translate the Vikramaditya II passage; he refers to Hultzsch, SII I p. 146, which is quoted in §3.
2. **Nandipotavarman was "put to flight", not "slain"**, in Fleet 1896 too: "attacked and put to
   flight the Pallava king Nandipôtavarman who had come out to meet him" (Gazetteer p. 374). The
   companion file §5 item 13 said Fleet 1896 renders it "slew". Wrong; it can be closed.
3. **The Navsari plates do not name Vikramaditya II.** The overlord is "the illustrious
   Vallabhanarendra" (Bhagwanlal's translation; text "Śrīvallabha-narendreṇa"). Mirashi: the suzerain
   "whose name has not been specifically mentioned, but who must have been Vikramāditya II". The
   identification is `scholarship` (sound, from the date), not `inscription`. See §5.
4. **The Navsari date is 739 (Fleet) or 740 (Mirashi).** Fleet: "corresponding, approximately, to
   the 22nd October, A.D. 739". Mirashi: "dated in K. 490 (740 A.C.)" and he dates the raid "about
   739 A.C.". Say "739 or 740" or "c. 739".
5. **Gunda is verified** (Fleet IA X, 1881, and Fleet 1896). His honours were the *patta* (fillet)
   called *mūme-perjjerepu* and the name *Tribhuvanāchārya*. The phrases "Tenkanadesiya sutradhari"
   and "Anikapuravastu-pitamaha" circulating online are garbled forms of titles in Fleet's No. C
   (see §6). "perjjerepu patta" alone is wrong: the patta is *mūme-perjjerepu*.
6. **The Lakshmeshwar record of his 2nd year is Sanskrit, in Old Kannada characters of the 10th
   century, and is a later copy.** Wikipedia calls it a "Kannada" inscription "dated 13 January 735".
   Fleet: "approximately, the 14th January, A.D. 735", and "only a copy of an original record, put on
   the stone after A.D. 967". See §2.
7. **"Three times conquered Kanchi" rests on Fleet's reading of one Old Kannada word, *mūme*.** The
   breakdown into "as prince, as king, and through his son" is Ramesh 1984 via Wikipedia (not opened).
   The Vakkaleri plates record one Kanchi expedition of the king and one led by his son as
   yuvaraja. See §6.
8. **Vijayaditya's "peaceful" reign:** the phrase "seems to have been a peaceful one", attributed to
   Fleet 1896 p. 29 in the companion file §1, was NOT found in the Gazetteer text. Fleet says only
   that, while his grandfather was in the south, Vijayaditya "was employed in maintaining peace and
   order in the home provinces". The "long peaceful reign of four decades" is Wikipedia's. Keep
   "long reign" (`inscription`: 34th year in 730), drop "peaceful" as a sourced claim.
9. **The end of the reign:** Fleet 1896 put the succession in 746 or 747 on the Vakkaleri year (Saka
   679 = 11th year). Pathak's Kendur plates (EI IX, 1907–08) give Saka 672 = 6th year of Kirtivarman
   II and argue the Vakkaleri year must be read 677. That moves Vikramaditya II's death to c. 744/745.
   This is where "733–744" comes from. See §2 and §7.

## 1. His father Vijayaditya, and the prince

Source: Fleet 1896 (Gazetteer pp. 371–374), `scholarship` (Fleet's synthesis of the records he lists).

- "Vinayâditya was succeeded, in A.D. 696, by his son Vijayâditya, 'the sun of victory,' who is
  styled, both in his own records and in the subsequent ones, the 'dear or favourite son' of his
  father."
- On Vijayaditya as prince: "It seems that, while his grandfather was engaged in reducing the
  southern countries, he himself was employed in maintaining peace and order in the home provinces.
  ... He assisted his father in a campaign to the north, and, pushing on further to the north even
  than his father, there acquired for him the signs of the rivers Gaṅgâ and Yamunâ. On one occasion,
  he was led into an ambush and taken prisoner by his enemies; but even then he contrived to
  tranquillise anarchical disturbances in his own territory, and, without any assistance, to escape
  and establish his power over the whole of his own dominions. He built the great temple of the god
  Śiva under the name of Vijayêśvara, now known as Saṅgamêśvara, at Paṭṭadakal."
- Long reign, dated: Lakshmeshwar (copy) "Saka-Saṁvat 651 expired, in the thirty-fourth year of his
  reign ... approximately, the 7th February, A.D. 730" (Fleet 1896, Gazetteer p. 373). `inscription`
  (as copied after 967; see Fleet's caveat below).
- Father and son together: Fleet IA X (1881) p. 165, No. CI, a red-sandstone tablet found near
  Pattadakal: "the grant that was given of Śrî-Vijayâditya and Vikramâditya, the favourites of the
  world, the great kings, the supreme kings, the supreme lords"; Fleet: "it is therefore not later
  than Śaka 655 (A.D. 733-4)". `inscription`. Fleet 1896 adds that Vikramaditya II issued a copper
  charter in his father's time (Nerur, undated, "authenticity is not quite certain") and is "mentioned
  as Yuvarâja in an inscription at Lakshmêshwar". `inscription` (reported).
- The Vijayesvara temple: Pattadakal pillar of Kirtivarman II (EI III no. 1, Fleet) l. 4–7, translated:
  "the glorious and blameless and generous Vijayâditya-Satyâśraya ... By him there was erected a great
  stone temple of (the god) the holy Vijayêśvara-bhaṭṭâraka". Fleet: "This temple is now known by the
  name of Saṁgamêśvara". `inscription`.
- A story beat for the father, from his son's charter (Vakkaleri ll. 22–33, as paraphrased by Pathak,
  EI IX p. 201–202): Vijayaditya "had greatly assisted his father and grandfather in their wars.
  Though suddenly taken prisoner by the enemy, whom he had put to flight, he skilfully effected his
  escape and restored the splendour of the Chalukya empire." `inscription` (Pathak's paraphrase of the
  formula shared by the Nerur, Vakkaleri and Kendur plates).

## 2. Accession and the vow against the Pallava

### 2a. Accession date (Fleet 1896, Gazetteer p. 374): `scholarship` from dated records
"Vijayâditya was succeeded, in A.D. 733 or 734, by his 'dear or favourite son' Vikramâditya II., who
had the usual biruda of Satyâśraya, the epithet of Śrîpṛithivîvallabha, and the titles of
Mahârâjâdhirâja, Paramêśvara, and in Sanskrit Bhaṭṭâraka or in Kanarese Bhaṭâra." Footnote 4: "On any
date from the pûrṇimânta Phâlguna kṛishṇa 1 of Śaka-Saṁvat 655 current, in A.D. 733, up to Mâgha
śukla 15 of Ś.-S. 656 current, in A.D. 734."

### 2b. Lakshmeshwar, Saka 656, his second year: Fleet, IA VII (1878) pp. 101–112, No. XXXVIII
Source: https://archive.org/details/india.history.resource.77343 (77343_djvu.txt). `inscription`, with
caveats.
- What the stone is: "a long stone-tablet inscription at the temple called Śaṅkha-basti at
  Lakshmêśwar"; "62 lines of writing in neatly formed Old Canarese characters of the tenth century
  A.D."; "It includes three separate inscriptions." Third part (l. 61 to end): "an inscription of the
  Western Chalukya Vikramâditya II. It is dated in the second year of his reign, when the Śaka year 656
  (A.D. 734-5) had expired, and must consequently have been copied here from a previous stone-tablet or
  copper-plate, for the sake of confirmation or of preservation." Language of that part: Sanskrit
  ("vijaya-skandhâvârê Raktapuram=adhivasati shaṭ-pañchâśad-uttara-shaṭ-chhatêshu Śaka-varshêshv=
  atîtêshu pravarddhamâna-vijaya-râjya-saṁvatsarê dvitîyê").
- Fleet's translation of the king's description: "Vikramâditya-Satyâśraya, the favourite of the world,
  the Great King, the supreme king, the supreme lord, the venerable one, whose manhood was increasing
  day by day; who enlarged his dominions by invading the territories of his foes; and the chief flavour
  of whose heroism was (like the scent) of musk and of the Kiśôra plant". The grant: at the request of
  "the merchant Bâhubali", the Śaṅkhatîrtha-vasati of Pulikara (Lakshmeshwar) was embellished and "the
  white Jinâlaya" repaired, and "a field of the measure of half a hundred nivartanas by the royal
  measure" given for the worship of Jina, "on the day of the full-moon of (the month) Mâgha".
- Date: Fleet 1896 "approximately, the 14th January, A.D. 735". Fleet 1896 fn. 11: "This, however, is
  only a copy of an original record, put on the stone after A.D. 967."
- Use: a Jain gift in the king's second year is safe. Present it as "a later copy of his grant".

### 2c. The vow (Vakkaleri plates, Hultzsch's translation, SII I p. 146): `inscription`
Source: https://www.whatisindia.com/inscriptions/south_indian_inscriptions/volume_1/kuram.html
(Hultzsch 1890; he transcribed this part of the Vakkaleri grant from Rice's facsimile).
"Vikramâditya Satyâśraya Śrî-Pṛithivî-Vallabha, the king of great kings, the supreme ruler, the lord,
to whom arose great energy immediately after the time of his anointment at the self-choice of the
goddess of the sovereignty of the whole world, and who resolved to uproot completely his natural
enemy, the Pallava, who had robbed of their splendour the previous kings born from his race, reached
with great speed the Tuṇḍâka-vishaya (i.e., the Toṇḍai-maṇḍalam) ..."
Sanskrit (Kielhorn EI V p. 203, ll. 36–39, OCR normalised): "sakala-bhuvana-sâmrâjya-lakshmî-
svayaṁvar-âbhishêka-samay-ânantara-samupajâta-mahôtsâhaḥ âtma-vaṁśaja-pûrvva-nṛipati-chchhây-
âpahâriṇaḥ prakṛity-amitrasya Pallavasya samûl-ônmûlanâya kṛita-matir atitvarayâ Tuṇḍâka-vishayaṁ
prâpya". Key words for the writer: *prakṛity-amitra* "natural enemy"; *samûl-ônmûlana* "uprooting,
root and all"; *mahôtsâha* "great energy" right after the *abhishêka*.
Dramatisation boundary: the vow is a charter formula written under his son; no speech, place or day of
the vow is recorded.

## 3. Kanchi: the march, the entry "without destroying it", the gold, the Kannada pillar

### 3a. Vakkaleri plates of Kirtivarman II: Kielhorn, EI V no. 22 pp. 200–205 (1898–99)
Source: https://archive.org/details/india.history.resource.92649 (92649_djvu.txt). `inscription`.
- Object (Kielhorn p. 200): "five copper-plates ... strung on a ring ... an elliptical seal ... bears,
  in relief on a countersunk surface, a standing boar which faces to the proper right." Found at
  "Vakkalêri, the head-quarters of a hôbali in the Kôlâr district of the Mysore state". First published
  Rice, IA VIII p. 23 ff. Language Sanskrit; "the writing is well engraved and is in an excellent state of
  preservation".
- Hultzsch's translation (SII I p. 146), continuing from §2c: "... attacked at the head of a battle and
  put to flight the Pallava, called Nandipôtavarman, who had come to meet him, took possession of the
  musical instrument (called) 'harsh-sounding' and of the excellent musical instrument called 'roar of
  the sea,' of the banner (marked with Śiva's) club, of many renowned and excellent elephants, and of a
  heap of rubies, which drove away darkness by the light of the multitude of their rays, and entered
  (the city of) Kâñchî, which seemed to be the handsome girdle (kâñchî) of the nymph of the southern
  region, without destroying it. Having made the twice-born, the distressed and the helpless rejoice by
  continual gifts, having acquired great merit by granting heaps of gold to (the temple) of stone
  (called) Râjasiṁhêśvara, which Narasiṁhapôtavarman had caused to be built, and to other temples, and
  having burnt by the unimpeded progress of his power the Pâṇḍya, Chôḷa, Kêraḷa, Kaḷabhra and other
  princes, he placed a pillar of victory (jayastambha), which consisted (as it were) of the mass of his
  fame that was as pure as the bright moon in autumn, on the Southern Ocean, ... whose shore glittered
  with the rays of the pearls, which had dropped from the shells, that were beaten and split by the
  trunks of the frightened elephants (of his enemies), which resemble sea-monsters."
  (The OCR reads "continental gifts"; the Sanskrit is *satata-pravṛitta-dâna*, so "continual".)
- The two key compounds (Kielhorn ll. 43–47; Pathak's Kendur text ll. 37–39 has the same words):
  *Kâñchîm avinâśya praviśya* "having entered Kanchi without destroying it";
  *Narasiṁhapôtavarmma-nirmmâpita-śilâmaya-Râjasiṁhêśvar-âdi-dêvakula-suvarṇa-râśi-pratyarppaṇ-
  ôpârjit-ôrjita-puṇyaḥ* "who earned great merit by the *pratyarpaṇa* (giving back, handing over) of
  heaps of gold to the stone temples, Rajasimhesvara and others, built by Narasimhapotavarman".
  Hultzsch translates *pratyarpaṇa* as "granting"; Pathak (EI IX p. 202) as "restored ... which had
  been taken away from them"; the Kanchi pillar (3c) settles it: the king inspected the temple's
  riches and gave them back.
- The trophies are a listing, so they can be drawn: the *kaṭumukha-vâditra* ("harsh-sounding"
  instrument), the *samudraghôsha* ("roar of the sea"), the *khaṭvâṅga-dhvaja* (club-banner; Fleet 1896
  "khaṭvâṅga-dhvaja or club-banner"), elephants, rubies. Fleet 1896 fn. on the "roar of the sea":
  "Doubtless, a large conch-shell".
- The ocean pillar: "on the Southern Ocean" (*dakshiṇ-ârṇavê ... jayastambham atishṭhipat*). No place is
  named. Kielhorn fn. 2 p. 202: "Owing to my different reading, I do not take the compound
  ghûrṇamân-ârṇas in line 49 as a proper name" (i.e. not a place name; it means "rolling-waved").
- "Unimpeded" is *anivârita* (l. 47 *anivârita-pratâpa-prasara*). See the *anivârita* thread, §6d.
- The writer of the charter (Kielhorn p. 201): "the Mahâsândhivigrahika Anivârita Dhanaṁjaya, sc.
  Puṇyavallabha" (text l. 76–77 "Mahâsândhivigrahika-śrîmad-Anivârita-Dhanaṁjaya-puṇyavallabhasya
  likhitam=idaṁ śâsanaṁ"), and fn. 5: "This, perhaps, is the Anivârita-puṇyavallabha who wrote the
  Kâñchî inscription of Vikramâditya (probably II., the father of Kîrtivarman II.)". `scholarship`
  (Kielhorn's "perhaps").

### 3b. Fleet 1896 on the same passage (Gazetteer pp. 374–375): `scholarship`
"... that he then entered Kâñchî, which, however, he refrained from destroying; that there he acquired
great merit by granting heaps of gold to the Râjasiṁhêśvara and other temples which Narasiṁhavarman
II. had caused to be built; and that, having destroyed the power of the Pâṇḍya, Chôla, Kêrala,
Kalabhra, and other kings, he set up a pillar of victory on the shore of the southern ocean. The
conquest of Kâñchî is mentioned in the Paṭṭadakal inscriptions. That Vikramâditya II. did really enter
Kâñchî, is proved by the remains of an inscription of his at the Râjasiṁhêśvara temple."
(The OCR prints "Narasiṁhavarman I."; the numeral is unclear in the scan. Do not put a regnal number on
the builder in the book: say "Narasimhapotavarman (Rajasimha)", which is what the plates say.)

### 3c. The Kannada pillar in the Kailasanatha temple: Hultzsch, EI III no. 48 pp. 359–360 (1894–95)
Sources: https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol3/epigraphiaindica359.html and
.../epigraphiaindica360.html. `inscription`.
- Where (p. 359): "the inscription is engraved on the back of a pillar of the maṇḍapa in front of the
  Râjasiṁhêśvara shrine, and nearly touches the east wall of another maṇḍapa which, at a later time,
  has been erected between the shrine and the front maṇḍapa. This circumstance makes it impossible to
  read the inscription from the stone and renders the preparation of good estampage a work of some
  difficulty." Edited "from excellent inked estampages recently prepared by my Tamil Assistant,
  Mr. T. P. Krishnasvami Sastri".
- Script and language: "The alphabet of the inscription is Old-Kanarese; and the language is Kanarese
  prose, with the exception of the concluding sentence, which is half Sanskṛit and half Kanarese."
- Text (11 lines, Hultzsch's transliteration):
  "Svasti [||*] Vikramâd[it]ya-Satyâśraya-Śr[î]-Prithuvi-vallabha-Mahârâjâdhirâja-Paramêśvara-Bhaṭṭârar
  Kañchiyân=koṇḍu Râjasi[ṁ*]ghêśvarada dhanamân=kaṇḍu maguḷdu dêvargge biṭṭâr [|*] Intu biṭṭa
  bhaṭârâra dharmmada sthitiyu[m*] îy=aksharaṅgaḷumân=alivâr=îy=ûrâ ghaṭigeyâ mahâjanamân=kondâra
  lôkakke sandâr=appâr [||*] Niravadya-śrîmad-A[ni]vâritapuṇyavallabhêna likhitam=i[daṁ]
  Vallabhadurjjayar=adhikâradi[m ||*]"
- Hultzsch's translation: "Hail! Vikramâditya-Satyâśraya, the favourite of Fortune and of the Earth,
  the Mahârâjâdhirâja Paramêśvara Bhaṭṭâra, having captured Kañchi (and) having inspected the riches
  (belonging) to (the temple of) Râjasiṅghêśvara (i.e. Râjasiṁhêśvara), gave (them) again to the god.
  Those who destroy these letters and the stability of the king's charity which was thus given, shall
  enter the world of those who have killed the men of the assembly (?) of this city. This (edict was)
  written by the blameless and illustrious Anivâritapuṇyavallabha under the authority of
  Vallabhadurjaya."
- Hultzsch fn. 2: "According to Mr. Kittel's Dictionary, Kañchi is a Kanarese tadbhava of Kâñchî".
  Fn. 4: "The Kanarese term ghaṭigê (ghaṭikâ in Sanskṛit) possibly corresponds to the sabhai (sabhâ in
  Sanskṛit) of Tamil inscriptions."
- Attribution (p. 359): the record "is not dated and might thus belong to the reign of either of the
  two Vikramâdityas, [but] it may be assigned with great probability to Vikramâditya II., because the
  Wokkalêri plates explicitly state that the latter, after his conquest of Kâñchî, made gifts to the
  Râjasiṁhêśvara temple." Tier for "it is Vikramaditya II's": `scholarship` (Hultzsch, strong).
- Condition: Fleet 1896 calls it "A much obliterated inscription at the Râjasiṁhêśvara shrine";
  Hultzsch 1890 (SII I) "much obliterated"; Hultzsch 1895 had "excellent inked estampages" and read it
  completely. Both statements are in the record; the 1895 reading is the edition to use.
- **Why this is unique (careful wording).** What the sources support: (i) the record is in Kannada, in
  Old Kannada script, in the Pallava capital, inside the Pallavas' own royal temple; (ii) its content is
  a conqueror returning a temple's treasure, not a victory boast; (iii) the curse protects the gift and
  "these letters" in the name of the city's own *ghaṭige* assembly. What the sources do NOT give: any
  statement that it is the "only" or "first" Kannada inscription in Tamil country. Write "a Kannada
  record left by a Chalukya king inside the Pallava capital's own temple", not "the only one".
- The *ghaṭige* thread (`scholarship`, link to the Kadamba series): Kielhorn (EI VIII, quoted in
  `kadamba-verified.md` §1) showed that a *ghaṭikâ* was "an establishment (probably founded in most
  cases by a king) for holy and learned men", and that the word is used so in the Kasakudi plates of
  this very Nandivarman Pallavamalla. The Talagunda pillar v. 10 says Mayurasharma went to "the city of
  the Pallava lords" and "entered the ghaṭikâ". Four centuries later Vikramaditya's Kannada curse is
  sworn on "the *mahâjanas* of the *ghaṭige* of this city". That both mean Kanchi's ghatika is a
  reasonable reading, not a statement of either record. Tell it in a "ನಿಮಗೆ ಗೊತ್ತೇ?" box with that hedge.

### 3d. The Pattadakal pillar's word "bruiser": Fleet, EI III no. 1, p. 3 fn. 1: `inscription`
The pillar (l. 9) calls him *Kâñchîpura-vimardana*, translated "the bruiser of the town of Kâñchî".
Fleet's footnote: "The word used is vimardana, which may mean either 'bruising' or 'destroying.' But the
Wokkalêri grant says that, though he entered Kâñchî, he did not destroy it (avinâśya praviśya ...)."
Use the Vakkaleri wording. A charter of his own son is the later and fuller source.

### 3e. Kendur plates of Kirtivarman II: Pathak, EI IX no. 28 pp. 200–206 (1907–08)
Source: https://archive.org/details/epigraphiaindica014770mbp and in.ernet.dli.2015.69958. `inscription`.
Five plates, boar seal "facing to the proper left", found at Kendur, Khed taluka, Poona. Issued from
Raktapura (Pathak: "probably to be identified with Lakshmêśvara"), "on the full moon day of the month
Vaiśâkha, during a lunar eclipse, in Śaka 672 expired, in the sixth year of his reign", "at the request
of his great queen". Its Vikramaditya II passage repeats Vakkaleri word for word (*Kâmchîm=avinâśya
praviśya ... suvarṇa-râśi-pratyarppaṇ-ôpârjit-ôrjita-puṇyaḥ*). Pathak's summary: Vikramaditya II "led an
excursion into the Tuṇḍâka country, defeated his natural foe the Pallava king Nandipôtavarman, and
entered the Pallava capital Kâñchî, but did not destroy it. He restored to the Râjasiṁhêśvara and
other temples, which had been caused to be built there by Narasiṁhapôtavarman, heaps of gold and
rubies, which had been taken away from them." ("and rubies" and "taken away" are Pathak's gloss; the
compound has only *suvarṇa-râśi*, heaps of gold.)

### 3f. The Pallava side
- The temple's builder, from the Chalukya side: *Narasiṁhapôtavarmma-nirmmâpita-śilâmaya-
  Râjasiṁhêśvara* "the stone (temple) Rajasimhesvara caused to be built by Narasimhapotavarman"
  (Vakkaleri l. 45). `inscription`. That Narasimhapotavarman here = Rajasimha (Narasimhavarman II) is
  the standard identification; Fleet's numeral in the scan is unclear (3b). `scholarship`.
- The defeated king: Hultzsch SII I p. 146: "this Nandipôtavarman must be identical with the Pallava
  king Nandivarman Pallavamalla, who is mentioned in Mr. Foulkes' grant" (Udayendiram). `scholarship`.
- Pallava records of Nandivarman's own reply, the Alampur material, and Pallava inscriptions of the
  Kailasanatha itself were NOT opened in this pass. UNVERIFIED. The story should not assert anything
  about Nandivarman's age, exile, or later return (Wikipedia: "a 13-year-old boy", "fled Kanchipuram
  into exile", from Ramesh 1984; not checked).
- Honour rule: the only sources for this campaign are Chalukya records. They are victors' records, so
  they are not hostile to the king; they are partisan toward him. Tell the Pallava with dignity: he
  "came out to meet him" in the field (Vakkaleri), which is to his credit.

## 5. The Tajika (Arab) invasion repelled: Navsari plates of Avanijanasraya Pulakeshin

### 5a. Edition read: Bhagwanlal Indraji, "Two new grants of the Chalukya dynasty", Verhandlungen des
VII. Internationalen Orientalisten-Congresses, Wien 1886, Arische Section (1888) pp. 211–237, grant
nro II (text pp. 230–233, translation pp. 235–237).
Source: https://archive.org/details/india.history.resource.89788 (89788_djvu.txt). `inscription` (PD).
- Date (text l. 49): "saṁvatsara-śata 490 kârttika-śuddha 15", i.e. Kalachuri year 490, full moon of
  Karttika. Bhagwanlal p. 223: "Saṁ 490 or about 740 A.D." Fleet 1896: "approximately, to the 22nd
  October, A.D. 739". Mirashi 1955: "K. 490 (740 A.C.)".
- The grant: village Padraka in the Kârmaṇêya district (Kamrej, near Surat) to a Brahman whose name
  Bhagwanlal "may rather doubtfully" read Kâñchali, son of Govindali, "a native of Banavâsi in North
  Kânarâ" (text "Vanavâsi-vinirggata"). Writer: "the minister for peace and war, a noble named
  Bappabhaṭṭa, son of the great military officer Haragaṇa." Note for the book: a Gujarat grant of the
  year of the Arab defeat goes to a Brahman from Banavasi.
- The battle passage, text ll. 23–35 (Bhagwanlal's transcription; key words):
  "...siramudgarodgâriṇi taralatara-târatara-vâri-dârit-ôdita-Saindhava-Kachchhella-Saurâshṭra-
  Châvoṭaka-Mauryya-Gûrjjar-âdi-râje niḥśesha-dâkshiṇâtya-kshitipati-jigîshayâ Dakshiṇâpatha-
  pravêś-âbhilâsha[?]... prathamam êva Navasârikâ-vishaya-prasâdhanây-âgatê ... samaraśirasi vijitê
  Tâjik-ânîkê śauryy-ânurâgiṇâ Śrîvallabha-narêndrêṇa prasâdîkṛit-âparanâma-chatushṭayas tad yathâ
  Dakshiṇâpathasâdhâra-Chalukikulâlaṁkâra-Pṛithivîvallabha-Anivarttakanivarttayitṛ-Avanijanâśraya-
  Śrî-Pulakeśirâjaḥ" (Bhagwanlal's fn.: the bracketed letters "are very doubtful").
- Bhagwanlal's translation (pp. 236–237), the core: "... the illustrious king Pulakeśi, whom the
  illustrious Vallabhanarendra, taking delight in valour, favoured with the following four other names
  ... when the army of the Tâjika, which vomited forth arrows and mudgaras, which destroyed by its
  brightly glittering very sharp swords the prosperous Saindhava, Kachchhella, Saurâshṭra, Châvoṭaka,
  Maurya and Gurjjara kings and others, which, wishing (?) to enter the Dakshiṇâpatha with the desire of
  conquering all the southern kings, came in the first instance to reduce the Navasârikâ-country, which
  darkened the regions of the sky with dust from the ground, that was dug up by the hard and noisy hoofs
  of its quick horses, ... had been conquered in a battle, in which quadrilles were formed by head-less
  trunks, dancing to the loud noise of the drums, beaten (by them) after (they felt) the satisfaction
  produced by the thought 'to day we have paid for one birth our debt to our lord by sacrificing our
  heads for him', by even countless chiefs of hosts of kings, unconquered before, who had paid with their
  heads for the acceptance of great honour and presents (bestowed) by their lord, ..."
  Grammar note: the head-paying warriors are in the instrumental, the agents by whom the Tajika army
  was conquered, i.e. Pulakeshin's side. The "fat bellies" and "intestines" clauses describe what the
  Tajika army's mail was stained with. The compound is long; Bhagwanlal's English is not always clear
  which side each clause belongs to. Narrate the defenders' oath; do not invent Arab cruelty beyond
  the plate's own words.
- Bhagwanlal p. 223: "The real proper name of the leader of the army is not mentioned. The word Tâjika
  ... is commonly used by Indian writers to denote the Arabs". His fn. on mudgara ("a common Sassanian
  weapon") is his gloss.

### 5b. The four titles: what the plate says, and two readings
- The plate: the overlord "favoured [him] with four other names" (*apara-nâma-chatushṭaya*). Five
  epithets follow, so the count depends on how they are split:
  - Bhagwanlal: (1) "the ornament of the Chalukika family, the conqueror of Dakshiṇâpatha"
    (*Dakshiṇâpathasâdhaka-Chalukikakulâlaṅkâra*, he emends *sâdhâra* to *sâdhaka*); (2) "the husband
    of the earth" (*Pṛithivîvallabha*); (3) "the repulser of the non-retiring" (*Anivarttaka-
    nivarttayitṛi*); (4) "the refuge of men on earth" (*Avanijanâśraya*).
  - Mirashi (CII IV, 1955, intro, "Early Chalukyas of Gujarat", whatisindia earlychalukyasofgujarat5):
    "the four titles Dakshināpathasādhāra (the Pillar of Dakshināpatha), Chalukkikulālankāra (the
    Ornament of the Chālukya family) Prithivīvallabha (the dear Lord of the Earth) and
    Anivartakānivartayitri (the Repeller of the unrepellable)". Avanijanasraya is then his existing
    biruda (Fleet 1896 also: "who had the biruda of Avanijanâśraya").
  - Book wording: give Mirashi's four (the plate's *sâdhâra* as engraved), and say "Avanijanasraya,
    'refuge of the people of the earth', was his name of honour". Do not say the emperor gave him
    "Avanijanasraya" (Wikipedia does; Bhagwanlal's split allows it, Mirashi's does not). Disputed.
- Spelling on the plate: Fleet 1896 (Gazetteer p. 337 fn.): "we have the very exceptional form
  'Chalukki'". The Navsari text has "Chalukikulâlaṁkâra".

### 5c. The overlord is not named on the plate
Bhagwanlal: "Śrîvallabha-narêndra" ("the illustrious Vallabhanarendra"). Mirashi: "The Chālukya,
suzerain of Bādāmi, whose name has not been specifically mentioned, but who must have been Vikramāditya
II (733-747 A.C.)". Fleet 1896 places it "in the time of Vikramâditya II". Tier: `scholarship`
(certain enough from the date, and Vallabha is the Badami house title). The book may say "the emperor
at Vatapi, Vikramaditya" but the evidence page must say the plate calls him only "Vallabha".

### 5d. Context and "why it matters": say only what the sources say
- Plate (`inscription`): the Tajika army had already "destroyed" six named western kingdoms; it meant
  "to enter the Dakshiṇâpatha with the desire of conquering all the southern kings"; it "came in the
  first instance to reduce the Navasârikâ-country"; it was "conquered".
- Fleet 1896 (`scholarship`): "a formidable invasion of Gujarât by the Tâjikas or Arabs ... There,
  however, they were met and conquered by the feudatory Chalukya prince Avanijanâśraya-Pulikêśin."
  His identifications: Saindhava "the ruler of Sindh"; Kachchhella "probably, the ruler of Kachh";
  Saurashtra "doubtless, the king of Valabhî"; Chavotaka "the Châpôtkaṭa or Châvaḍâ prince". He
  dates it after Bhagwanlal's 730 because the Gurjara Jayabhata III has a date of 736: "this seems to
  place the event some seven or eight years later than was supposed by the Pandit."
- Mirashi 1955 (`scholarship`, short quotes): the raid on Navsari "is not recorded by Muslim
  chroniclers probably because the Arabs this time sustained a crushing defeat"; "The Graphic and
  detailed description of the flight, which for its vigour is unsurpassed in inscriptional literature,
  suggests that it must have been composed soon after the fight." He places it after Junaid's raid on
  Broach (c. 725).
- Arab side (`inscription`, a 9th-c. chronicle): al-Baladhuri, Futuh al-Buldan, in Elliot & Dowson,
  History of India I (1867) pp. 126–127, https://archive.org/details/historyofindiaas01elli: Junaid
  "sent his officers to Marmad, Mandal, Dahnaj, and Barus [Broach]" and "conquered al Bailaman and
  Jurz"; then "In the days of Tamim, the Musulmans retired from several parts of India and left some of
  their positions, nor have they up to the present time advanced so far as in days gone by." Navsari and
  any Chalukya are not named. Do not connect the retreat to Navsari as cause; Mirashi only suggests the
  silence.
- Safe "why it matters" line for the book: an Arab army that had already overrun Sindh, Kachchh,
  Saurashtra and the Gurjara and Chavotaka kingdoms meant to enter the Deccan; at Navsari a Chalukya
  prince stopped it, and the Chalukya emperor honoured him for it. Avoid "saved India", "stopped Islam",
  "first defeat of the Arabs in India": none of the sources says so.
- The emperor was not at the battle: nothing in the plate places him there. The honours are "favoured
  ... with four other names"; nothing about a meeting, court or ceremony.
- Chinese or other notices of the 730s: none are in the research files, and none were checked in this
  pass. UNVERIFIED; do not use.

## 6. The queens, the temples and the architect Gunda

### 6a. Kirtivarman II's pillar at Pattadakal: Fleet, EI III no. 1 pp. 1–7: `inscription`
Source: https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol3/epigraphiaindica2.html … 7.html.
- The pillar is "stamped with the mark of the triśûla, or trident", set up "in the middle of these
  three shrines, by a sculptor named Śubhadêva, for an Âchârya named Jñânaśiva, who had come from the
  Mṛigathaṇikâhâra vishaya on the north bank of the Ganges".
- Translation, ll. 7–15: "The son, the dear (or favourite) son, of the glorious Vijayâdityadêva, (was)
  the illustrious unrepulsed one, to whose feet obeisance was done by all feudatory chiefs, the bruiser
  of the town of Kâñchî, the glorious Vikramâditya-(II.)-Satyâśraya ... His dear (or favourite)
  queen-consort, born in the race of the Haihayas, (was) one who, like the divine (goddess) Umâ, was a
  very mother of mankind. By her there was erected a great stone temple of (the god) the holy
  Lôkêśvara-bhaṭṭâraka. ... Of (her) the glorious Lôkamahâdêvî, the uterine younger sister, a dear (or
  favourite) queen of the glorious Vikramâdityadêva; (and) the mother of ... Kîrtivarman-(II.)-
  Satyâśraya ... Nṛipasiṁharâja, (was) the glorious Trailôkya-Mahâdêvî. By her there was erected a great
  stone temple of (the god) the holy Trailôkyêśvara-bhaṭṭâraka."
  "The illustrious unrepulsed one" = text l. 8 *śrî-anivârita*.
- Fleet p. 3: the Lokesvara temple "still exists, but is now known by the name of Virûpâksha; the
  identity is established by records on structural parts of it, which give its name as Lôkêśvara";
  Trailokyesvara "is not now in existence, I think". (Modern identification with the Mallikarjuna
  temple: UNESCO nomination, per companion file §5 item 16; `scholarship`, not re-checked here.)
- Date: "the English date is the 25th June, A.D. 754: on this day ... there was a total eclipse of the
  sun, which was visible right across India" (fn.: "there is no other eclipse that does so, for at least
  twenty years on either side of it"). Grant: "thirty nivartanas, which were purchased with thirty
  gadyâṇakas of gold".
- Script: duplicate text in local characters and in Nagari. Fleet p. 2: "this record and the Sâmângaḍ
  grant, which is six months earlier in actual date, give the earliest instances, as yet obtained, of the
  use of Nâgarî characters in Western India" (1894 statement). Nice fact; caption it "as of Fleet's
  time".
- Fleet 1896 fn. (Gazetteer p. 374): "There seems to have been some shrine of Lôkêśvara in existence
  before the building of Lôkamahâdêvî's temple; for, one of the inscriptions speaks of her confirming to
  the singers the covenants of former times, which had been made by Vijayâditya."

### 6b. The Virupaksha (Lokesvara) gateway and porch inscriptions: Fleet, IA X (1881) pp. 162–167
Source: https://archive.org/details/india.history.resource.89097 (89097_djvu.txt), "Sanskrit and
Old-Canarese Inscriptions" Nos. XCIX–CVI. `inscription`.
- Fleet p. 163: "The largest, though not the oldest, temple is that of the god Virûpâksha, or, as we
  learn from the inscriptions, originally of Lôkêśvara. And the present inscription and the next tell us
  that it was built by the Sûtradhârî Gunda for Lôkamahâdêvî, the queen-consort of the Western
  Chalukya king Vikramâditya II., in commemoration of her husband having three times conquered Kâñchî".
  Fn.: "The Sûtradhârî or Sûtragrâhî, 'the holder of the thread; the measurer,' was the assistant of the
  Sthapati, the master-carpenter, or master-mason; the architect."
- **No. XCIX** (east gateway of the courtyard, pilaster right/north of the door; "the engraving of the
  inscription was evidently synchronous with the building of the temple"). Old Kannada. Transcription:
  "Svasti Vikramâditya-śrî-pṛithivîvallabha-mahâdêviyarâ dêgulamaṁ mâḍida sûtradhâri Śrî-Gundan
  anivârit-Âchâriyge mûme-perjjerepu-paṭṭamuṁ Tribhuvanâchâriy=endu pesar=itta prasâdaṁgeyda
  pṛithiviya binnanigala ballligavartte illa ..." Translation: "Hail! There is no excommunication from
  caste of the skilful people of the world who have attained the favour (of the god), having given the
  paṭṭa called mûme-perjerepu (and) the name of Tribhuvanâchârya to Śrî-Gunda, whose (observance of
  the) established rules of conduct was unimpeded, the Sûtradhârî who made the temple of the queen of
  Vikramâditya, the favourite of the world; (and there is) immunity to the others who united themselves
  with the guilty man." Curse: "May he, who destroys this, be on an equality with people who kill a
  thousand tawny-coloured cows and a thousand Brâhmans of Vâraṇâsî!"
  Fleet's own caution: the sense of *balligavarte* (excommunication) "must not be accepted as
  altogether certain". So the "artisans' readmission to caste" reading is Fleet's tentative one. Do not
  build a scene on it.
  Fleet 1896 re-rendered Gunda's epithet: "the architect, Gunda, also called Anivâritâchârya, 'the
  unrepulsed Âchârya,' who built the temple", and the honour as "the fillet or badge of honour called
  mûme-perjerepu-paṭṭa, and the name of Tribhuvanâchârya or 'preceptor of the three worlds'". Fn. on
  *paṭṭa*: "a patent, royal grant or order, fillet or turban of honour".
- **No. C** (the matching pilaster, south side; "uncovered by me for the first time, having been
  previously almost entirely concealed by a rubble masonry wall"). Transcription opening: "Svasti
  Śrî-Vikramâditya-bhaṭârar=mmûme Kâñchiyan=mûme parâjisidorâ Śrî-Lôkamahâdêviyarâ Lôkêśvaramaṁ
  mâḍida sûtradhârige mûme-perjjerepu geyda-balikke ..." Translation: "Hail! Let it be known that these
  are the names of the Âchârya who averted the excommunication of the skilful people of this district,
  after that they had given the mûme-perjerepu to the Sûtradhârî who made this temple of Lôkêśvara of
  Lôkamahâdêvî, (the queen) of Vikramâditya, the worshipful one, who three times conquered Kâñchî:
  Hail! Śrî-Sarvasiddhi-âchârya; the asylum of all virtuous qualities; the Pitâmaha of many cities and
  houses; he whose conversation is entirely perfect and refined; he who has for a jewelled diadem and
  crest-jewel the houses and palaces and vehicles and seats and couches (that he has constructed); the
  (most eminent) Sûtradhârî of the southern country." (OCR of the Kannada title lines is garbled; the
  Old Kannada for the last epithet is the source of the web's "Tenkanadesiya sutradhari".)
  Fleet: "this inscription gives several titles of the builder of the temple. Among them is that of
  Sarvasiddhi-Âchârya. The Sarvasiddhi-Âchâryas ... seem to have been some celebrated guild of
  architects or builders."
  **Caution on "three times":** the phrase is *mûme Kâñchiyan mûme parâjisidor*; Fleet translates "who
  three times conquered Kâñchî". The same word *mûme* is the first element of the honour
  *mûme-perjjerepu*, which Fleet leaves untranslated. "Three times" is therefore Fleet's rendering
  (`scholarship`); the line itself is `inscription`. Wikipedia's spelling "mume-Kanchiyan-mume
  parajisidor" matches Fleet's transcription.
- **No. CII** (east porch): "Lôkamahâdêvî, (the queen) of Śrî-Vikramâditya, the worshipful one,
  confirmed to the singers the covenants of former times, which had been granted to the singers by
  Śrî-Vijayâditya-Satyâśraya". "Duggamâra, of Śrî-Guppadugga in (the country of) Ereya, obtained this
  (concession)."
- **No. CV** (east porch): "At the time of giving the Nareyaṅgal Fifty, (which was the grant) of the
  temple of Lôkêśvara of Lôkamahâdêvî (the queen) of Vikramâditya ... There is no peremptory demand;
  there is no (obligation of) presenting cattle; there is no right of perquisites (allowed) to the king's
  servants." Fleet: Nareyangal "is in all probability the modern Narêgal in the Dhârwâḍ District, about
  twenty-five miles almost due south of Paṭṭadakal."
- Pattadakal's old name (Fleet p. 163): "the ancient Kisuvoḷal or Paṭṭada-Kisuvoḷal"; *paṭṭada* "of the
  (regal) fillet or turban ... 'of the coronation'"; *kisu* "perhaps ... 'a ruby'" (Fleet's guesses,
  `scholarship`).

### 6c. Queens: origin and order
- Lokamahadevi: *mahâdêvî*, "born in the race of the Haihayas" (pillar). Fleet 1896: "of the Haihaya
  race, i.e. of the Kalachuri family". No inscription names her father (companion file §5 item 17; no
  new evidence found).
- Trailokyamahadevi: *râjñî*, "the uterine younger sister of Lôkamahâdêvî", mother of Kirtivarman II.
  Fleet 1896 fn.: "With this instance of the marriage of sisters to one and the same husband, compare
  the cases of the wives of the Rashtrakûṭa Jagattuṅga II ... and ... the Hoysaḷa Ballâḷa I."
- Dating the temple: no inscription gives a year for Lokesvara. UNESCO's "c. 740" is an estimate.
  The gateway records are of Vikramaditya II's reign (Fleet). Say "in his reign".

### 6d. The *anivârita* thread (observation, not a source claim)
The word *anivârita* "unrepulsed, unimpeded" recurs around this king:
the king himself, *śrî-anivârita* "the illustrious unrepulsed one" (Pattadakal pillar l. 8);
his power, *anivârita-pratâpa-prasara* (Vakkaleri l. 47);
his architect, *Gundan anivârit-Âchâri* (IA X No. XCIX; Fleet 1896 "Anivâritâchârya");
the writer of the Kanchi pillar, *Anivâritapuṇyavallabha*, and of the Vakkaleri plates, *Anivârita-
Dhanaṁjaya Puṇyavallabha* (Kielhorn: "perhaps" the same man);
and his vassal's new title, *Anivarttaka-nivarttayitṛi* "repeller of the unrepellable" (Navsari; a
related verb, *ni-vṛt* not *ni-vṛ*).
No source says the officers took the name from the king. It can be a "ನಿಮಗೆ ಗೊತ್ತೇ?" observation
with that caveat. Do not claim Gunda was given the king's own title.

## 7. Kirtivarman II and the end of the dynasty

- Second Kanchi expedition, by the son as yuvaraja (Vakkaleri ll. 52–58; Fleet 1896, Gazetteer p. 377):
  "learning the use of weapons in his childhood, he so pleased his father as to be invested with the
  dignity of Yuvarâja, and to be intrusted with the command of an expedition against the 'family-foe,'
  the Pallava, the lord of Kâñchî, in which the Pallava king came out to meet him, but proved unable to
  fight in the open country; whereupon, Kîrtivarman II. drove him back into his fortress; broke his
  power; and seized multitudes of elephants and rubies, and much gold, which he presented to his
  father." Text: *svakula-vairiṇaḥ Kâñchîpatêr nigrahâya mâṁ prêshaya* "send me to subdue the lord of
  Kanchi, enemy of our house" (the son asks; Kielhorn ll. 54–55). `inscription`. This is a real line of
  request in the record; it may be voiced in the screenplay as the prince's plea, marked dramatised in
  form, sourced in substance.
- Succession: Fleet 1896 "in A.D. 746 or 747"; Pathak (Kendur, EI IX p. 201): Saka 672 expired = 6th
  year, so "Saka 677 expired was really the eleventh year", against Rice and Kielhorn's 679 for Vakkaleri
  ("the first word describing the Saka year is not legible"). Kielhorn's own text prints the year word as
  "ga(na)vasaptaty-uttara", i.e. an emendation of the engraved letter. Disputed: accession of Kirtivarman
  II c. 744/745 (Kendur) or 746/747 (Fleet). Wikipedia's "733–744" follows the Kendur reckoning (via
  Ramesh 1984, not opened).
- Last Chalukya charter (Fleet 1896): Vakkaleri, issued "when his victorious camp was at the village of
  Bhaṇḍâragavittage on the north bank of the river Bhîmarathi"; "The corresponding English date is the
  2nd September, A.D. 757" (if 679 is right).
- Dantidurga (Fleet 1896, Gazetteer pp. 378, 389), `scholarship` paraphrasing the Samangad grant
  (IA XI p. 114; not opened, the IA XI scan's OCR is unusable): the Samangad grant "states explicitly
  that that king acquired the supremacy by conquering Vallabha, i.e. Kîrtivarman II., and by overcoming
  the army of the Karṇâṭaka army, i.e. the Western Chalukya forces, which had been expert in defeating
  the lord of Kâñchî, the king of Kêrala, the Chôlas and the Pâṇḍyas, and Harsha and Vajraṭa".
  Rashtrakuta chapter: "with but a small force, he quickly overcame the boundless Karṇâṭaka army".
  Date: "for whom we have a date in January, A.D. 754"; EI III no. 1 says Samangad is "six months
  earlier" than 25 June 754. So "753/754", not a firm "753".
- The last stand (Fleet 1896 p. 378): "An attempt to re-assert the Western Chalukya sovereignty was
  evidently made, by Kîrtivarman II. himself, in the time of Dantidurga's successor Kṛishṇa I.; for, the
  latter is described as transforming into a deer, i.e. putting to flight, the great boar, the crest of
  the Chalukyas, 'which was seized with an itching for battle, and which, kindled with the warmth of
  bravery, attacked him'". Fleet: "it is possibly on this occasion that Kîrtivarman II. made ... the
  grant which is recorded in the Vakkalêri plates." "No record of any immediate successor of his has ever
  been obtained." `scholarship` / the Krishna I quote is a Rashtrakuta record (hostile).
- Telling it with dignity: every line about the fall comes from the Rashtrakuta victors (hostile). Even
  so, the hostile record itself praises the Chalukya army as the force that had beaten Kanchi, Kerala,
  the Cholas, Pandyas and Harsha, and calls the boar "kindled with the warmth of bravery". The last
  Chalukya charter is a gift to a Brahman from a camp on the Bhima. End there; no death scene is
  recorded for Kirtivarman II.

- **Kanchi pillar:** on the back of a pillar of the mandapa before the Rajasimhesvara shrine, Kailasanatha
  temple, Kanchipuram, partly hidden by a later mandapa wall (Hultzsch 1895, §3c). Visitors cannot read
  it on the stone; say so in "ಇಂದು ನೋಡಬಹುದು".
- Virupaksha east-gateway pilasters (Gunda, Nos. XCIX–C) and east-porch pillars (Nos. CII, CV); the
  trident pillar of 754 north of the Virupaksha enclosure (EI III no. 1).
- Lakshmeshwar Shankha-basti tablet (10th-c. copy containing his 2nd-year grant).

### 9a. Portrait
No portrait, coin portrait or named sculpture of Vikramaditya II is known from any source read in this

## 10. Popular claims to avoid (with the line that rejects or hedges them)

1. **"He sacked / destroyed Kanchi."** Rejected. Vakkaleri and Kendur: *Kâñchîm avinâśya praviśya*,
   "entered (the city of) Kâñchî ... without destroying it" (Hultzsch, SII I p. 146). The pillar's
   *vimardana* "bruiser" is explained by Fleet (EI III p. 3 fn. 1) against the Vakkaleri wording.
2. **"He fought the Arabs personally" / "the Navsari plates show him at the battle."** Rejected. The
   victor is "the illustrious king Pulakeśi"; the overlord only "favoured [him] with ... names"
   (Bhagwanlal p. 236). The overlord is not even named: "Śrîvallabha-narêndra"; Mirashi: "whose name has
   not been specifically mentioned".
3. **"Vikramaditya II gave him the title Avanijanasraya."** Disputed (Wikipedia). Mirashi's four titles
   exclude it; Fleet: he "had the biruda of Avanijanâśraya". §5b.
4. **"Dantidurga fought alongside the Chalukyas against the Arabs."** (Wikipedia, citing Kamath 2001.)
   Not in the Navsari plates read here. UNVERIFIED; omit.
5. **Rameswaram pillar.** No source read names Rameswaram or any place for the victory pillar:
   Vakkaleri says only "on the Southern Ocean". Kielhorn even refuses to read *ghûrṇamân-ârṇas* as a
   proper name. The only "Rameśvara" in Fleet 1896 is a Kirtivarman II grant "at the Râmêśvara tîrtha on
   the Tungabhadra" (known from a Govinda III charter of 804): a different place. Reject.
6. **"Kannada Lakshmeshwar inscription dated 13 January 735" (Wikipedia, Ramesh 1984 p. 156).** The
   record is Sanskrit in Old Kannada characters, a post-967 copy (Fleet IA VII p. 104; Fleet 1896 fn.
   11); Fleet dates it "approximately, the 14th January, A.D. 735". The 13 vs 14 January difference is
   a calendar computation not checked here; write "January 735".
7. **"733–744."** Derived from Pathak's Kendur reckoning; Fleet 1896 had 733/4 to 746/7. Write "c. 733
   to c. 744/746" in the facts, "about 733 to 745" in prose, with the disputed note (§7).
8. **"Three times conquered Kanchi: as prince, as king, and through his son."** The "three times" is
   Fleet's translation of *mûme* (§6b). The three-occasion breakdown is Ramesh 1984 (not opened). The
   plates record his own expedition and one by Kirtivarman as yuvaraja. The prince-era campaign of c. 730
   with the Ganga Ereyappa / Sripurusha and the battle of Vilande (Wikipedia, Sastri 1955) was not
   checked. Tell two expeditions plus the queen's "three times" line as her inscription says it.
9. **Chitramaya, Nandivarman "a 13-year-old boy", "fled into exile", "Narwan plates 741/742".**
   (Wikipedia, Ramesh 1984 pp. 157–158.) Not found in Fleet, Hultzsch, Kielhorn or Pathak. UNVERIFIED;
   omit.
10. **"He ensured the residents of the city were not harmed."** Overstatement. The records say he did
    not destroy the city, gave gifts to "the twice-born, the distressed and the helpless", and returned
    the temple's riches. Nothing about residents' safety beyond that.
11. **"Dantidurga helped Nandivarman II recover Kanchi" (Wikipedia, Thapar 2003).** Fleet's reading of
    the Ellora record has Dantidurga subjugating "the lord of Kâñchî" (Gazetteer p. 389), the opposite.
    Not part of this story; omit.
12. **"Vikramaditya II married into the Rashtrakutas to keep the peace" (Wikipedia, Sen 1999).** No
    source read supports it. Omit.
13. **"Lokamahadevi was a Kalachuri princess, daughter of X."** Only "of the Haihaya race" is recorded.
14. **"Gunda Anivaritachari was honoured with the perjjerepu patta" / titles "Tenkanadesiya sutradhari",
    "Anikapuravastu-pitamaha".** Use Fleet: the patta is *mûme-perjjerepu*; the name given was
    *Tribhuvanâchârya*; "Pitâmaha of many cities and houses" and "Sûtradhârî of the southern country"
    are titles in No. C, which Fleet ascribes to "the builder"; the name there is Sarvasiddhi-Âchârya.
    Whether Gunda and the Sarvasiddhi-acharya named in No. C are one man is Fleet's reading (he calls No.
    C's list "titles of the builder"); keep it as "Gunda, the master builder, whose titles were ...",
    flagged.
15. **"The Kanchi pillar is the only Kannada inscription in Tamil Nadu" / "first".** Not in any source
    read. Avoid superlatives (§3c).
16. **"Pallava sculptors were brought to Pattadakal."** No primary citation found (companion file Story
    C). Dramatise only, marked.
17. **"Vijayaditya's forty years of peace."** Long reign is recorded; "peace" is an inference (§0 item 8).

## 11. Hostile-only or thin episodes (owner rule)

- The fall of the dynasty: only Rashtrakuta records (Samangad, Ellora, Krishna I's) describe it.
  Hostile. Tell it from the facts of dates and the last charter; quote the enemy's own praise of the
  Karnataka army.
- The Arab side of 739/740: only the Chalukya plate. Partisan, not hostile to the king.
- Nothing in the record shows the king in a demeaning light. The Kuram plates' "covered only by a rag"
  belongs to Vikramaditya I, not II; do not let the two blur.

## 12. Five strongest "goosebump" facts (exact lines)

1. He entered the enemy capital and did not destroy it. Vakkaleri / Kendur, *Kâñchîm avinâśya
   praviśya*: "entered (the city of) Kâñchî ... without destroying it" (Hultzsch, SII I p. 146).
2. In the Pallavas' own temple, in Kannada: "Vikramâditya-Satyâśraya ... having captured Kañchi (and)
   having inspected the riches (belonging) to (the temple of) Râjasiṁhêśvara, gave (them) again to the
   god." (*Kañchiyân koṇḍu Râjasiṁghêśvarada dhanamân kaṇḍu maguḷdu dêvargge biṭṭâr*; Hultzsch, EI III
   p. 360.)
3. A queen's temple for "three times" Kanchi: "this temple of Lôkêśvara of Lôkamahâdêvî, (the queen) of
   Vikramâditya, the worshipful one, who three times conquered Kâñchî" (*mûme Kâñchiyan mûme
   parâjisidor*; Fleet, IA X p. 165, No. C), and the master builder Gunda given "the paṭṭa called
   mûme-perjerepu (and) the name of Tribhuvanâchârya" (IA X p. 164, No. XCIX).
4. At Navsari the defenders fought on the thought "'to day we have paid for one birth our debt to our
   lord by sacrificing our heads for him'", and the Tajika army that had "destroyed ... the Saindhava,
   Kachchhella, Saurâshṭra, Châvoṭaka, Maurya and Gurjjara kings" was "conquered"; the victor was named
   *Anivarttaka-nivarttayitṛi*, "the Repeller of the unrepellable" (Bhagwanlal 1888 pp. 236–237;
   Mirashi's rendering).
5. The vow at the crowning, and the son's plea: "to whom arose great energy immediately after the time
   of his anointment ... and who resolved to uproot completely his natural enemy, the Pallava, who had
   robbed of their splendour the previous kings born from his race" (Hultzsch, SII I p. 146); and the
   prince Kirtivarman asking, *svakula-vairiṇaḥ Kâñchîpatêr nigrahâya mâṁ prêshaya*, "send me to subdue
   the lord of Kanchi, the enemy of our house" (Vakkaleri ll. 54–55, Kielhorn EI V p. 204).

Runner-up: the curse on the Kanchi pillar names "the men of the assembly (?) of this city", the
*ghaṭige* of Kanchi, the institution Mayurasharma entered four centuries earlier (Talagunda v. 10):
Kadamba and Chalukya stories meet in one Kanchi word (link is `scholarship`, hedge it).
