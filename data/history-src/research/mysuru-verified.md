## 1. The house before him (context for the story's opening)

### 1a. Origin and the 1399 date
HR I p. 28 (archive text c. line 3547):
> The traditional date 1399, assigned in the Annals to the founding of the dynasty, remains, however,
> uncorroborated, there being, so far, hardly any reference to the Ruling House in Mysore, in the
> inscriptional and other records prior to c. 1550.

HR I p. 29:
> The circumstance that there is no reference to the early members of the House, in the records up to
> about 1550, is, perhaps, due to their having been purely local rulers within the jurisdiction of the
> Terakanambi or the Seringapatam province of the Vijayanagar Empire. The dynasty, it would seem,
> emerged from small beginnings into an important local power about the early years of the sixteenth
> century.

RICE 1897 p. 362 prints the palace list: "The following is the succession of the Mysore Rajas,
according to annals compiled in the palace, Vijaya being here called Yadu Raya : Yadu Raya, Vijaya
1399-1423 ... Raja Wodeyar (I) 1578-1617 ... Ranadhira Kanthirava-Narasa-Raja Wodeyar 1638-1659 ...
Dodda Deva-Raja Wodeyar 1659-1672 ... Chikka Deva-Raja Wodeyar 1672-1704 ... Kanthirava Wodeyar,
Muka-rasu 1704-1713 ... Dodda Krishna-Raja Wodeyar (I) 1713-1731 ... Krishna-Raja Wodeyar (II)
1734-1766 ... Krishna-Raja Wodeyar (III) 1799-1868 ... Chama-Rajendra Wodeyar (X) 1868-1894 ...
Krishna-Raja Wodeyar (IV) 1895". Tier: `scholarship` (palace chronicle list); 1399 is `legend`.

### 1b. Srirangapatna, 1610
HR I p. 59–60:
> he entered and formally took possession of the city of Seringapatam on February 8, 1610 (Saumya,
> Magha ba. 10). Thus passed into Mysore hands the old town of Seringapatam and the territory
> subordinate to it, which had been a Vijayanagar possession and subject to its direct rule for nearly
> a century and a half. The acquisition of Seringapatam by Raja Wodeyar was thus an act of conquest.

WILKS I p. 43 (archive line c. 3205):
> All that can be determined with certainty is, the quiet retirement of Tremul Raj to Talcaud, where
> he soon afterwards died ; and the peaceable occupation by Raj Wadeyar of the fort of Seringapatam,
> which thenceforth became the seat of the government of his family.

EC III Seringapatam 14 (1686), translation p. 9 (the dynasty's own account, 76 years later):
> Of these the first was Rajadharadhiraja, who according to his vow thrashed the proud lord of
> Karugahalli on the field of battle with his riding whip. Having conquered Tirumala-Raja, and taken
> S'rirangapattana, he seated himself on the throne and gained the dominion of a Sarva-bhauma.

Tier: `inscription` (EC III Sr 14) + `scholarship`. Disputed: conquest (HR, Sr 14) vs peaceable
occupation (Wilks).

## 2. Birth, youth, Hangala

### 2a. Birth and education
HR I p. 269:
> Born on September 22, 1645, Chikkadevaraja Wodeyar was, we learn, brought up in Mysore by his father
> Doddadevaraja Wodeyar, till the latter's renunciation and departure for the banks of the Kaundini
> (by 1659).
(fn 3: Mys. Dho. Pur. II. 56, "Parthiva, Asvija su. 12, Monday".) `scholarship` (palace chronicle).

HR I p. 270 (from Tirumalarya's C. Vam. 173–184):
> being educated and trained along sound lines and acquiring proficiency in the principles of drama,
> rhetoric, poetry and linguistics, in dialectics, Puranas, Dharma-sastras and politics, in music
> (including the lute), gymnastics, archery and swordsmanship, and in horse-riding and elephant-riding
> and various other manly exercises.

HR I p. 270: "In February 1662, he was married to Devajamma, daughter of Lingarajaiya of Yelandur,
and Devamma, daughter of (Dalavai) Kumaraiya of Kalale." `scholarship`.

### 2b. Hangala
HR I p. 271:
> In or about 1668, Devaraja accordingly sent him with his family to Hangala, a village in the south of
> Mysore in the present Gundlupet taluk. There arrangements were made for the continuance of his
> education and for affording him training befitting the character and dignity of the future ruler of
> the kingdom of Mysore.
(Same page: his uncle "resolved to keep him under some restraint at a place remote from
Seringapatam", because "From about 1667 onwards ... Chikkadevaraja, as a young man of twenty-two,
appears to have shown a tendency to fall off from his higher leanings".)

HR I p. 271 on the friends made there:
> During his stay in Hangala, Chikkadevaraja Wodeyar, we note, came into contact with Shadaksharaiya
> (Shadaksharadeva), Vira-saiva (Aradhya) preceptor to the family of Mudda-Bhupa of Yelandur, and with
> Visalaksha-Pandit, a learned Jaina Brahman of Yelandur, both of whom, together with Tirumalarya,
> became his friends and colleagues.
and: "it seems not improbable that the foundations of Chikkadevaraja's greatness as the ruler of
Mysore were securely laid in Hangala during c. 1668-1673."

WILKS I p. 88:
> His early youth had been passed at the remote town of Yellandoor, where he had formed an intimacy
> with Visha Lacksha Pundit, of the sect of Jain, who was afterwards more generally known by the
> appellation of the Jain Pundit, or Yellandoor Pundit. When, in the preceding reign, Chick Deo Raj and
> his father had been placed under restraint at the more obscure fort of Hengul, in the district of
> Goondul, the Pundit continued his attachment, and followed his friend to the place of his confinement.

RICE 1909 p. 128: "a rival claimant, afterwards Chikka-Deva-Raja, being sent, with his father, into
confinement at Hangala (Gundalpet taluq)."

Disputes: (1) when and why he went to Hangala (Wilks and Rice: confined with his father as a
passed-over claimant in the previous reign; HR: sent c. 1668 with his family, for restraint and
education); (2) whether he knew Visalaksha from boyhood at Yelandur (Wilks) or met him at Hangala
c. 1668 (HR fn 6, p. 270: "There is, however, no evidence in support of the position of either
Devachandra or Wilks"). Wilks I p. 57 fn also says father and son "were kept as prisoners at
Turkanamby during this reign" (a second place name).

### 2c. Tirumalarya
HR I p. 328:
> Born in 1645 (Parthiva), about the same time as Chikkadevaraja, he was, as depicted in his own works,
> brought up, and was intimately connected, with the latter as his co-student and colleague from his
> boyhood
and p. 329: "in the Chamarajanagar Plate (1675) he specifically refers to himself as having been
'like Brihaspati in the council of Chikkadevaraja.'" (EC IV Ch 92, ll. 106–107.) `inscription` for
the plate line, `scholarship` otherwise.

### 2d. Visalaksha Pandit
HR I p. 327:
> He was, we learn, a Jaina Brahman of Yelandur, son of Bommarasa-Pandit and Summambika.
HR I p. 328 (from Ananta-Kavi's *Belagolada Gommatesvara-Charitre*, c. 1780):
> the endowment of a glittering car (ratha) to God Gommatesvara at Sravana-Belagola and the grand
> performance, with the approval of his master, of the head-anointing ceremony (Mastaka-puje) in
> honour of that shrine, on March 5, 1677.
and: "From a lithic record dated in 1685, we further learn that he was familiarly known as 'Dodda-
Pandita of Yelandur.'" (EC III Nj 41.) Tier `scholarship`; the 1685 record is `inscription`.

The astrology tale, WILKS I p. 88:
> by the aspect of the stars he had discovered that Chick Deo Raj would certainly succeed to the
> throne. This prediction had, in their hours of confidence, been frequently repeated ; and Chick Deo
> Raj had been induced to declare, that in such event Visha Lacksha should become his prime minister.
HR I p. 272 fn 13: "His story of Visalaksha-Pandit bringing about the accession of Chikkadevaraja by
his personal influence, is not founded on fact." Tier: `legend`.

## 3. Accession and the first wars

### 3a. Accession, 1673
HR I p. 272:
> On February 28, 1673, sixteen days after the death of Devaraja Wodeyar, Chikkadevaraja Wodeyar was
> with due pomp and ceremony installed on the throne of Mysore in Seringapatam, Dalavai Kumaraiya
> having, it is said, brought him in state with his family from Hangala to the capital city. It was
> thus as a young man, just in his twenty-eighth year, that Chikkadevaraja Wodeyar began his reign
fn 12: Annals give 5 March 1673; Devachandra 16 October 1673; "Wilks (I. 105) places the accession
in 1672". WILKS I p. 87: "he now succeeded to the throne at the mature age of forty-five" (HR fn 14:
"not borne out by evidence"). Age disputed.

HR I p. 272 on the council:
> The first act of Chikkadevaraja, on his accession, was the formation of an executive council
> (mantralochana-sabhe) [dash] a sort of cabinet [dash] consisting of Visalaksha-Pandit as Prime
> Minister and Tirumalaiyangar (Tirumalarya of literary works), Shadaksharaiya, Chikkupadhyaya and
> Karanika Lingannaiya as Councillors

### 3b. Madhuvana, March 1673
HR I pp. 274–275:
> On March 5, 1673, i.e., on the fifth day after his installation (pattava-kattida-dinadol),
> Chikkadevaraja Wodeyar proceeded on an expedition towards the east, taking in rapid succession the
> forts of Dhuligote, Malali, Muttanjatti, Paramatti and Salem (Salya).
> ... encountered Chokkanatha himself at the head of his forces (consisting, we are told, of eight
> thousand horse, a lakh of foot and a hundred elephants) commanded by his Dalavai Venkatakrishnama
> Nayaka and lying in wait at Madhuvana on the borders of the forest region of Sadamangalam. In the
> action that took place, Chokkanatha was repulsed and hotly pursued ; his forces severely crushed,
> and several elephants, horses and valuables in his camp plundered and captured by the Mysore army.
Tier `scholarship` (from Mallikarjuna's *Sriranga-Mahatmya*, 1678, and other contemporary Kannada
works); the force figures are a court poet's and should be told as "it is said".

### 3c. Shivaji, 1677
HR I p. 283:
> About the middle of August 1677, Sivaji, on his way from Gingee to his ancestral possessions in the
> Karnatak, proceeded up to Seringapatam in southern Mysore.
(fn 46: Sarkar, *Shivaji*, p. 400 fn, citing Chitnis: "The details, however, are not given.")

ORME p. 63:
> he returned not to Rairee, as had hitherto been his usage, at the setting in of the rains ... and it
> is probable that he had reduced much more; for some of his parties at this time plundered as far as
> Seringapatam in Mysore.
(The OCR prints "1667"; the marginal date and context are 1677.)

ORME Notes p. 233–234, Bombay to Surat, 24 August 1677:
> SEVAGI is at present in the Upper Carnatic, where he has taken the strong castle of Chingy (Gingee)
> ... and 'tis believed has robbed Seringapatam, and carried away great riches from thence; and they
> say he designs, on his return back, to take Bridroor, and so join Canara to his own conquests.

HR I pp. 283–284 on the Kannada works:
> when Sivaji entered the country of the Kannadigas his attention towards Seringapatam was directed by
> the assemblage of local chiefs who had been subdued by Chikkadevaraja Wodeyar ; Sivaji, advancing at
> their head, surprised Chikkadevaraja (probably in the neighbourhood of Seringapatam) offering a
> covert fight ; Chikkadevaraja withstood Sivaji and was able to repulse him, causing disorder and loss
> in his ranks.
HR p. 284: "Chikkadevaraja Wodeyar is spoken of as having assumed the title Apratima-Vira
(unparalleled hero) after curbing the pride of the famous Sivaji". HR fn 53: "Curiously enough, other
contemporary writers like Chikkupadhyaya, Timma-Kavi and Mallikarjuna do not refer to this event".

RICE 1909 p. 130 (from EC III Sr 64 of 1722): "he conquered the lord of Madhura, and withstood Sivaji
at the time when the rulers of the countries around Agra, Delhi, and Bhaganagara (Haidarabad) were
falling down before him and presenting tribute. He thus acquired the title Apratima-vira (unrivalled
hero)".

**Dispute: did Shivaji come in person?** The English letter says "'tis believed"; Orme says "some of
his parties"; Chitnis via Sarkar says Shivaji went up to Seringapatam but gives no details; the Kannada
works (Tirumalarya, c. 1695–1700, and an inscription of 1722) say the king withstood Shivaji. No
casualty figures anywhere. Telling: Maratha horse under Shivaji's banner reach the island and are
beaten off; disclose.

### 3d. Trichinopoly and the Maratha attack on Srirangapatna, 1682
HR I p. 295–296:
> Mahratta cavalry led by Dadaji, Jaitaji and Nimbaji among others, taking advantage of the absence of
> the Mysore army from Seringapatam and of the serious predicament of Dalavai Kumaraiya at
> Trichinopoly, moved on from the east and the north of Mysore. ... they encamped in the neighbourhood
> of Seringapatam, on the fields of Kottatti and Honnalagere
> ... Doddaiya, under special instructions from Chikkadevaraja, proceeded against the Mahrattas, making
> a surprise night-attack on their camp and stupefying them by means of the illumination of torches
> carefully fastened to the horns of the oxen of the transport corps (two to three thousand in number)
> under him.
> ... Dadaji, Jaitaji and Nimbaji were themselves captured and slain on the battle-field, their noses,
> ears and limbs being cut off ; the head of Dadaji was paraded in the army (mandiyol mereyisi) and
> those of Jaitaji and Nimbaji were presented as trophies before Chikkadevaraja Wodeyar and later
> displayed on the Mysore Gate of the fort of Seringapatam.

WILKS I pp. 97–98 (his version of the same stratagem):
> In the evening the Dulwoy sent a small detachment in the direction opposite to that on which he had
> planned his attack ... This detachment was furnished with the requisite number of torches and an
> equal number of oxen, which were arranged at proper distances, with a flambeau tied to the horns of
> each ... So soon as it was perceived that the enemy were making a disposition to receive the army of
> torches, Dudeia silently approached their rear, and obtained an easy but most sanguinary victory.
and p. 98–99: the Raja next day had his officers presented "in the same military habits in which they
had fought, 'covered with the blood of his enemies;'".

EC III Seringapatam 14 (copper plate, 1686), translation p. 11 (contemporary, four years after):
> As formerly Hari, incarnate as Rama, subdued (there) Khara, Dushana and other giants, so (now) did
> he, incarnate as Chikka-Deva, subdue in Panchavati, Dadoji, Jaitaji and others in the form of
> Maratas, S'ambhu lost his valour, Kutupu-Shah failed in his purpose, Ikkeri-Basava was disgraced,
> Ekoji was deserted by all, when the mighty Chikka-Deva-Raja, having slain Dadoji, having cut off all
> the limbs and slit the noses of Jaitaji and Jasavata, set forth for war.
Tier `inscription`.

**Date dispute**: Wilks dates the Maratha attack 1697 and admits "This is one of the few dates which I
have failed in arranging to my satisfaction" (I p. 96 fn); HR Appendix VI-(1) argues c. April 1682
"as against Wilks's date"; the 1686 copper plate already records it, which rules out 1697. Use 1682.

HR I p. 297–298, Jesuit letter on Kumaraiya at Trichinopoly: "the combat was only a horrible butchery
... and made a large number of prisoners, among whom was Kumara Raya [Kumaraiya] himself." HR p. 298:
"on May 26, 1682 ... he retired from the office of Dalavai". `inscription` (contemporary letter).

ORME p. 141 (events of 1684):
> The country of Mysore was at this time divided amongst several Rajahs, of whom the most ancient and
> considerable was he of Seringapatam ; and all of them had paid tribute to Viziapore
`inscription` (Orme's Company records).

## 4. The post and the spies

WILKS I p. 89:
> Among the earliest measures of the new reign was the establishment for the first time of a regular
> post throughout his dominions. The post-office was not only, as in England, the passive instrument
> for conveying intelligence, but the active agent for obtaining it. The postmasters at the several
> stations were, in addition to their passive duties, what in the modern vocabulary of Europe would be
> named confidential agents of police ; and all the inferior servants of the department were professed
> spies, who made regular reports of the secret transactions of the district, which were as regularly
> transmitted to court
and: "this new and terrible instrument of despotism, which we shall hereafter find improved, and
actually organized, under the celebrated Hyder".

HR I p. 337: "A net-work of postal system was established, a news-carrier (anche-harikara) being
stationed over each division. It was his duty to look after the transmission of letters from place to
place and to report on matters coming within his direct knowledge." Also a torch-bearer
(divatigeya-jana) on each unit's staff. RICE 1897 p. 366 repeats Wilks. Tier `scholarship`.

## 5. Taxes, the Jangama rising, Nanjangud (the hardest chapter)

### 5a. Wilks's account (1810), the only detailed one
WILKS I p. 204 (the taxes):
> the Raja, with that profound knowledge of human nature which distinguished all his measures,
> exempted from these new imposts all the lands which were allotted to the provincial soldiery in lieu
> of pay
His footnote list (pp. 204–205) of twenty taxes includes "Menne Terege, or house-tax", a tax on straw,
on looms, marriage, shops, cattle sales, salt, washermen's kettles, flocks, fishing nets, firewood, and
"Teared Baguloo (opening a door) ... The husbandman paid it, as expressed by the name, for the
permission to open his door."

WILKS I pp. 205–206:
> One of the earliest measures of this Raja's reign had been to compel the dependant Wadeyars and
> Poligars ... publicly to renounce that assumption of independence ... This object was aided by first
> inviting, and then compelling them to fix their residence at Seringapatam
> ... the Jungum priests, deprived of their local importance, and much of their pecuniary receipts, by
> the removal of these mock courts from the provinces, were foremost in expressing their detestation of
> this new and unheard-of measure of finance, and in exhorting their disciples to resistance. Every
> where the inverted plough, suspended from the tree at the gate of the village, whose shade forms the
> coffee-house or the exchange of its inhabitants, announced a state of insurrection. Having determined
> not to till the land, the husbandmen deserted their villages, and assembled in some places like
> fugitives seeking a distant settlement; in others, as rebels breathing revenge.

WILKS I pp. 206–207 (the massacre):
> Before proceeding to measures of open violence, he adopted a plan of perfidy and horror, yielding in
> infamy to nothing which we find recorded in the annals of the most sanguinary people. An invitation
> was sent to all the priests of the Jungum to meet the Raja at the great temple of Nunjendgode, about
> fourteen miles south of Mysoor, ostensibly to converse with him on the subject of the refractory
> conduct of their followers. Treachery was apprehended, and the number which assembled was estimated
> at about four hundred only. A large pit had been previously prepared in a walled inclosure, connected
> by a series of squares composed of tent walls, with the canopy of audience, at which they were
> successively received one at a time, and after making their obeisance were desired to retire to a
> place, where, according to custom, they expected to find refreshments prepared at the expence of the
> Raja. Expert executioners were in waiting in the square, and every individual in succession was so
> skilfully beheaded, and tumbled into the pit, as to give no alarm to those who followed, and the
> business of the public audience went on without interruption or suspicion. Circular orders had been
> sent for the destruction, on the same day, of all the Jungum mûts (places of residence and worship)
> in his dominions ; and the number reported to have been in consequence destroyed was upwards of seven
> hundred. The disappearance of the four hundred Jungum priests was the only intimation of their fate
> received by their mournful disciples ; but the traditionary account which I have above delivered has
> been traced through several channels to sources of the most respectable information, and I profess my
> entire belief in the reality of the fact.
> ... The orders were distinct and simple ; to charge without parley into the midst of the mob ; to cut
> down in the first selection every man wearing an orange-coloured robe (the peculiar garb of the Jungum
> priests) ; and not to cease acting until the crowds had every where dispersed.
p. 207–208: "there is a tradition which I have not been able to authenticate, that the Raja exacted
from every village a written renunciation, ostensibly voluntary, of private property in the land".

**Correction to mysuru.md §3 and §5**: the number "about four hundred" is Wilks's own (p. 206),
not a 2003 addition. Wilks names beheading by "expert executioners", not strangling by wrestlers;
the "royal wrestlers" detail (Nagaraj 2003, via WP) has no 1810 source and is not used.

RICE 1897 p. 367 accepts it ("The Raja's resolution was prompt, but sanguinary and treacherous. He
invited all the Jangama priests to meet him at Nanjangud ... Only four hundred attended") and quotes
Wilks. RICE 1909 p. 129: "Many important administrative changes were made by him, some of which
created serious discontent. This was suppressed by a treacherous massacre of Jangama priests, who had
fomented it." Tier: `scholarship` resting on oral tradition gathered c. 1805–1810.

### 5b. The contemporary Jesuit letter (1686)
HR I p. 463 (quoting the letter of Louis de Mello to Noyelle, 1686, in S. K. Aiyangar's *Nayaks of
Madura* p. 288, and Bertrand's *La Mission du Madure* III):
> Attacked in the heart of his kingdom by the armies of Sambogi [Sambhaji], the king of Mysore, to
> provide for the expenses of the war, resorted, in the eastern provinces of his dominions, to
> exactions and cruelties so revolting that his subjects rose in a body against him and all his
> ministers.
> ... The king of Mysore, incensed at their (his subjects') insolence, sent an army against them to
> carry fire and sword everywhere, and toss the rebels on the point of the sword, without distinction
> of age or sex. These cruel orders were executed.
Tier `inscription` (contemporary letter, hearsay from a distance). It does not mention Nanjangud,
priests or a pit.

### 5c. Devachandra (1838)
HR I pp. 477–478 summarising *Rajavali-Kathe* XII: in October 1684 the Jangamas assembled "a huge
crowd of people, numbering nearly a lakh, on an extensive field near Tayur", appointed a king, minister
and commander from among themselves; on Visalaksha's counsel "Paridulla Khan (Faridulla Khan), an
officer commanding 200 horse" entered their camp feigning submission and "knocked them down with the
aid of his arrows"; Gurikar Nanje-Gauda of Kamaravalli captured "a thousand Jangamas"; the king "in
great wrath, had them all put to death". Tier `legend` (19th-c. Jain chronicler).

### 5d. Hayavadana Rao's verdict (1943)
HR I p. 484:
> there was some public disturbance in Mysore during 1684-1686, i.e., about the middle of
> Chikkadevaraja's reign : it appears to have been due not so much to religious persecution or
> political aggrandizement on the part of Chikkadeva as to fear engendered in the rural classes as to
> the effect of the fiscal measures introduced by him ... The disturbance that followed was quelled
> with a strong hand ; the ringleaders were put to death
HR I pp. 488–489:
> to say that the king ordered a general hunting down of the disguised and craven Jangamas, as
> Devachandra puts it, or ordered their massacre at Nanjangud at the hands of expert executioners, as
> detailed by Wilks, is to ask posterity to believe in a story which does not figure even in a cryptic
> form in the contemporary account of the Jesuit Fathers ; nor in the accounts of earlier Jain writers ;
> nor in any of the many inscriptions of the period
HR I p. 494: "It may be conceded that the minister crushed the insurrection with measures which were
harsh even for his times and the harshness, as a matter of course, came to be attached to his
sovereign". HR p. 353: towards the close of 1686 "the levy of house-tax and other imposts, altogether 19
in number, was systematised" (Annals I. 124). Note: HR is a court-sponsored history and openly
partisan for the king ("an intensely humane personality", p. 485); state his verdict as one side.

**How to tell it honestly (for the outline)**: a rising against new taxes certainly happened c. 1684–86
(Jesuit letter, Annals, Wilks, Devachandra agree); it was crushed with killing (all four agree). The
Nanjangud pit and the 400 are Wilks's tradition, believed by Wilks and Rice, rejected by Hayavadana Rao;
no contemporary document names Nanjangud. Tell the Nanjangud night as the tradition Wilks recorded, in
the story's voice, and put the dispute on the evidence page.

## 6. The murder of Visalaksha Pandit (1686)

WILKS I p. 90:
> The Pundit was attacked and mortally wounded, while returning at night, in the usual manner, from
> court to his own dwelling; and as, in addition to religious motives, the Jungum had a deep account of
> revenge to retaliate, for the murder of their priests ... the suspicion of this assassination fell
> chiefly upon that people
> ... He was much affected at the intelligence of this event, and immediately proceeded to the house of
> the minister to console him in his last moments, and to receive his advice regarding the choice of a
> successor. The advice was entirely unprejudiced, and he recommended, as the most able and honourable
> man of the court, a person of adverse religion, namely, Tremalayangar, a bramin of the sect of
> Vishnoo. To him the Raja gave his whole confidence ; and, in conformity to his advice, soon afterwards
> made an open profession of the doctrines of that prevailing religion.
(margin date 1686; Wilks p. 89: "The first fourteen years of this reign were occupied in these
financial measures".)

Devachandra via HR I p. 479: the Vira-Saivas "prevailed upon an individual by name Naganna, who had
practised at arms ... One day, as the Pandit was proceeding to the Palace seated in a palankeen
(sibigeyaneri), the hireling flung himself at him and pierced him through leaving him unconscious".
The king granted the Pandit's son Bommarasa the village of Yechiganahalli "as a rakta-kodige".

HR I p. 476 fn 13: "The latest available reference to Visalaksha is in a lithic record dated January
24, 1685 [E.C. III (1) Nj. 41] ... the death of Visalaksha-Pandit must be held to have occurred not
earlier than July 1686." HR p. 493: "it was the minister's own unpopularity that led to his death in
the streets of Seringapatam."

**Disputes**: who killed him (Wilks: suspicion on the Jangamas; Devachandra: a hireling named Naganna
paid by Vira-Saivas; HR: his own unpopularity, killer unknown); where (Wilks: on his way home at night;
Devachandra: on his way to the palace in a palanquin). Story telling: night, the street, a single
assailant, his last words naming Tirumalarya; disclose that the killer's name and patrons are not
known for certain.

## 7. Bengaluru, 1687, and Qasim Khan

WILKS I pp. 91–92:
> Eccojee in Tanjore finding his distant dominion of Bangalore to be an expensive and precarious
> possession ... wisely determined to sell it to the highest bidder. Chick Deo Raj finally agreed to be
> the purchaser ; at a price (three lacs of rupees) which sufficiently marks the public opinion of the
> instability of all possessions in those days of general convulsion
> ... Kasim Khan, the officer of Aurungzebe, making forced marches to the westward of the range of
> ghauts, arrived first, and the place being in the dismantled state which may be imagined when about
> to be sold, was incapable of making a proper defence, and yielded to Kasim Khan without material
> opposition. ... The imperial colours, however, were only hoisted for four days on the ramparts of
> Bangalore ; for Kasim Khan, who had more important objects in view, found that by accepting the price
> which the Raja was still willing to pay, he should, exclusively of a pecuniary aid, be relieved from
> the necessity of making a large detachment for the occupation of the place ... it was accordingly
> delivered in July 1687 to the troops of Chick Deo Raj.
WILKS I p. 96 fn: "According to Pootia's manuscript, the flag of Mysoor was hoisted at Bangalore on the
29th of July 1687". WILKS I pp. 211–212: "Bangalore was captured in the year Prabava on the 11th
Ashadum, by Causim Khan from the house of Eccojee, and on the 15th of the same month it was occupied by
the people of the Raja."

HR I pp. 306–307:
> He accordingly proposed to sell it to Chikkadevaraja Wodeyar for rupees three lakhs. A vakil was sent
> from the court of Tanjore to Seringapatam to conduct the negotiations. ... Meanwhile, Khasim Khan,
> advancing with the Mughal detachment, had occupied the place, finally hoisting the imperial flag over
> the fort on July 10, 1687. ... At this juncture, Chikkadevaraja Wodeyar stood before the walls of
> Bangalore. A fight took place between the forces of Mysore and the Mughal troops, in which the latter
> were thoroughly put to rout and Chikkadevaraja took possession of Bangalore on July 14.
and p. 307: "The ultimate delivery of Bangalore to Chikkadevaraja Wodeyar was, however, it would seem,
effected by Khasim Khan under an amicable arrangement ... On the completion of this arrangement, Khasim
Khan retired to Sira, where he remained as the Mughal Governor (Faujdar)." fn 128: Wilks's purchase
"from Khasim Khan, for which there is no evidence".

ORME pp. 155–156: the Mughal army "marching west of the Carnatic mountains invested Bengalore, before
the Morattoe generals had concerted their measures for the same attack ... The three generals agreed
to march to the relief of Bengalore; but on the way heard that the place had surrendered to the
Mogul's army. This happened in the beginning of August". p. 157: "The Mogul's troops having taken
Bengalore were reinforced before Keisswa Puntolo arrived in Mysore". Tier `inscription` (Madras
records).

RICE 1897 p. 368: "the sale at this time of Bangalore by Venkoji, or Ekoji, to the Mysore Raja for
three lakhs of rupees ; its seizure by Khasim Khan, the Mughal general, before the entry of the Mysore
troops, and the conclusion of the bargain notwithstanding".

**Disputes**: whom the money went to (Wilks: to Qasim Khan; HR: to Ekoji, the sale was done before the
Mughals arrived); whether there was a fight (Kannada works: the Mughals were routed; Wilks, Orme: no);
dates (flag 10 July per Mys. Dho. Pur.; Mysore entry 14/15 July; Wilks/Pootia 29 July; Orme "beginning
of August"). Agreed: three lakhs, Ekoji the seller, Qasim Khan there first, Mysore holding it by
the end of the summer, Qasim Khan afterwards Faujdar at Sira.

Qasim Khan in stone: EC IX Dod-Ballapur Taluq 31 (Persian, 1691): "in the year 32 of his reign,
corresponding with the Hijri year 1101, the fort of Balapur ... was in the hands of the Samba family.
It then passed from the hands of Samba-dad, son of Siva-dad Marhatta, through the exertions of Khasim
Khan, Faujdar of the Karnatak Government, a dependency of the Subah of Bijapur, and came into the
possession of the Supreme Government." `inscription`.

WILKS I p. 99: "Kasim Khan, the friend and protector of the Raja at the court of Aurungzebe, who had
for many years held some of the highest offices of the state, died in the following year" (margin
1698).

Chikkadevaraja in Bengaluru: EC IX introduction: "Bn 118 states that the Venkatesvara temple in the
Bangalore Fort was erected by Deva-Raja, that is Chikka-Deva-Raja, and endowed by his son
Kanthirava-Narasa-Raja. It immediately adjoins Tipu Sultan's palace on the north-east." (Bn 118 is
dated 1705.) `inscription`. This is today's Kote Venkataramana temple.

## 8. The embassy to Aurangzeb, 1699–1700, and the seal

WILKS I pp. 99–100:
> The embassy which departed in the year 1699 found the imperial court at Ahmednuggur, and returned in
> the year 1700. The Dulwoy and other great officers of state were sent out in due form to receive the
> supposed letter, presents, and insignia of honour despatched by the emperor, which were carried in
> solemn procession through the town ; and after being exhibited in the great temple at the feet of
> the idol Sree Runga, were brought in similar state to the palace. Among the presents was a new signet
> prepared by the emperor's direction, bearing the title of Jug Deo Raj, which was thenceforth
> employed ; and part of the ceremonial was the new dignity alleged to have been conferred by the
> emperor of being seated on an ivory throne. This was afterwards used by his successors, and is the
> same which, in the year 1799, was found in a lumber-room of Tippoo Sultaun's palace
WILKS I p. 99 and fn:
> The splendour of the embassy does not, however, appear to have made much impression at the imperial
> court; and if we may judge from the trifling sum* recorded to have been expended in the entertainment
> of the ambassadors, the Zemindar of Mysoor (as he is called) was not held to be a person of very high
> consideration.
> * Two hundred rupees. Notes on the Asophia Dufter, communicated by Colonel W. Kirkpatrick to Major
> Mackenzie.

HR I p. 319:
> The embassy, we are told, was led by Karanika Lingannaiya, one of the councillors of Chikkadevaraja,
> appointed Vakil to represent Mysore at the court of the Mughal. Lingannaiya presented the Padshah with
> rich khillats, and met with a favourable reception at his hands.
> ... he conferred on him the great title "Raja-Jagadev" ("King of the World"), a seal engraved in
> Persian characters, with the words "Raja-Chikkadevaraj-Muhammad-Shayi," and numerous insignias (such
> as the red ensign, the Hanuma, Garuda, Makara, Ganda-bherunda, Dharani-Varaha and other emblems of
> sovereignty), and sent him costly presents with a friendly letter recognizing Chikkadevaraja's right
> to hold Durbar seated on the "celebrated throne of the Pandavas."
HR I p. 320: "Although we have so far no independent evidence confirmatory of this account, there
seems nothing inherently improbable in it". HR fn 178 (p. 321): Wilks "hardly specifies his authority
... it is difficult to accept Wilks's position in regard to the 'throne,' which implies there was no
throne at all in Mysore before 1699-1700" (the kings sat on a "jewelled throne (ratna-simhasana)"
since 1610).

The seal as later used: Commons File:Seal of Krishnaraja Wadiyar II.jpg (from Constance Parsons,
*Mysore City*, 1930; PD), described there as "Seal of Krishnaraja Wadiyar II, based on the seal of
Chikka Devaraja"; the printed caption reads "1163 AHMED SHAH RAJA JAGADEV RAJ SAN 2 / THE MYSORE
ROYAL SEAL" (a round Persian seal, AH 1163 = 1750, i.e. under Krishnaraja II, not the 1700 original).

**UNVERIFIED**: any text of Aurangzeb's farman or letter; any Mughal-side record of the embassy other
than Wilks's 200-rupee note from the Asaf Jahi records (secondhand through Kirkpatrick). No farman was
found in Aitchison, the EC volumes or HR. Tell the procession and the seal as the Mysore court's
record; mark the Mughal honours "as the Mysore records say".

## 9. Eighteen departments, treasury, canal

WILKS I pp. 100–101:
> It was soon after the return of this embassy that he is also stated, in some manuscripts, to have
> distributed the business of the government into eighteen cutcheries or departments, in consequence of
> learning from the ambassadors that such was the practice of the imperial government, and consequently
> fit to be adopted by so great a prince as Jug Deo Raj (the sovereign of the world) ; but this
> arrangement is referred by others to an earlier period.
His list includes "14. Beakin Chaouree (the department of expedition), or the post-office: the business
of espionage belonged also to this department", "12. Benne Chaouree: benne, butter (the butter
department)", "17. Cubbin Chaouree, iron cutchery", "18. Hooge Suppin; the smoking leaf or tobacco".

HR I pp. 355–357: "Organization of the eighteen departments, c. 1700-1704" with the Kannada names,
e.g. "(1) Nirupada-chavadi ... (7) Sunkada-chavadi: customs department ... (12) Beneya-chavadi ...
(14) Behina-chavadi: department of intelligence [dash] dealing with the speedy transmission by couriers
(anche-harikara) of reports of events from the administrative units to the capital ... (17)
Kabbinada-chavadi ... (18) Hogesoppina-chavadi". p. 358: "The idea of eighteen departments
(Athara-gucheri) may have been borrowed from Mughal administrative practice and procedure but the
details of the departments ... were essentially indigenous". Devachandra puts them in 1686 (HR p. 479).

WILKS I p. 102:
> It was his fixed practice, after the performance of his morning ablutions, and marking his forehead
> with the upright insignia of Vishnoo, to deposit two bags (thousands) of pagodas in this treasury from
> the cash despatched from the districts, before he proceeded to break his fast. If there were any delay
> in bringing the money he also delayed his breakfast
> ... he had accumulated a treasure, from which he obtained the designation of Nou-kote-Narrain, or the
> lord of nine crores (of pagodas)

HR I pp. 377–378 (canal and the Jesuit letter of 1701, Father Martin to Father de Villette):
> Damming the Cauvery to the west of Seringapatam, we are told, he had canals excavated from both sides
> of the river, the northern canal being led on to a considerable distance by way of the Karighatta
> hill and named after himself as Chikkadevaraja-sagara, and the southern canal ... designated as
> Deva-nala.
Jesuit letter: "had not the king of Maissoor [Mysore] stopped their course by a prodigious mole he
raised and which extended the whole breadth of the canal ... the instant they fell abundantly, the
river swelled to such a degree that it broke the mole and dragged it impetuously along." `inscription`
(contemporary letter).

## 10. Faith, writings, appearance, titles

HR I p. 359 ff (ch. XIII): June 1693, courtiers of other sects "were, agreeably to a representation of
Tirumalaiyangar, desired to wear the Sri-Vaishnava marks (namam) on their foreheads while attending at
the Palace during the king's Namatirtham." Wilks I p. 200: "There is little doubt that he was educated
in the principles of the Jungum; but he openly conformed to the ceremonial of the Vishnoo". HR p. 485:
"a devout Sri-Vaishnava by faith and by profession". Disputed: his faith before 1686.

Writings, HR I pp. 443–444: songs "composed in the saptapadi and tripadi metres ... Chikkadevaraya-
Saptapadi and Tripadigala-Tatparya" (c. 1690–1695); "two Kannada productions entitled the
Chikkadevaraja-Binnapam and Gita-Gopalam ... Both begin with invocation to Vishnu (as Yadugiri-Narayana),
and both are assignable to the period c. 1700-1704"; the *Sachchudrachara-Nirnaya* (c. 1687–1690) in
Sanskrit "with a view to bring about a much-needed social reform, namely, the uplift of the Sudras".
The *Binnapam* was printed in the Karnataka-Kavya-Kalanidhi series, Mysore 1906 (HR fn 132). **UNVERIFIED**:
no public-domain English translation of the Binnapam, Gita-Gopalam, Vamsavali or Vijayam was found;
only HR's Kannada quotations (OCR-garbled) were reached.

Appearance, HR I p. 501 (from contemporary Kannada poets):
> a handsome personage (Chenniga Chikadevaraya), with features characteristic of a great man destined
> to rule as a sovereign [dash] features suggestive of budding manhood, charming round face, large
> lotus-like eyes, well-proportioned nose, soft arms, round chest, well-built thighs, tender feet, white
> complexioned body, pleasing countenance and excellent voice.
HR p. 502: he would "deftly cut off his adversaries ... at the point of his famous sword Nandaka and
make himself conspicuous by displaying his shining standard, the Garuda-dhvaja". (Poetic; `legend`
tier for the specifics, `scholarship` that the poets describe him so.)

Court dress, HR I p. 392 (contemporary poems):
> Dressed in glittering and well-bordered upper garment of white silk ..., wearing the richly
> embroidered coatee (kanchuka) and the crown ..., having the tika on his forehead, his person
> beautified with badges (pendeya), medallions (padaka), necklaces ..., ear-rings ..., bracelets ...,
> wristlets ..., sashes (kati-sutra) and anklets ... set with pearls and precious stones ..., his knot
> of hair tied up with fragrant flowers ..., with ornamented sandals ..., and with the jewelled dagger
> (rannada chikkathari) in his hand
he went to the Durbar Hall "Seated in the golden palankeen", occupying "the jewelled throne
(ratna-simhasana, simha-pitha)". The hall was "known as Saundarya-vilasa". Scholars at court received
"silken garments ... silken head-dress (pattaganitam), ear-rings ... and finger-rings" (p. 390).

Titles, HR I p. 508: "Dharani-Varaha (boar), Ganda-bherunda (double-headed eagle), ... Apratima-Vira
(unparalleled hero)", "Dakshinadik-Chakravarti, Tenkana-Raya (Emperor of the South), ...
Karnataka-Chakravarti". EC IV Ch 92 (1675) via RICE 1909 p. 129: "seated on the throne of the Karnata
dominion like the great Indra". `inscription`.
