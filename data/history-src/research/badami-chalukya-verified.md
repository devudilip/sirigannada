## 1. Aihole prashasti (ಐಹೊಳೆ ಶಾಸನ), Kielhorn's translation, verses 11–37 — VERBATIM

Source: F. Kielhorn, "Aihole inscription of Pulikesin II.; Saka-Samvat 556", Epigraphia Indica VI
(1900–01) pp. 1–12, hosted at
- https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol6_1900-01/kielhorn8.html (v. 4–13)
- https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol6_1900-01/kielhorn9.html (v. 14–18a)
- https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol6_1900-01/kielhorn10.html (v. 18b–25)
- https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol6_1900-01/kielhorn11.html (v. 26–33)
- https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol6_1900-01/kielhorn12.html (v. 34–37 + fn.)
(kielhorn7.html carries v. 1–3 only.) Tier: `inscription` (public domain, 1900). Kielhorn's own
convention (his fn. 3 on v. 15): "In my translation I have written the pronoun, when it refers to
Polekêśin, with an initial capital letter." Bracketed numbers are his footnote markers; the footnotes
that matter for the story are copied after the verses.

(V. 11.) When his desire was bent on the dominion of the lord of the gods,[7] his younger brother
Maṅgalêśa became king, who by the sheets of dust of his army of horse, encamped on the shores of the
eastern and western seas, stretched an awning over the quarters.[8]

(V. 12.) Who in that house which was the battle-field took in marriage the damsel, the Fortune of the
Kaṭachchuris, having scattered the gathering gloom, (viz.) the array of elephants (of the adversary),
with hundreds of bright-rayed lamps, (viz.) the swords (of his followers).

(V. 13.) And again, when he was desirous of taking the island of Rêvatî, his great army with many
bright banners, which had ascended the ramparts, as it was reflected in the water of the sea appeared
like Varuṇa's forces, quickly come there at once at his word (of command).

(V. 14.) When his elder brother's son, named Polekêśin, of a dignity like Nahusha's, was coveted by
Fortune,[1] and finding his uncle to be jealous of him thereat, had formed the resolution to wander
abroad as an exile,[2]─

(V. 15.) That Maṅgalêśa, whose great strength became on all sides reduced by the application of the
powers of good counsel and energy gathered by Him,[3] abandoned, together with the effort to secure
the kingdom for[4] his own son, both that no mean kingdom of his and his life.

(V. 16.) Then, on the subversion of that rule encompassed by the darkness of enemies, the whole world
grew light again, invaded as it were by the lustrous rays of His irresistible splendor. Or when was
it that the sky ceased to be black like a swarm of bees with thundering clouds, in which flashes of
lightning were dancing like banners, and the edges of which were crushed in the rushing wind ?[5]

(V. 17.) When, having found the opportunity, he who was named Âppâyika, and Gôvinda approached with
their troops of elephants to conquer the country north of the Bhaimarathî, the one in battle through
His armies came to know the taste of fear,[6] while the other at once received the reward of the
services rendered by him.

(V. 18.) When He was besieging Vanavâsî, which for a girdle[7] has the rows of haṁsa birds that sport
on the high waves of the Varadâ as their play-place, and which by its wealth rivalled the city of the
gods, that fortress on land, having the surface of the earth all around covered with the great sea of
his army, to the looker-on seemed at once converted into a fortress in the water.

(V. 19.) Although in former days they had acquired happiness by renouncing the seven sins, the Gaṅga
and Âḷupa lords, being subdued by His dignity, were always intoxicated by drinking the nectar of close
attendance upon him.[1]

(V. 20.) In the Koṅkaṇas the impetuous waves of the forces directed by Him speedily swept away the
rising wavelets of pools[2]─ the Mauryas.

(V. 21.) When, radiant like the destroyer[3] of Pura, He besieged Purî, the Fortune of the western
sea, with hundreds of ships in appearance like arrays of rutting elephants, the sky, dark-blue as a
young lotus and covered with tiers of massive clouds, resembled the sea, and the sea was like the
sky.[4]

(V. 22.) Subdued by His splendor, the Lâṭas, Mâḷavas and Gûrjaras became as it were teachers of how
feudatories, subdued by force, ought to behave.[5]

(V. 23.) Harsha, whose lotus-feet were arrayed with the rays of the jewels of the diadems of hosts of
feudatories prosperous with unmeasured might, through Him had his mirth (harsha) melted away by fear,
having become loathsome with his rows of lordly elephants fallen in battle.

(V. 24.) While He was ruling the earth with his broad armies, the neighbourhood of the Vindhya, by no
means destitute of the lustre of the many sandbanks of the Rêvâ, shone even more brightly by his great
personal splendor, having to be avoided by his elephants because, as it seemed, they by their bulk
rivalled the mountains.[6]

(V. 25.) Almost[7] equal to Indra, He by means of all the three powers, gathered by him according to
rule, and by his noble birth[8] and other excellent qualities, acquired the sovereignty over the three
Mahârâshṭrakas with their nine and ninety thousand villages.

(V. 26.) Through the excellencies of their householders prominent in the pursuit of the three objects
of life, and having broken the pride of other rulers of the earth, the Kaliṅgas with the Kôsalas by
His army were made to evince signs of fear.

(V. 27.) Hard pressed (pishṭa) by Him, Pishṭapura became a fortress not difficult of access ;
wonderful (to relate), the ways of the Kali age to Him[1] were inaccessible !

(V. 28.) Ravaged by Him, the water of Kunâḷa[2]─ coloured with the blood of men killed with many
weapons, and the land within it overspread with arrays of accoutered elephants─ was like the
cloud-covered sky in which the red evening-twilight has risen.[3]

(V. 29.) With his sixfold forces,[4] the hereditary troops and the rest, who raised spotless chowries,
hundreds of flags, umbrellas, and darkness,[5] and who churned the enemy elated with the sentiments of
heroism and energy, He caused the splendour of the lord of the Pallavas, who had opposed the rise of
his power, to be obscured by the dust of his army, and to vanish behind the walls of Kâñchîpura.[6]

(V. 30.) When straightway He strove to conquer the Chôḷas, the Kâvêrî, who has the darting carps for
her tremulous eyes, had her current obstructed by the causeway formed by his elephants whose
rutting-juice was dripping down, and avoided the contact with the ocean.[7]

(V. 31.) There He caused great prosperity to the Chôḷas, Kêraḷas and Pâṇḍyas, he being the hot-rayed
sun to the hoar-frost─ the army of the Pallavas.

(V. 32.) While He, Satyâśraya, endowed with the powers of energy, mastery and good counsel,─ having
conquered all the quarters, having dismissed the kings full of honours, having done homage to gods
and Brâhmaṇs, having entered the city of Vâtâpî─ is ruling, like one city, this earth which has the
dark-blue waters of the surging sea for its moat ;[8]

(V. 33.) (Now) when thirty (and) three thousand and five years besides, joined with seven hundred
years, have passed since the Bhârata war ;

(V. 34.) And when fifty (and) six and five hundred years of the Śaka kings also have gone by in Kali
age ;

(V. 35.) This stone mansion of Jinêndra, a mansion of every kind of greatness, has been caused to be
built by the wise Ravikîrti, who has obtained the highest favour of that Satyâśraya whose rule is
bounded by the three oceans.

(V. 36.) Of this eulogy and of this dwelling of the Jina revered in the three worlds,[1] the wise
Ravikîrti himself is the author and also the founder.

(V. 37.) May that Ravikîrti be victorious, who full of discernment has used the abode of the Jina,
firmly built of stone, for a new treatment of his theme,[2] and who thus by his poetic skill has
attained to the fame of Kâḷidâsa and of Bhâravi ![3]

### Kielhorn's footnotes that bear on the story (verbatim, selected)
- p. 8 fn. 7 (on v. 11 "dominion of the lord of the gods"): "I.e. when he died."
- p. 9 fn. 1 (v. 14): "A comparison with Ragh. V. 38 suggests the interpretation that it was desired to
  confer on Polekêśin the dignity of Yuvarâja, or heir apparent."
- p. 9 fn. 2 (v. 14, on aparuddha): "The verb apa-rudh means ' to debar, to shut out from, to banish,
  to exile ;' ... From all this it is clear that what our poet wishes us to understand, is, that
  Polekêśin, either banished by Maṅgalêśa or having left the country from fear of him, went to
  neighbouring princes and asked their assistance in the recovery of his rights."
- p. 9 fn. 5 (v. 16): "The first half of the verse states that, as the rising sun dissipates the
  darkness of night, so Polekêśin dispersed the enemies who on the destruction of Maṅgalêśa's rule
  (literally, of the umbrella which is the sign of sovereignty) on all sides beset the realm."
- p. 10 fn. 6 (v. 24): "Really the mountainous country of the Vindhya had to be avoided by the king's
  elephants, because it was impassable for them ; but the poet's reason is, that the elephants were
  higher than the Vindhya. If they had gone there, the Vindhya by the presence of these mountain-like
  elephants would have transgressed the command of the sage Agastya ... that it should not grow higher
  so long as Agastya remained in the south."
- p. 10 fn. 7 (v. 25): "He was like Indra because, like that deity, he possessed certain śaktis ; but
  was inferior to him because his śaktis were only three (the powers of mastery, good counsel, and
  energy), while Indra possesses eight Śaktis".
- p. 11 fn. 2 (v. 28): "I.e. the Kunâḷa (Kolanu, Kolleru) lake".
- p. 11 fn. 6 (v. 29): "The splendour of the Pallava first (when he was defeated in the open) was only
  obscured ; afterwards (when he had to retire within the walls of his fortress) it entirely vanished.
  The poet of course wishes us to understand that the splendor of the Pallava is compared with the
  sun."
- p. 11 fn. 7 (v. 30): "... in his verse the real reason for the Kâvêrî's keeping away from the ocean
  is, that her current was obstructed by the bulky elephants on which Polekêśin crossed the river."
- p. 12 fn. 1 (v. 36): "Or ' the preceptor of the three worlds.'"
- p. 12 fn. 2 (v. 37): "Viz. the history of the Chalukyas."
- p. 12 fn. 3: "I purposely omit from my translation the line which follows in the original, and
  which is a later addition to the poem. The first part of it enumerates six villages, the revenues of
  which apparently were assigned to the temple of Jinêndra founder by Ravikîrti. The concluding part
  of it, which speaks of boundaries, I do not understand."

## 2. Kuram plates of Paramesvaravarman I (SII I no. 151, Hultzsch) — VERBATIM + Sanskrit

Sources read:
- https://www.whatisindia.com/inscriptions/south_indian_inscriptions/volume_1/kuram.html (Hultzsch
  1890, SII I pp. 144 ff.; OCR has mojibake quotes but the words are intact)
- https://dharmalekha.info/texts/INSSIIv01p0i0151 (DHARMA digital edition of the same, with the
  Sanskrit text — clean diacritics; fetched with curl)
Tier: `inscription`.

Hultzsch's summary (SII I p. 144): "Of Narasiṁhavarman it says, that he "repeatedly defeated the Choḷas,
Keraḷas, Kaḷabhras and Pāṇḍyas," that he "wrote the (three) syllables of (the word) vijaya (i.e.,
victory), as on a plate, on Pulakeśin's back, which was caused to be visible (i.e., whom he caused to
turn his back) in the battles of Pariyaḷa, Maṇimaṅgala, Śūramāra, etc.," and that he "destroyed (the
city of) Vātāpi." No historical information is given about Mahendravarman, who, accordingly, seems to
have been an insignificant ruler. A laudatory description of the virtues and deeds of his son
Parameśvaravarman fills two plates of the inscription. The only historical fact contained in this long
and difficult passage is that, in a terrible battle, he "made Vikramāditya,—whose army consisted of
several lakshas,—take to flight, covered only by a rag.""

Hultzsch's translation, line 12 ff. (verbatim): "(Line 12.) The grandson of Narasiṁhavarman, (who
arose) from the kings of this race, just as the moon and the sun from the eastern mountain; who was
the crest-jewel on the head of those princes, who had never bowed their heads (before); who proved a
lion to the elephant-herd of hostile kings; who appeared to be the blessed Narasiṁha himself, who had
come down (to earth) in the shape of a prince; who repeatedly defeated the Choḷas, Keraḷas, Kaḷabhras,
and Pāṇḍyas; who, like Sahasrabāhu (i.e., the thousand-armed Kārtavīrya), enjoyed the action of a
thousand arms in hundreds of fights; who wrote the (three) syllables of (the word) vijaya (i.e.,
victory), as on a plate, on Pulakeśin's back, which was caused to be visible (i.e., whom he caused to
turn his back) in the battles of Pariyaḷa, Maṇimaṅgala, Śūramāra, etc.; and who destroyed (the city
of) Vātāpi, just as the pitcher-born (Agastya) (the demon) Vātāpi;—"

Sanskrit (DHARMA edition, plate 2v, lines 15–17):
"muhuravajitacoḷakeraḷakaḷabhrapāṇḍyasya sahasrabāhoriva samaraśatanirvviṣṭasahasrabāhukarmmaṇaḥ
pariyaḷamaṇimaṁgalaśūramāraprabhṛtiraṇavidarśitapulakeśiṣṭaṣṭhapaṭṭalikhitavijayākṣarasya
kalaśayoneriva vimathitavātāpeḥ pautro"
— i.e. the key compound is pariyaḷa-maṇimaṁgala-śūramāra-prabhṛti-raṇa-vidarśita-Pulakeśi-pṛṣṭha-
paṭṭa-likhita-vijaya-akṣarasya "(of him) by whom the syllables vijaya were written as on a paṭṭa
(plate/tablet) on the back (pṛṣṭha) of Pulakeśi, shown (= made to turn) in the battles of Pariyaḷa,
Maṇimaṅgala, Śūramāra etc.", and kalaśayoner iva vimathita-Vātāpeḥ "who crushed Vātāpi as the
pitcher-born (Agastya) did [the demon] Vātāpi". The word "three" is Hultzsch's gloss (vi-ja-ya = three
syllables); the plate itself says vijayākṣara "the syllables of vijaya". The edition prints
"ṣṭaṣṭha" for the expected pṛṣṭha (engraver's/editor's form; not re-read from the plate here).

Also on the same page (Hultzsch, p. 145): "Vallabharāja corresponds to the Pulakeśin of the Kūram
grant"; the Udayendiram grant of Nandivarman Pallavamalla "likewise states, that he "destroyed Vātāpi"
and that he "frequently defeated Vallabharāja at Pariyaḷa, Maṇimaṅgala, Śūramāra, and other
(places)."" and that Parameśvaravarman I "defeated the army of Vallabha in the battle of
Peruvaḷanallūr" (fn.: "line 16, Peruvaḷanallū[r]yyuddhe"). Hultzsch: the Chalukyas "more indico,
likewise boast of having conquered their antagonists" — quoting a Pulikesin II grant "he caused the
leader of the Pallavas to hide his prowess behind the ramparts of Kāñchīpura" (Ind. Ant. VIII p. 245)
and a Vikramaditya I grant "this lord of the earth, conquering Īśvarapotarāja (i.e., Parameśvaravarman
I.), took Kāñchī ..." (Ind. Ant. VI p. 77), whose corrupt preceding passage names "Śrīvallabha, who had
crushed the fame of Narasiṁha, destroyed the power of Mahendra and surpassed Īśvara by his polity."
Date of the Kuram plates: none stated on the page (Hultzsch gives only the Chalukya Saka dates 532/556
and 592?/602?); the grant is Paramesvaravarman I's, so c. 670–690 by reign — `scholarship`.

## 3. Badami rock inscription of Narasimhavarman I Mahamalla (SII XI pt. 1 no. 1) — VERBATIM

Source: https://dharmalekha.info/texts/INSSIIv11p0i0001 (DHARMA digital edition of SII XI.1 no. 1,
Krishnamacharlu, Panchamukhi, Lakshminarayan Rao & Narasimhaswami 1940, p. 1; first published Ind.
Ant. IX p. 99 [Fleet]). Tier: `inscription`.

Heading: "No. 1. (B.K. No. 100 of 1928-29). BĀDĀMI, BADAMI TALUK, BIJAPUR DISTRICT. ON A ROCK BEHIND
THE TEMPLE OF MALLIKĀRJUNADĒVA."

Editors' summary (verbatim): "This inscription in Pallava-Grantha characters is much obliterated. Of
the king's name, only the epithet or surname 'Mahāmalla' is preserved, and the record has to be
assigned to king Narasiṁhavarman I of the Pallava dynasty of Kāñchī. It is dated in the 13th year of
the king's reign. From the characters it may be ascribed to about the 7th century A.D. Bādāmi is
herein mentioned under the ancient name of 'Vātāpi.' According to Dr. Fleet, the present inscription
will have to be assigned to the end of the reign of Pulakēśin II." (note: Bomb. Gaz. Vol. I, pt. II,
p. 328). Language: Sanskrit, Grantha script.

Text (9 lines, bracketed numbers = lost akṣaras; parentheses = unclear):
⟨1⟩ [12+] (saṁ)vatsare Ātmano rājyavarṣe ca varddhamāne tra(yodaśe) (|)
⟨2⟩ [3+] siṁhena mahāma(lle)na vidviṣām vātāpiratimā(nena) [6+]
⟨3⟩ bhāradvāja(malaṁka)riṣṇuratulaṁ gotraṁ guṇairātmano [10+]
⟨4⟩ [2+] (ta)yaḥ kṣitibhujāmagresaraḥ palla [9+]
⟨5⟩ [6+] śiṁhaviṣṇura(dhi)paḥ stambhañjaya(śrī) [7+]
⟨6⟩ [3+] (maṇḍa)pairbbahumate prakhyāta(khaṇḍopale) [7+]
⟨7⟩ [7+] sti śakavarṣe ṣa(ṣṭya) [8+]
⟨8⟩ [6+] śame [15+]
⟨9⟩ [9+]
Apparatus: "⟨1⟩ tra(yodaśe) • The letter śe has been read from the stone." Commentary: "Lines 7 and 8
are engraved in the Chālukyan characters of the 7th century A.D. and from a separate inscription."

What this gives the writer: the regnal year is "rājyavarṣe ca varddhamāne tra(yodaśe)" = "in the
increasing thirteenth year of his own reign" (the "13" rests on a partly-read akṣara). The word for
Vatapi is "vātāpi" in line 2, in the compound "vidviṣām vātāpiratimā(nena)" — fragmentary; the line
reads "...siṁhena mahāma(lle)na" = "by [Narasiṁha], by Mahāmalla". The stone names the place Vātāpi,
not Badami. Line 7's "śakavarṣe ṣa(ṣṭya)..." is a separate, later Chalukya inscription (Saka 6x?), not
Mahamalla's. The Fleet observation that it belongs to the end of Pulakeshin II's reign is Fleet's
inference (Bombay Gazetteer I.ii p. 328), `scholarship`.

Note for Story B beat 3: "graffito" is a fair description; it is a nine-line, mostly lost, formal
Sanskrit record, not a scratched name. "much obliterated" is the editors' phrase.

## 7. Ship depictions for the visual bible (pre-800 CE, India) — with Commons links

### 7a. Ajanta Cave 2, the three-masted sea-going ship (c. 5th–6th c., Vakataka/post-Vakataka)
Best public-domain description: Griffiths 1896 as quoted in R. K. Mookerji, Indian Shipping (1912)
pp. 40–41 (https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfIndianShipping, OCR), `scholarship` (PD):
"The representations of ships and boats furnished by Ajanta paintings are mostly in Cave No. 2, of
which the date is, as we have seen, placed between 525-650 a.d." ... "the first shows "a sea-going
vessel with high stem and stern, with three oblong sails attached to as many upright masts. Each mast
is surmounted by a truck, and there is carried a lug-sail. The jib is well filled with wind. A sort of
bowsprit, projecting from a kind of gallows on deck, is indicated with the out-flying jib, square in
form," like that borne till recent times by European vessels. The ship appears to be decked and has
ports. Steering-oars hang in sockets or rowlocks on the quarter, and eyes are painted on the bows.
There is also an oar behind ; and under the awning are a number of jars, while two small platforms
project fore and aft." (Mookerji's fn.: Griffiths, The Paintings in the Buddhist Cave-Temples of
Ajanta, p. 17.) Mookerji adds that the second Ajanta boat is "the emperor's pleasure-boat ... with
painted eyes at stem and stern, a pillared canopy amidships, and an umbrella forward, the steersman
being accommodated on a sort of ladder ... while a rower is in the bows."
Commons files:
- https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ajanta_Cave_2_Ship_transporting_amphorae.jpg — photo
  reproduction from Pia Brancaccio, The Buddhist Caves at Aurangabad (Brill 2010); tagged PD-Art /
  Public Domain Mark; categorised "Cave 2, Ajanta". The best photographic reference.
- https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ajanta_Caves_boat_sketched_by_Mookerji.jpg — the 1912
  line drawing (PD-US); "raised bow and stern, three oblong sails on three masts, and steering oars
  (double quarter rudders)".
- https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ajanta_Cave_2_three-mast_sailship.jpg — PD-Art, but its
  source field says "detail of Ajanta Cave 17 frescoe" from Google Street View while the title says
  Cave 2: internally inconsistent; use only after checking against Brancaccio's photo.
- https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:INDIAN_SHIP_AJANTA_CAVE_2.jpg — NOT a photo: a 2024
  "own work" illustration by user HISTORYHUMANITY, CC BY-SA 4.0. The companion file §6.4 cites this
  as the model; replace it with the Brancaccio photo + Mookerji sketch. Also: the companion file's
  "sewn-plank, square sail" is not in Griffiths; what the painting shows is three oblong (lug) sails,
  a square jib on a bowsprit, quarter steering-oars, painted oculi, jars under an awning. Do not say
  "sewn-plank" from the painting (that comes from the 2025 Indian Navy reconstruction INSV
  Kaundinya, which is a modern interpretation).
Date caveat: Mookerji's "525–650" is Griffiths/Fergusson-era dating; Spink's chronology puts Cave 2's
painting c. 462–480 (see companion §5 item 7). Either way it is pre-Chalukya and Vakataka — caption
accordingly.

### 7b. Satavahana lead "ship coins" (2nd–3rd c. CE, Coromandel coast)
Mookerji 1912 p. 49–50 quoting V. A. Smith and Alexander Rea (ASI New Imperial Series XV p. 29):
"a remarkable find of some Andhra coins on the east coast, belonging to the 2nd and 3rd century a.d.,
on which is to be detected the device of a two-masted ship, "evidently of large size."" ... "The
obverse of the first shows a ship resembling the Indian dhoni, with bow to the right. The vessel is
pointed in vertical section at each end. On the point of the stem is a round ball. The rudder, in the
shape of a post with spoon on end, projects below. The deck is straight, and on it are two round
objects from which rise two masts, each with a cross-tree at the top." `inscription` (coin) / PD text.
Commons: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Andhra_Ship_Coins.jpg (from The Modern Review vol. 8,
1910; PD-India). Good for a two-masted coastal type older than Ajanta.

### 7c. Sanchi reliefs (1st c. BCE) — boats, not sea-ships
Mookerji ch. (pp. 36 ff.) describes canoes/barges on Stupa 1 gateways (Maisey, Sanchi and its Remains
pp. 42–43, 59); river craft only. Not useful for Puri's "hundreds of ships".

### 7d. Borobudur (Java, c. 800–825 CE) — NOT Indian, NOT pre-800
Mookerji pp. 46–47 reproduces six Borobudur ships ("No. 1 has got two masts and one long sail. No. 3
has got square sails and one stay-sail in front"; outriggers). Use only as a general Indian-Ocean
reference and caption it Javanese, 9th c.; it must not be shown as a Chalukya fleet.

### Bottom line for the artist
Pulakeshin's Puri fleet (Aihole v. 21): no Chalukya image exists. Draw from Ajanta Cave 2 (three
masts, oblong sails, square jib on bowsprit, high stem and stern, twin quarter steering-oars, painted
eyes on the bow, awning amidships with storage jars) for the big ships, and the Satavahana two-master
with cross-trees for smaller craft. No stern rudder, no lateen, no outriggers (Borobudur), no cannon.

## 8. Badami fort (ಬಾದಾಮಿ ಕೋಟೆ): what is Chalukya, what is later

### 8a. The Chalukya evidence (6th c.)
Badami cliff inscription of Chalikya Vallabhesvara (Pulakesin I), Saka 465 = 543 CE, ed. R. S.
Panchamukhi, EI XXVII no. 2 (1947–48), https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol27_1947-1948/panchamukhi.html
through panchamukhi6.html. Tier `inscription`.
- Findspot (panchamukhi.html): "in the northern fort at the back of the Battērappa temple", on the
  northern face of a cliff "about 250 feet high", the record about 120 feet up — i.e. the inscription
  is INSIDE the north fort's rock. The editor notes the rock "appears to have been cut through, east
  to west, forming a narrow path-way" and the eastern outlet is closed by "artificial brick walls
  rendering the fort impenetrable to the enemies" (he does not date the brick walls).
- Text (panchamukhi5.html), verses 1–2: "Svasti [||*] Śaka-varshēshu chatuś-śatēshu pañcha-shashṭi-
  yutēshu / Aśvamēdh-ādi-yajñānāṁ yajvā śrauta-vidhānataḥ [|*] / Hiraṇyagarbha-sambhūtaś=Chalikyō
  Vallabhēśvaraḥ [||1||*] / Dharādharēndra-Vātāpim=ajēyam=bhūtayē bhuvaḥ [|*] / adhastād=uparishṭāch=
  cha durggam=ētad=achīkarat [||2||*]"
- Translation (panchamukhi6.html): "In the Śaka years four hundred and sixty-five, ... performer of the
  Aśvamēdha and other sacrifices according to the śrauta rites ... Hiraṇyagarbha ... the Chalikya
  Vallabhēśvara ... made the best hill of Vātāpi (or Vātāpi in the best hill) ... into a fortress
  unconquerable from the top as well as from the bottom". Editor's summary (panchamukhi2.html): he
  "made the great hill of Vātāpi into an invincible citadel unapproachable from the top as well as from
  the bottom"; the word in line 5 is durgga. panchamukhi3.html: "the construction of the Fort in Śaka
  465 as recorded in the inscription under publication". The editor also notes the inscription makes no
  reference to cutting the pass through the hill.
So: the 543 CE record says the Chalukya made the HILL (dharādharēndra) a durga — i.e. the fortress
was the natural sandstone mass above the town, strengthened at top and bottom. No wall is described.

### 8b. What the later layers are (dated by inscription or record)
Bombay Gazetteer XXIII, Bijapur (1884), https://archive.org/details/cu31924070623743 (OCR), tier
`scholarship`/`inscription` where it quotes a record:
- p. 397: a Kannada inscription at Badami "dated 1339-40 (S.1261) records ... the building of a fort,
  presumably the northern part of Bādāmi fort, and the construction of its parapet wall by one of
  Harihar's nāiks or captains" (Vijayanagara, Harihara I).
- p. 557 (Cave III veranda pillar): "an undated inscription in three lines recording the building of a
  bastion on Bādami fort by the Vijaynagar king Kondrāj."
- p. 560: "The fortifications of Bādāmi consist of a lower and inner fort enclosing the town and on a
  level with the plain, commanded by two strong forts on the hill overhanging the town, one to the
  north called Bāvanbande-kote or Fifty-two Rocks fort and one to the south called Ranmandal or
  Battle-field fort. The two forts stand about 300 yards apart each about 240 feet above the plain.
  Both were dismantled about 1845." Then the 1842 inspection report: the north fort "built upon
  detached masses of steep rock, or rather on one huge rock, cut by narrow chasms into separate blocks.
  The chasms were 30 to 100 feet deep, carefully filled with strong and hard masonry wherever they
  opened through the rock ... Over these rocks at irregular intervals were bastions of various sizes
  joined by strong loopholed masonry walls ... all holding ordnance"; the approach "over a series of
  stone steps and through several narrow gates built in the masonry between the rocks on the
  south-west, the last gate being only four feet high by two feet broad"; interior "bare, uneven, and
  rocky and, except a few store rooms and a magazine, contained no buildings"; water from "four
  cisterns" and a conduit into "a reservoir built in one of the chasms".
- p. 563 history: "In 1778 Bādami was taken by Haidar Ali"; "In 1786 Bādāmi surrendered to the allied
  force of the Nizam and the Peshwa under Nāna Fadnavis, after a memorable siege of about four weeks";
  1818 General Munro took it (14 guns, 17 jingals found); the works were "deservedly esteemed one of
  the strongest hill forts in India".
- The ASI Dharwad Circle page (https://www.asidharwadcircle.in/monument/the-north-fort-and-temples/)
  repeats the Gazetteer: lower fort round the town + "Bavanbande-kote" (N) and "Ranamandala-kote" (S);
  "Both these forts were dismantled in about 1845"; north fort bastions "joined by strong loopholed
  masonry walls"; two Chalukya temples on it, Upper and Lower Sivalaya. It assigns NO date to any wall.
- Cousens, Chalukyan Architecture of the Kanarese Districts (ASI NIS XLII, 1926) p. 53,
  https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.529290: the cliffs "throw out fortified horns to the
  north and the south ... the ruined forts above. That on the north is known as the Bavan-bande-koti
  ... while that on the southern horn is the Ranamandala-koti ... The old town walls, with their
  ditches, join up the northern with the southern forts, bowing out westward"; "an old iron cannon
  which still lies upon the top of the western bastion of the southern fort. It is an old English
  piece dated 8-2-25". He also treats the Vatapi/"Pallava, the foremost of kings" rock on the north
  bank of the tank (cf. §3 above).
- Wikipedia "Badami Fort" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badami_Fort) adds Tipu's 1786 garrison and
  Adil Shahi control but with travel-site citations; no ASI source dates the loopholed walls. The
  Haidar Ali / Tipu attribution of the south-fort walls comes from travel blogs only — UNVERIFIED.

### 8c. Drawing rule for the 6th–7th-century images
- Keep: the two red sandstone horns (north hill with the Upper and Lower Sivalaya, 7th c.; the
  Malegitti Sivalaya "beneath the northern fort ... perched upon an overhanging crag", Cousens p. 53),
  the cave temples in the south cliff, the lake (Agastya tirtha — the Gazetteer's "tank"), narrow
  boulder gorges and rock-cut steps as the only ways up, a citadel that is mostly bare rock with a few
  store rooms and cisterns.
- Drop: loopholed curtain walls with gun bastions (1842 description; Vijayanagara parapet 1339–40;
  Kondaraja's bastion; Adil Shahi/Mysore/Maratha artillery works), the lower town wall with "bastioned
  stone and mud walls with loopholed parapets and a deep broad dry ditch" (1842), the iron cannon, the
  Tipu-era mosque. Masonry fills in the chasms are undated — if shown, show plain dry-stone or brick
  closures, not loopholed battlements. The Bhutanatha temple group on the lake is 7th–11th c.; the
  Yellamma temple is later — check before drawing.
- Tier for the drawing rule: `scholarship` (inference from dated records above); no excavation report
  on the walls was found.
