# Kalyani (Later Western) Chalukyas — ಕಲ್ಯಾಣಿ ಚಾಲುಕ್ಯರು (973–1189 CE)
# Research fact sheet (follows data/history-src/research/BRIEF.md)

Scope: Tailapa II to Someshvara IV, the Kalachuri interregnum of Bijjala, Basavanna's
Kalyana, the poets Ranna and Bilhana, Attimabbe, Akkadevi, Vijnaneshvara, Manasollasa.
Research date: 2026-10-10. Tiers: `inscription` (inscription / coin / contemporary text /
eyewitness), `scholarship` (historian's conclusion from primary evidence, named),
`legend` (tradition with no contemporary evidence; first recording named).
Conventions: "Fleet 1896" = J. F. Fleet, Dynasties of the Kanarese Districts, in Gazetteer
of the Bombay Presidency vol. I part II (1896), page numbers of that volume;
"Bhandarkar 1896" = R. G. Bhandarkar, Early History of the Dekkan, reprinted in the same
volume (pp. 138–229); "Bühler 1875" = Vikramankadevacharita ed. G. Bühler, Bombay
Sanskrit Series 14, Introduction page numbers; "EC VII" = Epigraphia Carnatica vol. VII
(Shimoga pt I, Rice 1902), Sk = Shikarpur taluk numbers; "EI V" = Epigraphia Indica V
(1898–99), Fleet, "Inscriptions at Ablur", no. 25; "SII III" = South Indian Inscriptions
vol. III pt 1 (Hultzsch 1899). Note: the "WebSearch" budget ran out mid-task; every claim
below was checked against the downloaded primary editions or the fetched page named.

Supporting chronology notes:
- The Kalachurya "interregnum" is counted 1156/1162–1182/84 (Fleet); Bhandarkar 1896 p. 228 puts the
  Chalukya recovery "about Saka 1104" (= 1182 CE). `scholarship`
- Hoysala Vira-Ballala II's Gadag inscription of 1192 claims he took the sovereignty from the
  Chalukya general Brahma (Bamma) "with cavalry only" (Fleet 1896 p. 464 n. 4, Ind. Ant. II p. 299). `inscription`
- The capital: Kalyana (modern Basavakalyana, Bidar dist.) is "nowhere mentioned in the records of
  the earlier Chalukya period, nor even in those of the Rashtrakuta period"; the earliest record
  calling it the capital is one of Someshvara I dated 1053 (Fleet 1896 pp. 427–428 and n.). Rice's
  note that Tailapa's capital "may very possibly have been Malkhed" (Manyakheta) is Fleet's own
  suggestion, p. 430. `scholarship`
- Minor royal residences (nelevidu / rajadhani) named in Vikramaditya VI's records: Nadaviyuppayana-
  vidu (early 1077), Etagiri = Yadgir (late 1077–78), Vijayapura = Bijapur (1091–92), Manneyakere
  (1125–26), Vikramapura = Arasibidi (Fleet 1896 p. 450). `inscription`

## 2. Primary sources: what survives, where to read them, licence

### 2a. Inscriptions (stone and copper), by edition

1. **EC VII, Shikarpur taluk (Balligave/Balagamve records)**, B. L. Rice 1902, public domain.
   https://archive.org/details/ec-07-1902 (full text: EC_07_1902_djvu.txt).
   Key records: Sk 136 (1068, Someshvara I's death and Someshvara II's coronation, Chola siege
   of Gutti); Sk 83 (1053, Vikramaditya as viceroy at Balligave); Sk 152 (1060, the Sila-Brahma
   stone, Tuluva Chandiga's self-impalement); Sk 170 (1063, Jayanti Buddhist vihara); Sk 118
   (Someshvara I's titles: "a lion to the elephant Chola… in the middle of the battle the Chola
   king grew faint and died"); Sk 124 (Vikramaditya VI's Permadi-basadi, founded "when he was a
   Prince"); Sk 123 (1159, Bijjala's governor Kesiraja); Sk 102 (1162, Bijjala at Balligave on his
   southern expedition, grants to Dakshina-Kedaresvara and "temples at Abbalur"); Sk 185 (April
   1168, Bijjala's transfer of the kingdom to Sovideva); Sk 197 (Kalachurya succession list).
   Rice's introduction pp. 19–28 summarises them. Licence: public domain (1902, author d. 1927).
2. **Fleet 1896**, Dynasties of the Kanarese Districts (Gazetteer of the Bombay Presidency I.ii).
   https://archive.org/details/india.history.resource.77877 — the standard record-by-record
   synthesis of every dated Chalukya and Kalachurya inscription known in 1896, with exact
   findspots. Public domain. Chapter IV (pp. 426–467) Western Chalukyas of Kalyani; Chapter V
   (pp. 468–489) Kalachuryas, including the Basava traditions and the Ablur record.
   Also bound in the same volume: Bhandarkar, Early History of the Dekkan (pp. 138–229): Section
   XII on Vikramaditya VI's patronage (Bilhana, Vijnaneshvara) and Section XIII on the Kalachuris
   and the "religious revolution at Kalyana".
3. **EI V (1898–99) no. 25, "Inscriptions at Ablur" (Fleet), pp. 213–265**: seven records A–G from
   the Somesvara temple, Ablur (Abbalur, Hirekerur taluk, Haveri dist.). Record E (undated, time of
   Kadamba Kamadeva c. 1181–1203) is the only epigraph narrating the Shaiva revival of Bijjala's
   day: the story of Ekantada-Ramayya. Text and English translation pp. 237–260.
   https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.56279 . Public domain. Revisited by P. B. Desai,
   EI XXIX (1951–52), "Ablur inscriptions re-examined" — summary at
   https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol29_1951-52/desai18.html (Desai
   notes Fleet misread some records and that later finds "established [Basava] as a historic
   figure"). Desai's article is not public domain (1951); use as reference only.
4. **SII III pt 1 (Hultzsch 1899)**: Chola side of the wars. No. 28 and No. 29 (Manimangalam,
   Rajendradeva) narrate Koppam; No. 55 (Tiruvallam, 3rd year) the Kolhapur pillar and Koppam;
   Virarajendra records (Hultzsch's introduction and
   https://www.whatisindia.com/inscriptions/south_indian_inscriptions/volume_3/virarajendra_i.html)
   narrate Kudalasangamam, the burning of Kampili, the Tungabhadra pillar, and the marriage of his
   daughter to Vikramaditya VI. https://archive.org/details/india.history.resource.107642 .
   Public domain.
5. **SII XI pt 1 (Bombay-Karnatak inscriptions)**, Panchamukhi 1940 — not public domain in full,
   but the summaries at whatisindia.com are usable finding aids:
   Someshvara I's records (nos. 78–106):
   https://www.whatisindia.com/inscriptions/south_indian_inscriptions/volume_11/trailokyamalla_somesvara_1.html
   Vikramaditya VI (nos. 119–139):
   https://www.whatisindia.com/inscriptions/south_indian_inscriptions/volume_11/tribhuvanamalla_vikramaditya.html
   Attimabbe's Lakkundi inscription is SII XI no. 52 (Saka 929 = 1007 CE) and no. 53 (damaged
   duplicate) — summarised at https://puratattva.in/lakkundi-inscriptions/ .
6. **SII XVIII (Bombay-Karnatak III) — Kalachuryas**:
   https://www.whatisindia.com/inscriptions/south_indian_inscriptions/volume_18/the_kalachuryas.html
   (Bijjala counted regnal years from 1156–57; a 1136 hero-stone from Tambur shows him as
   Someshvara III's subordinate; Sovideva's record no. 186 says Bijjala, after abdicating, was
   "engaged in putting down the insurgents").
7. **SII XX (Chalukyas of Kalyana)**:
   https://www.whatisindia.com/inscriptions/south_indian_inscriptions/volume_20/the_chalukyas_of_kalyana_1.html
   (Lakshmeshwar no. 52 of 1077 carries both "Chalukya-Vikrama year 2" and "Saka 1000", proving the
   Saka era was not literally abolished.)
8. **Bühler 1875**, Vikramankadevacharita, Bombay Sanskrit Series 14 — Sanskrit text of the 18
   cantos plus a 46-page English Introduction summarising every canto with historical commentary.
   https://archive.org/details/vikramnkadevacha00bilh . Public domain. Also
   https://archive.org/details/india.history.resource.78270 and
   https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.163467 (duplicate scans).
9. **Gadag inscription of 1098** (Chalukya-Vikrama era verses, Bachaladevi as mother of the three
   brothers) — Fleet 1896 p. 447 nn. 2–3 gives the text; original in Carnatic-Desa Inscriptions I
   p. 410 (Elliot MSS). Public domain via Fleet.
10. **Kauthem plates of Vikramaditya V (1009)** — Ind. Ant. XVI p. 15 (Fleet), genealogy of the
    dynasty back to Taila II, Jakavve's parentage, Munja ("Utpala") imprisoned. Public domain.
11. **Miraj plates of Jayasimha II (1024)** — Ind. Ant. VIII p. 10; genealogy from Vijayaditya to
    Tailapa (Bhandarkar 1896 p. 229 prints the table). Public domain.
12. **Hottur inscription (1007–08)** — Satyashraya vs the Chola: Fleet 1896 p. 433 (from an
    ink-impression): the Chola "having collected a force numbering nine hundred thousand, had
    pillaged the whole country, had slaughtered the women, the children, and the Brahmans, and,
    taking the girls to wife, had destroyed their caste"; Satyashraya "put the Chola to flight".
13. **Annigeri inscription 1071** (Jain temple) — the "wicked" Chola burned the Jain temples of
    Belvola, but "eventually yielded his head to Somesvara I in battle, and thus, losing his life,
    broke the succession of his family" (Fleet 1896 p. 441). The Chalukya version of Rajadhiraja's
    death. Carn.-Desa Inscrs I p. 216.
14. **Sudi inscription of 20 Jan 1060** — Someshvara I camped at Puliyappayanavidu "on his return
    from a conquest of the southern countries and of the Chola" (Fleet 1896 p. 441).
15. **Arasibidi inscriptions (1047–48, 1053)** and **Sudi (1050)**, **Hottur (1066)** — Akkadevi's
    governorship and the siege of Gokage (Fleet 1896 p. 435 and nn.).
16. **Managoli inscription 1161** (Bijjala's 6th year) — mentions a Basava / Basavarasayya of the
    Kasyapa gotra, "in whom we may find the original of the Basava of the Lingayat Puranas"
    (Fleet, EI V p. 244); listed in EI VII appendix no. 278 (whatisindia appendix50). `inscription`
17. **Arjunavada (Arjunwad) inscription, 1260 CE**, of the Seuna king Kannara — said to name
    Basavaraja and Sangana Basava; cited only in an uncited Wikipedia image caption
    (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basava). Editio princeps NOT verified in this pass; treat as
    "reported, check EI/Ind. Ant. before use". `unknown`

### 2b. Contemporary literary sources

- **Bilhana (ಬಿಲ್ಹಣ), Vikramankadevacharita** (Sanskrit, 18 sargas), written at Kalyana before 1088
  (Bühler 1875 pp. 20–22: the poem ends with a Chola war and "knows nothing of the great expedition
  into Central India beyond the Narmada which Vikrama undertook in 1088"; it speaks of Harsha of
  Kashmir as a prince, and Harsha came to the throne in 1088/89). Sole manuscript: palm-leaf, 158
  leaves, in the Jain Brihajjnanakosha under the Parsvanatha temple in Jaisalmer fort, copied by
  Bühler and H. Jacobi "in about seven days" (Bühler pp. 44–46). Canto XVIII is Bilhana's
  autobiography. Licence: Sanskrit text public domain; Bühler's 1875 introduction public domain.
  Modern English translations exist (not PD).
- **Kalhana, Rajatarangini VII.936–938** on Bilhana: "whom Parmadi, lord of Karnata, made his chief
  Pandit, whose parasol, when he travelled on elephants through Karnata-land, was seen borne aloft
  before the king" (Bühler's translation, p. 21). `inscription` (contemporary chronicle, 1148–50).
- **Ranna (ರನ್ನ), Gadayuddha / Sahasabhimavijaya (ಗದಾಯುದ್ಧ / ಸಾಹಸಭೀಮವಿಜಯ)** — Kannada champu;
  completion date 27 Oct 982 per Datta 1988 p. 1335 (via Wikipedia "Ranna"); Govinda Pai proposed
  a 982 first version later revised (N. T. Bhat 1993 p. 104 via Wikipedia "Tailapa II"). Patron
  disputed: Tailapa II (Ahavamalla) or Satyashraya — see §5. **Ajitapurana / Ajita-tirthankara-
  purana-tilaka** (993 CE, Sastri 2002 p. 356) commissioned by Attimabbe. Kannada text: public
  domain; no Kannada Wikisource transcription found (search 2026-10-10 returned only Mysore
  encyclopaedia entries, e.g. https://kn.wikisource.org/wiki/ಮೈಸೂರು_ವಿಶ್ವವಿದ್ಯಾನಿಲಯ_ವಿಶ್ವಕೋಶ/ರನ್ನ).
- **Vijnaneshvara (ವಿಜ್ಞಾನೇಶ್ವರ), Mitakshara (ಮಿತಾಕ್ಷರ)** — Sanskrit commentary (vivriti) on the
  Yajnavalkya-smriti, written at Kalyana in Vikramaditya VI's reign; closing stanzas quoted in §4.
  Colebrooke's 1810 translation of the inheritance chapter:
  https://archive.org/details/DayaBhagaAndMitaksharaTwoTreatisesOnTheHinduLawOfInheritanceH.T.Colebrooke
  (public domain).
- **Someshvara III, Manasollasa / Abhilashitartha-chintamani (ಮಾನಸೋಲ್ಲಾಸ)** — Sanskrit
  encyclopaedia in five vimshatis (100 chapters), "written in the fourth year of his reign, the
  Saumya samvatsara, Saka-Samvat 1051 (expired)" = 1129–30 (Fleet 1896 p. 456, citing Burnell's
  Tanjore catalogue p. 141 and Bhandarkar 1884 p. 167). Edition: G. K. Shrigondekar, Gaekwad's
  Oriental Series 28 (1925), 84 (1939), 138 (1961). Vol. 1 scan:
  https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.313752 ; vol. 3 text scan:
  https://archive.org/details/TxtSktMAnasOllAsaSomesvaraVol319610024b . Licence: Sanskrit text PD;
  Shrigondekar's apparatus (1925) is public domain in India by term but check for your use; 1939/1961
  volumes are not.
- **Vachanas (ವಚನ)** of Basavanna (ankita ಕೂಡಲಸಂಗಮದೇವಾ), Allama Prabhu (ಗುಹೇಶ್ವರಾ), Akka
  Mahadevi (ಚೆನ್ನಮಲ್ಲಿಕಾರ್ಜುನಾ) — 12th-century Kannada prose-poems, the nearest thing to first-person
  sources for Kalyana. The texts are public domain; Kannada Wikisource holds author indexes with
  one page per vachana (CC BY-SA on the wiki layer): https://kn.wikisource.org/wiki/ಬಸವಣ್ಣ (index
  past 684 entries), https://kn.wikisource.org/wiki/ಅಕ್ಕಮಹಾದೇವಿ , https://kn.wikisource.org/wiki/ಅಲ್ಲಮಪ್ರಭು ,
  portal https://kn.wikisource.org/wiki/ವಚನ_ಸಾಹಿತ್ಯ . Caution: vachanas are devotional, undated,
  transmitted in later anthologies (from c. 1400, e.g. Shunyasampadane); they are evidence of ideas,
  not of events.
- **Harihara (ಹರಿಹರ), Basavarajadevara Ragale** — earliest Basava biography (13 of 25 sections
  survive; Shiva Prakash 1997 p. 179 via Wikipedia "Basava"). Harihara's date: 13th century; his
  Girijakalyana is quoted in the Suktisudharnava of 1245 (Ben-Herut 2018 p. 43 via Wikipedia
  "Harihara (poet)"). Tier: `legend` for miracles, `scholarship` where Desai (EI XXIX) uses it as the
  account "written close to the events".
- **Later hagiography** (all `legend`): Palkuriki Somanatha's Telugu Basava Purana (13th c.); Bhima
  Kavi's Kannada Basava Purana (1369); Channabasava Purana (1585 — Fleet 1896 p. 480 n. 5); the Jain
  Bijjalarayacharitra (Wilson, Mackenzie Collection pp. 309–320); Merutunga's Prabandhachintamani
  (1304) for Munja. Wurth's abstract translations of the two Puranas: JBBRAS VIII pp. 65–221 (PD).

### 2c. Coins
- Western Chalukya gold "pagodas" (gadyana, c. 96 grains) are punch-marked with Kannada or Nagari
  legends; mints at Lakkundi and Sudi; sample legends "Sri Jaya" (Jayasimha II), "Sri Tre lo ka malla"
  (Someshvara I), "Sri Jagade" (Jagadekamalla II); punches of a stylised lion, spearhead, lotus
  (Wikipedia "Western Chalukya Empire", coin section, notes 66–69; the underlying catalogue was not

- Lakkundi inscriptions of 1184 and 1186 (SII XV nos. 61, 70) record gifts "out of the income from
  minting transactions" — direct epigraphic proof of the Lakkundi mint under Someshvara IV
  (https://puratattva.in/lakkundi-inscriptions/). `inscription`
- A Kalyana coin "possibly" of Someshvara IV is illustrated on Wikipedia "Someshvara IV" (CNG Coins). 
  No Wikimedia Commons file for a Kalyani Chalukya coin was found by search (2026-10-10).

### 2d. Travellers
- None for this dynasty. Bilhana is the only outsider's eyewitness (a Kashmiri at Kalyana c. 1076–

## 3. Candidate stories

### Story A — "The Ganga of the South": Ahavamalla's last walk (ಆಹವಮಲ್ಲನ ಕೊನೆಯ ನಡಿಗೆ)
People: Someshvara I Ahavamalla (ಆಹವಮಲ್ಲ ಸೋಮೇಶ್ವರ); his sons Someshvara II (ಭುವನೈಕಮಲ್ಲ),
Vikramaditya (ವಿಕ್ರಮಾದಿತ್ಯ), Jayasimha (ಜಯಸಿಂಹ); queens Mailaladevi, Ketaladevi, Hoysaladevi;
the Chola Rajadhiraja and Rajendradeva; the ministers who "consented".
Arc (beats):
1. 1044–50: the new king moves the court to Kalyana (first attested as capital 1053) and is
   raided by the Cholas — Rajadhiraja burns the Jain temples of Belvola (Annigeri 1071). [inscription]
2. c. 1054, Koppam "on the bank of the great river": Ahavamalla, hearing the Chola has entered
   Rattapadi, "exclaimed: 'This is a disgrace to me!', sprang up, his eyes burning, went into
   Koppam… and commenced to attack"; his arrows pierce the Chola elephant's forehead and the Chola
   king's thigh and shoulders; Rajadhiraja dies; Rajendradeva brings up fresh regiments; the
   Chalukya's younger brother Jayasimha, "the warlike Pulikesi", Dasapanman, Nanni-Nolamba and
   "princes without number" fall; "the victorious banner of the boar", the queens Sattiyavvai and
   Sangappai and the war elephants Satrubhayamkara, Karabhadra, Mulabhadra are captured; Ahavamalla
   flees "with dishevelled hair" (SII III no. 29, ll. 6–11). [inscription, Chola side]
3. The Chalukya counter-claim: the Chola "yielded his head to Somesvara I in battle" (Annigeri 1071)
   and Someshvara returns Jan 1060 "from a conquest of the southern countries and of the Chola"
   (Sudi); Sk 118: "in the middle of the battle the Chola king grew faint and died". [inscription]
4. c. 1064–68: Virarajendra claims to have "seen the back of Ahavamalla three times", to have burnt
   Kampili and set a pillar of victory on the Tungabhadra, and to have taken Ahavamalla's queen,
   treasures and vehicles at Kudalasangamam; the Chola says Ahavamalla failed to appear at an
   "appointed battle-field" (SII III, Virarajendra introduction). [inscription, Chola side]
5. Spring 1068: Vikramaditya is away in the south; the king is "supremely happy on learning his
   son's success against the Chola, Pandya and Simhala. In the midst of his rejoicings he had been
   attacked by a malignant fever. Finding that all remedies were of no avail, he had resolved to
   finish his life in the Tungabhadra, the Ganga of the south. With the consent of his ministers he
   had travelled to the sacred stream and had died in its waves, meditating on Siva" (Bühler's
   summary of Vikramankadevacharita IV.44–68; verse 58 is his "confession of faith in Siva").
   [contemporary literary]
6. Sunday 29 March 1068 (Saka 990, Kilaka, Chaitra bahula 8): "thereby increasing his fame, he
   performed in Kuruvatti the rites of supreme yoga (parama-yoga), and in the Tungabhadra the master
   of the world king Ahavamalla ascended to heaven" (EC VII Sk 136, Balligave, dated the same year).
   The same record lists the kings "who did not remain insolent, but gave tribute", and says that his
   arm and mind "not being exhausted, he fought against Indra, defeated him… and returned alone, a
   universal emperor" — i.e. the inscription frames the death as a final conquest. [inscription]
7. The messenger finds Vikramaditya on the Krishna performing propitiatory rites against evil omens;
   he "at first refused to be consoled and had to be disarmed lest he should attempt his own life"
   (Bühler p. 32). [contemporary literary, partisan]
8. Friday 11 April 1068, thirteen days later, Someshvara II takes "the jewels, umbrella, and
   throne"; the Chola, "saying 'A new reign; a kingdom fit for a hero; this is the time to invade it;
   I will surround Gutti and besiege it'", lays siege; the Chalukya cavalry drives him off (Sk 136).
   [inscription]
Goosebump moment: the king, his body burning with fever, wading into the Tungabhadra at Kuruvatti
until "the water reached his neck, and amid the din of the waves and of all manner of musical
instruments deliberately drowned himself" (Rice's paraphrase of Bilhana, EC VII intro p. 20;
Bilhana IV.44–68) — and the state inscription recording it as the day he "fought against Indra".
Evidence: Sk 136 gives the exact day and place; Bilhana (writing within twenty years at the son's
court) gives the fever and the river rite; the two agree. The Chola records give the humiliations
that precede it. This is the best-attested royal death in Kannada history before Vijayanagara.
Dramatised vs recorded: recorded — date, place, river, fever, ministers' consent, Vikramaditya's
absence, the Chola siege a fortnight later, the lost boar banner and named queens at Koppam.
Dramatised — any dialogue; what the king felt; whether the death was penance for Koppam (not stated
anywhere; Sk 136 calls it fame-increasing, Bilhana calls it illness). Do not make him a Jain convert
(Lassen's conjecture, rejected by Bühler p. 32 n. 4).
Why it grips adults: a sixty-year-old emperor who has been beaten three times by the Cholas and has
lost a brother, his queens and his banner, chooses the hour and manner of his own death in a sacred
river while his brilliant second son is winning the war for him — and his court inscribes it as his
greatest victory.

### Story B — "Rubbing out the Saka": Vikramaditya VI and the brother he deposed
People: Vikramaditya VI (ಆರನೇ ವಿಕ್ರಮಾದಿತ್ಯ); Someshvara II (ಇಮ್ಮಡಿ ಸೋಮೇಶ್ವರ); Jayasimha III;
Virarajendra Chola and his daughter; Rajiga = Kulottunga I; Chandaladevi (ಚಂದಲದೇವಿ) of Karahata;
Bilhana (ಬಿಲ್ಹಣ); Seunachandra II; Vijnaneshvara (ವಿಜ್ಞಾನೇಶ್ವರ).
Arc:
1. The father wants to make Vikramaditya yuvaraja over the elder brother; Vikramaditya declines
   "on the grounds that it did not belong to him by right" (Bilhana III.26–59; Bühler flags this
   whole passage as "touched up in order to whitewash V.'s character", p. 31 n. 1). [literary/partisan]
2. 1068–c.1075: the brothers "lived for sometime after in concord"; then Someshvara II "fell into
   evil courses… became suspicious, cruel, and avaricious"; Vikramaditya leaves Kalyana with his
   followers and the youngest brother Jayasimha; progresses through Banavasi, receives Jayakesin I
   of Goa and the Alupa; the Chola Virarajendra, instead of fighting, offers his daughter and the
   two meet on the Tungabhadra (Bühler pp. 33–35; Fleet 1896 pp. 444–445). [literary; the marriage
   is confirmed by Chola records: Virarajendra's daughter "given in marriage to… Vikramaditya VI"
   and the Chola tying the heir-apparent's necklace on the younger brother — SII III Virarajendra
   intro; Hultzsch Report 1892]. [inscription]
3. c. 1070–72: Virarajendra dies; Vikramaditya marches to Kanchi and Gangaikondacholapuram and
   installs his brother-in-law (Adhirajendra); "a few days" after his return the brother-in-law is
   killed in a fresh rebellion and "Rajiga, the lord of Vengi" (Kulottunga I) seizes the Chola throne
   (Bühler pp. 35–36). [literary; chronology disputed, see §5]
4. Rajiga induces Someshvara II into an alliance; battle; Rajiga flees, Someshvara II is captured;
   Vikramaditya "at first intended to restore his brother to liberty and to the throne. But
   eventually he decided otherwise" and is proclaimed king (Bühler pp. 36–38; Fleet p. 445). The
   Kalige inscription says only that Someshvara "became intoxicated with pride, and neglected the
   sufferings of his subjects, whereupon Vikramaditya, being virtuously minded, punished or confined
   him"; the Gadag 1098 record: "by the strength of his own arm, Vikramaditya seized in battle the
   sovereignty of Somesvara while it was still of no long duration" (Fleet p. 444). Hemadri's
   Vratakhanda credits the Yadava Seunachandra II with saving Vikramaditya from "a coalition of his
   enemies" (Fleet p. 445). "What ultimately became of Somesvara II, is not known." [inscription +
   literary]
5. 1076: the new reckoning. Gadag 1098: "Having rubbed out the brilliant Saka-varsha, he, the
   impetuous one… published his own name throughout the world under the form of the Vikrama-varsha";
   Yedarave 1094–95: "Having said 'Why should the glory of the kings Vikramaditya and Nanda be a
   hindrance any longer?', he, with a loudly uttered command, abolished that (era) which has the name
   of Saka, and made that (era) which has the Chalukya counting" (Fleet 1896 p. 447). [inscription]
6. Spring at Kalyana: the Silahara princess Chandralekha/Chandaladevi of Karahata (Karad) holds a
   svayamvara; she rejects the lords of Ayodhya, Chedi, Kanyakubja, Kalinjara, Malava, Gurjara,
   Pandya and Chola "one by one… signifying her displeasure by various contemptuous gestures" and
   garlands the Chalukya (Bilhana VII–IX, Bühler pp. 38–39). Inscriptions confirm her as piriyarasi,
   mother of Jayakarna (1102–03) (Fleet p. 449). [literary set-piece; marriage is inscription-tier]
7. c. 1080–82: the informer's report that Jayasimha, viceroy of Banavasi, "amassed treasures by
   oppressing his subjects… tried to seduce Vikramaditya's soldiers"; spies confirm; Vikramaditya
   writes "friendly exhortations"; Jayasimha burns villages on the Krishna and "sent insulting
   messages"; battle, elephants give Jayasimha early advantage, Vikramaditya's "personal bravery
   restored the fortune of the day"; Jayasimha captured in the jungles and "spoke kindly to" (Bühler
   pp. 42–43). Epigraphy: Jayasimha is yuvaraja at Banavasi 1077–1081/82 and then vanishes from the
   records (Fleet p. 449). "Vikrama's war with Jayasimha is not mentioned in the inscriptions… the
   reason is, no doubt, that Vikrama did not care to proclaim the fact, that he had fought with both
   his brothers" (Bühler p. 43 n. 1). [literary + inscription]
8. Fifty years: the Vishnu-Kamalavilasin temple, the tank and the city Vikramapura (Arasibidi);
   Bilhana made Vidyapati with a parasol borne before the king (Rajatarangini); Vijnaneshvara's
   Mitakshara declares "never was a monarch like the prosperous Vikramarka seen or heard of"; 1088–89
   crossing the Narmada; Buddhist vihara grants at Dambal 1095; the Hoysala Vishnuvardhana's revolt
   c. 1117 and the night attack at Kannegala; last date 3 Jan 1126 (Fleet pp. 447–453). [inscription]
Goosebump moment: the two inscriptions on the era — a king literally ordering the Saka count erased
and his own name put in its place, year 1 = 1076; and Bühler's blunt verdict (p. 37 n.) "I am
inclined to give the verdict against Vikrama and Bilhana and to assume that the former designedly
used his superior talents to oust his weaker brother" — against the court poem that says the
opposite.
Evidence base: excellent on dates and marriages (150+ inscriptions); the fratricide and the
svayamvara rest on one partisan Sanskrit poem written for the winner; the Chola side is independent
and confirms the Chola marriage and Vikramaditya's elevation over his brother.
Dramatised vs recorded: recorded — the deposition, the alliance of Someshvara II with Kulottunga,
the era, the queens (six named), Jayasimha's disappearance, Bilhana's office. Dramatised — motives,
Someshvara II's "cruelty", the svayamvara as staged (Bühler p. 40 n. treats it as a literary device
modelled on the Raghuvamsa), what happened to the captured brother.
Why it grips adults: a usurpation told by the usurper's paid poet, checkable line by line against
stone — the reader watches propaganda being made.

### Story C — "Seven days": Ekantada Ramayya's head, and the fall of Kalyana (ಅಬ್ಬಲೂರಿನ ಪಣ)
People: Ekantada Ramayya (ಏಕಾಂತದ ರಾಮಯ್ಯ) of Alande; the Jain headman Sankagaunda of Ablur;
Bijjala (ಬಿಜ್ಜಳ); Someshvara IV and his general Brahma (Bamma, ಬಮ್ಮರಸ); Basavanna (ಬಸವಣ್ಣ) as the
figure the inscriptions do not name; the blinded Haralayya and Madhuvayya of the Puranas.
Arc:
1. 1156–62: Bijjala, Mahamandalesvara of Tardavadi, counts his own years from 1156–57, takes
   Maharajadhiraja by Dec 1157 and the full imperial titles in 1162; Taila III "is the last in the
   lineal succession" in his records (Fleet 1896 pp. 473–475). [inscription]
2. Ablur, shortly before 1162: Ramayya, a Shaiva Brahman "with speech, body, and mind entirely given
   up to Siva", sits at the Brahmesvara site; the Jains led by Sankagaunda sing Jina's praise beside
   his Siva; the dispute: "Who is it that creates the earth?… does your Arhat bestow gifts as Hara
   does?"; the Jains: "cut off thine own head, under such conditions that all people may know of it,
   and offer it to him, and get it back from him"; the wager written on a palmyra leaf — the Jains
   stake their Jina (EI V no. 25 E, ll. 30–40, Fleet's translation pp. 255–256). [inscription]
3. "The brave Rama, with a loud shout, and with an unfaltering hand, set himself to cut off his
   head"; the onlookers mutter that he has blunted the sword; he "cut through his own neck with as
   little difficulty as if he were shearing through a bundle of grass"; "after the severed head had
   been exhibited in public during seven days, Hara kindly gave it back; the head became sound again
   without any scar" (ll. 40–44). [inscription — the miracle is the inscription's own claim]
4. The Jains renege; Ramayya "fell on it like a thunderbolt from a clear sky, and broke the head of
   the Jina", scatters guards, horses and chieftains "like a wild elephant in rut… in a grove of
   plantain-trees", and sets up Vira-Somanatha (ll. 44–46). [inscription]
5. The Jains complain to Bijjala, who "became mad with anger"; Ramayya produces the palmyra leaf,
   asks the king to keep it in his treasury, and offers to repeat the feat — "even after they shall
   have burned it" — if the Jains stake "the various Jinas of their eight hundred shrines, the chief
   of which is the Anesejjeyabasadi"; the Jains refuse ("we have certainly not come to lay a wager
   and lose any more of our Jinas"); Bijjala laughs, dismisses them "bidding them… to live in peace",
   gives Ramayya a jayapatra, washes his feet and grants Gogave village (ll. 46–60). Bijjala is still
   styled Mahamandalesvara here, so Fleet dates the episode "shortly before A.D. 1162" (EI V p. 244).
   [inscription]
6. The Puranas' Kalyana (all `legend`): Basava, Bijjala's treasurer-minister, feeds the Jangamas
   from the treasury; Bijjala blinds (Basava Purana) or drags to death with ropes (Channabasava
   Purana) the devotees Halleyaga and Madhuveyya; Basava leaves for Kudala-Sangama; Jagaddeva and
   two companions stab Bijjala "even in the midst of his court"; "men fought with men, horses with
   horses, and elephants with elephants; the race of Vijjana was extinct, Kalyana was a heap of
   ruins" (Bhandarkar 1896 pp. 226–227, from Wurth's translation); the Jain Bijjalarayacharitra
   instead has a poisoned fruit sent by Basava, Basava's suicide in a well at Ulavi, and Nilamba's
   poison (Bhandarkar p. 227; Fleet p. 481).
7. What stone says instead: Bijjala abdicated alive after 19 July 1167; a Balagamve record of April
   1168 says he "transferred the burden of government to his dear or favourite son Sovideva"
   (Fleet p. 477); a Sovideva record says the ex-king was "engaged in putting down the insurgents"
   (SII XVIII no. 186). No inscription of Bijjala's time names Basava as minister (Fleet 1896 p. 481:
   "No epigraphic mention of Basava and Channabasava has been obtained"); the 1161 Managoli
   Basavarasayya is the only candidate (EI V p. 244). The Channabasava Purana dates the murder to
   Saka 707 = 785 CE, "too early by close upon four centuries" (Fleet p. 481). [inscription +
   scholarship]
8. 1182–84: the Kalachurya general Brahma, "in contempt of his father" Kavana (himself a Kalachurya
   dandanayaka), turns the army and restores Someshvara IV — "a fire of death to the Kalachuryas"
   (Annigere 1184–85; Gadag 1192; Fleet pp. 464–465); Someshvara IV hears Ramayya's story at an
   assembly at Seleyahalliyakoppa, summons him to the palace, washes his feet and grants Ablur
   itself (EI V no. 25 E). [inscription]
Goosebump moment: the seven days with the head on display — told not by a devotee's purana but cut
into the temple wall within one generation, with a sculptured panel (record F) marking "the exact
spot" (EI V p. 243 n. 4; Fleet 1896 p. 483 n. 1: the sculptures show a fight on the right and "a
linga, with a standing priest and a kneeling figure" offering the Jina's head).
Evidence: the strongest single document of the Shaiva upheaval; Desai (EI XXIX) accepts Ramayya as
historical and Bijjala as "a staunch follower of Saivism", not a Jain. The Basava material is all
later. The writer must keep the two layers visibly separate.
Dramatised vs recorded: recorded — the wager, the palm leaf, Bijjala's laugh and grant, the 800
shrines and the Anesejjeya basadi of Lakshmeshwar, Someshvara IV's grant, the abdication of 1167.
Dramatised — the murder of Bijjala (legend, two incompatible versions), Basava's office, the Anubhava
Mantapa (no contemporary evidence; Wikipedia's article carries no footnote for its existence and a
2016 Hindu report that the site "eludes researchers"), Akka Mahadevi's arrival, the Haralayya–
Madhuvarasa wedding (not in Bhandarkar/Fleet's summaries of the Puranas; first recorded in later
Kannada tradition — source not pinned in this pass).
Why it grips adults: a religious revolution where the only contemporary voice is a rival saint's
temple wall, and the beloved national story (Basava, the murdered king, the burning city) turns out
to be told first four centuries later — the story can be about memory itself.

### Story D — "Sahasabhima": Ranna, Attimabbe and the court of Satyashraya (ಗದಾಯುದ್ಧದ ಕವಿ)
People: Ranna (ರನ್ನ); Tailapa II (ಇಮ್ಮಡಿ ತೈಲಪ); Irivabedanga Satyashraya (ಇರಿವಬೆಡಂಗ ಸತ್ಯಾಶ್ರಯ);
Attimabbe (ಅತ್ತಿಮಬ್ಬೆ) and her husband Nagadeva; Chavundaraya of the Gangas; the Paramara Munja;
the Chola prince Rajendra.
Arc:
1. 973: Taila II, a Rashtrakuta feudatory, overthrows Karka II and "acquired the whole of the
   Rashtrakuta kingdom, with the exception of the Gujarat provinces"; the realm is "Rattapadi", the
   seven-and-a-half-lakh country (Fleet pp. 430–431). [inscription]
2. The Munja war: Munja "conquered Taila II no less than sixteen times, before he met his fate";
   against his minister Rudraditya's advice he crosses the Godavari, is "defeated and taken captive…
   made a futile attempt to escape… and finally was executed" (Fleet p. 432, from Merutunga). The
   Sangamner plates of 1000 say the Yadava Bhillama II killed Munja; the Kauthem plates of 1009 say
   Taila imprisoned Utpala (Munja); Gadag 1098 says Taila killed him. [inscription for death in
   Taila's hands; `legend` for Mrinalavati and the begging bowl, first in Merutunga 1304]
3. 982 (or revised later): Ranna, a Jain from the bangle-seller caste by tradition (not verified
   in this pass; Wikipedia "Ranna" does not support it — mark unknown), composes Gadayuddha, the
   mace duel of Bhima and Duryodhana, identifying Bhima with his patron and giving Duryodhana a
   tragic nobility (Datta 1988 pp. 1335–36 via Wikipedia). Patron: Ahavamalla = Taila II, or
   Satyashraya "Sahasabhima" (Bhatia 1970 p. 370) — disputed. [contemporary literary]
4. 993: Ranna writes Ajitapurana for Attimabbe, daughter of Mallapa, wife of Nagadeva (son of
   Dhallapa), praising her as Danachintamani; she is said to have commissioned 1000 palm-leaf copies
   of Ponna's Shantipurana and 1500 Jain shrines (Jain Journal via Wikipedia "Attimabbe" — numbers
   are panegyric). [contemporary literary]
5. 1007 (Saka 929): the Lakkundi slab (SII XI no. 52) records Attiyabbe's gift of land to the Jain
   temple she built at Lokkigundi, to her preceptor Archanandi-pandita, and "certain interesting
   miracles connected with the life of Attiyabbe"; the temple survives as the Brahma Jinalaya
   (puratattva.in/lakkundi-inscriptions). [inscription]
6. 1007–08: the Chola prince Rajendra's 900,000 (Hottur) ravage Rattapadi; "had slaughtered the
   women, the children, and the Brahmans, and, taking the girls to wife, had destroyed their caste";
   Satyashraya "put the Chola to flight" and is reigning "at Tavareyaghatta" (Fleet p. 433); the
   Chola records claim the reverse (SII I pp. 51–52, 95–96). [inscription, both sides]
7. 1008: Satyashraya's last records; Vikramaditya V, Ayyana, Jayasimha II follow — Jayasimha's queen
   Suggaladevi and the Channabasava Purana's "Desinga" conversion story (Fleet p. 437 n. 5). [legend]
Goosebump moment: Ranna making the enemy, Duryodhana, the hero of the last night — while his
patron's kingdom is being burnt by the Chola; and Attimabbe, a widow (Wikipedia WCE note 98:
queen/lady Attimabbe "long outlived" her husband), raising a stone basadi at Lakkundi in 1007 while
the war is on.
Evidence: good for Attimabbe (inscription + contemporary poem), fair for Ranna (texts survive, dates
argued), poor for Munja's romance (14th-century Jain prabandha).
Dramatised vs recorded: recorded — the basadi, the grant, the Hottur atrocities, Taila's four
inscriptions, Munja's death in Taila's hands. Dramatised — Ranna's caste origin, his meetings with
Attimabbe, Mrinalavati, the "sixteen defeats".
Why it grips adults: Kannada's first great war poem is about the loser's dignity, written in a
decade of invasion, paid for by a woman.

### Story E (shorter) — Akkadevi, "a very Bhairavi in battle" (ಅಕ್ಕಾದೇವಿ)
People: Akkadevi (ಅಕ್ಕಾದೇವಿ), elder sister of Jayasimha II, aunt of Someshvara I; her Kadamba
husband (name untraced); son Toyimadeva. Beats: (1) 1021/22 governing Kisukad-70 under her brother;
(2) called gunada-bedangi "a marvel of virtuous qualities", ekavakye "she whose speech is single and
uniform", and "a very Bhairavi in battle and in destroying hostile kings" (Fleet p. 435); (3) 1047–48
(Arasibidi): laying siege to the fort of Gokage (Gokak), "probably to quell some local insurrection";
(4) 1050 (Sudi): governing Kisukad-70, Toragare-6, Masavadi-140; (5) 1053: seat at Vikramapura
(Arasibidi); (6) 1066 (Hottur): mother of the Kadamba Mahamandalesvara Toyimadeva, ruling Banavasi
12,000 and Panungal 500; SII XI no. 86 calls her "evidently the paternal aunt of king Somesvara".
Goosebump: a princess on campaign against a fort in her fifties, named as general in a grant.
Evidence: all `inscription`; no narrative source. Dramatise sparingly: no battle description exists.
Anachronism warning: Wikipedia's "1010–1064" dates and "Bidar–Bagalkot–Bijapur" province are not
from the inscriptions; Kisukad-70 is the Pattadakal–Sudi country (Fleet p. 431).

## 4. Little-known facts (one line + source + tier)

1. Someshvara I's death is dated to the day — Sunday 29 March 1068 — and his son's coronation to
   Friday 11 April 1068, thirteen days later (EC VII Sk 136, pp. 101–102). `inscription`
2. The same Balligave record says the Chola immediately besieged Gutti crying "A new reign; a
   kingdom fit for a hero; this is the time to invade it" (Sk 136). `inscription`
3. At Koppam the Cholas captured the Chalukya "victorious banner of the boar", three named war
   elephants (Satrubhayamkara, Karabhadra, Mulabhadra) and two named queens, Sattiyavvai and
   Sangappai (SII III no. 29, l. 11). `inscription`
4. Someshvara I's own records claim the opposite: the Chola king "yielded his head" to him in battle
   (Annigeri 1071) and "in the middle of the battle the Chola king grew faint and died" (Sk 118).
   `inscription`
5. Vikramaditya VI "rubbed out the brilliant Saka-varsha" and renamed the count after himself, year
   1 = 1076–77; but Lakshmeshwar no. 52 (1077) still gives Saka 1000 alongside "Vikrama year 2", so
   the Saka era was not really abolished (Gadag 1098 in Fleet p. 447; SII XX intro). `inscription`
6. The Chalukya-Vikrama era was kept up by feudatories after his death — records exist in its
   52nd to 94th years (1127–1169/70) (Fleet p. 447 n. 4). `inscription`
7. Bilhana rode elephants through Karnata with his parasol carried before the king — recorded not
   by Bilhana but by Kalhana in Kashmir, Rajatarangini VII.937 (Bühler p. 21). `inscription`
8. The whole Vikramankadevacharita survives in one palm-leaf manuscript of 158 leaves in a Jain
   library in Jaisalmer fort; Bühler and Jacobi copied it in about seven days in 1873–74 (Bühler
   pp. 44–46). `scholarship`
9. The Mitakshara ends: "there was not, there is not, and there will be not, a town like Kalyana;
   never was a monarch like the prosperous Vikramarka seen or heard of; and — what more? —
   Vijnanesvara, the Pandit, does not bear comparison with any other" (Bhandarkar 1896 pp. 219–220,
   from the Bombay lithograph and a MS of Samvat 1535). `inscription` (contemporary text)
10. Kalhana says Harsha of Kashmir fell in love with Vikramaditya's queen Chandaladevi from a
    portrait and "contemplated acquiring possession of her by destroying Vikramaditya VI"
    (Rajatarangini VII.1122 ff., in Fleet p. 449 n. 3). `inscription` (contemporary chronicle)
11. In 1060 a man named Tuluva Chandiga, having staked his finger on an agreement about the Banavasi
    fort, cut the finger off at the Permmalu temple, climbed the Bherundesvara pillar at Balligave and
    "threw himself down on the points of spears and died"; the "Sila-Brahma stone" with a figure
    impaled on stakes marks it (EC VII Sk 152, intro p. 20). `inscription`
12. Buddhism was still alive: 1063 a dandanayaka founded the Jayanti Buddhist vihara at Balligave
    with worship of Tara Bhagavati (Sk 170; Rice saw the Tara image); 1095 sixteen Vaidya merchants
    built a vihara at Dambal (Fleet p. 452; Bhandarkar p. 228). `inscription`
13. Bijjala's banner was a golden bull (suvarna-vrishabha-dhvaja) and he was "heralded in public
    with the sounds of the double drum called damaruga"; the family styled itself "lord of Kalanjara"
    in Bundelkhand (EI V no. 25 E l. 55; Fleet p. 469). `inscription`
14. Bijjala did not die on the throne: he abdicated alive after 19 July 1167, and a Sovideva record
    shows him afterwards "putting down the insurgents" (Fleet p. 477; SII XVIII no. 186).
    `inscription`
15. The Ablur temple wall records that Ramayya offered to let the Jains burn his severed head and
    still recover it, against the stake of their "eight hundred shrines" (EI V p. 256). `inscription`
16. Lakkundi had a royal mint: gifts in 1184 and 1186 were paid "out of the income from minting
    transactions" (SII XV nos. 61, 70; puratattva). `inscription`
17. The Kuruvatti Mallikarjuna temple's deity is called Ahavamallesvara in inscriptions of 1181 and
    1194–1205, i.e. the god is named after the drowned king; the temple is dated 1070–1090 (Foekema
    2003) and a Penn Museum Brahma image names Chavundoja as architect of a "Trailokyamallesvara"
    at Kuruvati (puratattva.in/kuruvathi). `inscription`/`scholarship`
18. Hemadri's Vratakhanda credits the Yadava Seunachandra II, not Vikramaditya's own valour, with
    placing him on the throne of Kalyana (Fleet p. 445). `inscription` (13th-c. text)

## 5. Disputed or uncertain points

- **Date and site of Koppam.** Rajendradeva's accession is fixed to c. 1052 by Kielhorn (Wikipedia
  "Battle of Koppam" infobox: 28 May 1052 for the battle is a conflation); the battle is before his
  3rd year (SII III no. 55), i.e. 1052–54; most scholars say 1054 (Sastri). Site: Fleet 1896 p. 441
  = Koppa on the Tunga (Kadur dist.); Hultzsch: "on the bank of the great river"; Krishnamacharlu,
  Sastri, Desai = Koppal (Kopbal) on the Hirehalla; Fleet later = Khidrapur near Kolhapur (Wikipedia
  note 13). Unresolved.
- **Who won Koppam.** Chola records: victory, anointment, Kolhapur pillar. Chalukya records: the
  Chola king "yielded his head". Both agree Rajadhiraja died there. Rajendradeva "crowned himself on
  the battlefield" is Sastri's phrase (1958), not an inscription's.
- **Was Someshvara I's death suicide, euthanasia or rite?** Sk 136 "rites of supreme yoga… ascended
  to heaven in the Tungabhadra"; Bilhana: incurable fever, ministers' consent, walked in "till the
  water reached his neck"; Bhandarkar calls it jalasamadhi (EC VII intro p. 20 n.). Modern "1062"
  (Wikipedia Kuruvatti article) is wrong — the inscription is dated 1068.
- **Vikramaditya VI's coronation day.** Wadageri record: Phalguna sukla 5 = 31 Jan 1077 (Fleet),
  but the weekday fits 11 Feb 1076 (Kielhorn, Ind. Ant. XXII p. 110; Fleet p. 446 n. 6) — Fleet
  thinks "an anniversary festival"; EI XXXIV argues coronation before Phalguna sudi 5 of Nala and
  era from Chaitra sudi 1 = 8 March 1076. Some records start the era in Pingala (1077) or even
  Rakshasa/Kalayukti (EI XXXIV via jainqq).
- **Rajiga/Kulottunga's seizure of the Chola throne**: Bilhana puts it shortly before 1076; Eastern
  Chalukya records give 1070 as Kulottunga's first year (Fleet p. 445 n. 3: "some anachronism here").
- **Someshvara II's fate** after capture: unknown (Fleet p. 445; Wikipedia "Someshvara II" citing
  Sen 1999 p. 386).
- **The three brothers' mothers.** Rice (EC VII) makes Vikramaditya "a younger son of the king by a
  Ganga mother" and Vishnuvardhana-Vijayaditya a son "by an Eastern Chalukya mother"; Fleet p. 440
  n. 3: the Gadag records "say distinctly that they were uterine brothers" of Bachaladevi, the
  Ganga/Pallava/Vengi titles reflecting their governorships.
- **Ranna's patron for Gadayuddha**: Tailapa II or Satyashraya (Bhatia 1970 p. 370); date 982 vs a
  later revision (Govinda Pai). Ranna's birthplace Mudhol is "tradition" (Wikipedia "Ranna").
- **Attimabbe's dates** (Pandit project c. 950–1016) and the numbers 1000 copies / 1500 basadis are
  panegyric from Ranna, not independently attested.
- **Akkadevi**: Wikipedia "Sudi" makes her Satyashraya's daughter; Fleet (and SII XI no. 86) make
  her Jayasimha II's elder sister and Someshvara I's paternal aunt — follow Fleet. Her husband's
  name "has not yet been traced" (Fleet p. 435); modern pages naming him are unsupported here.
- **Bijjala's religion**: Fleet 1896 p. 464 ("himself a Jain", from tradition) vs Desai EI XXIX
  ("a staunch follower of Saivism… no particular interest… in Jainism"); the Ablur record shows him
  arbitrating, laughing at the Jains, and granting to a Shaiva temple. Wikipedia "Bijjala II"
  infobox "Jainism" cites Settar 1989 for Jain patronage only.
- **Bijjala's death**: assassination (Lingayat Puranas, 1369 and 1585 texts; also Wikipedia citing
  Sastri 1955 p. 366 "radical Sharanas"), poisoning (Jain Bijjalarayacharitra), or unrecorded natural
  death after 1167 abdication (inscriptions silent). Fleet: "if tradition is to be believed".
- **Basava's historicity and office**: Fleet 1896 (no epigraph names him; Managoli 1161 Basavarasayya
  possible); Desai 1951–52 (later epigraphs and texts "established him as a historic figure"); dates
  1131–1196 (Olson 2007 via Wikipedia) vs death 1167/68 in Kannada tradition — the 1196 date sits
  thirty years after Bijjala's abdication and is doubtful; Britannica: "administrator of the royal
  treasury of… Bijjala I (reigned 1156–67)". Mahamatya/prime minister title: Kamath 2001 p. 134.
- **Anubhava Mantapa**: no contemporary inscription; Wikipedia's claims are unfootnoted; the
  physical site "eludes researchers" (The Hindu 2016). Treat as tradition rooted in the vachanas'
  own reports of gatherings.
- **Kudala Sangama** of Basava's end: Elliot = Sangam at the Krishna–Malaprabha junction (Hungund
  tk., with the "depression in the linga"); Fleet p. 480 n. 1 prefers Kudal-Sangam at the Krishna–
  Tungabhadra junction. Modern Kudalasangama (Bagalkot) follows Elliot.
- **Ekantada Ramayya vs Basava**: Fleet suggested Ramayya "in whom we may find the original of the
  Basava of the Lingayat Puranas" is the true originator; Desai rejects the identification and
  treats them as contemporaries leading one upsurge.
- **Munja's end**: inscriptions (Sangamner 1000, Kauthem 1009, Gadag 1098) only say captured/killed
  by Taila or Bhillama; "sixteen defeats", Mrinalavati, door-to-door begging are Merutunga 1304.
- **Someshvara IV's first year** (1182–83 or 1183–84) and his end: Fleet "driven back into the
  extreme south-west… unlikely that he survived any length of time" after 1189; Wikipedia gives
  1189–1200 variously and "exile to Banavasi" without citation.
- **Chandaladevi's father**: Bilhana names the Silahara of Karahata, "probably Narasimha" (Fleet
  p. 449); not confirmed by a Silahara record.

### Dress, armour, weapons (what the sources actually attest)
- Royal insignia named in inscriptions: the white umbrella (chhatra), jewels, throne (Sk 136:
  "elephants and horses, the jewels, umbrella, and throne"); the five great drums (panchamahasabda)
  of feudatories; the Chalukya boar banner (varaha-dhvaja — captured at Koppam, SII III no. 29);
  the Kalachurya golden-bull banner and damaruga drum (EI V). `inscription`
- Ganda-bherunda (two-headed bird) atop Chavunda-Rayarasa's 1047 pillar at Balligave (EC VII intro
  p. 19) — a Chalukya feudatory emblem later adopted by Mysore; usable as a court motif. `inscription`
- Weapons in the Ablur record: scimitar/sword (the head-cutting), horses, guards; Koppam: "shower of
  straight arrows", elephants with mounted warriors "wearing ankle-rings" (SII III no. 29 l. 8);
  camels in the Chalukya baggage train (ibid. l. 11). `inscription`
- The Manasollasa (1129) describes royal dress, jewellery, tilak styles, perfumes, hunting with
  dogs and falcons, elephant and horse veterinary care, wrestling, polo-like team sports, and a
  court that eats idli/dosa-type fermented foods but no beef (Kamat 1980; Achaya 2003 via Wikipedia
  "Manasollasa") — the single best costume/props source for a 12th-century Kalyana court.
  `contemporary text`
- Sculpture to copy for costume: Kuruvatti bracket figures and door-guardians; Itagi mantapa
  figures; Balligavi hero stones (male dress: dhoti, bare torso, turban/crown, sword and shield;
  female: heavy jewellery, breast-band or bare torso, elaborate hair) — all 11th–12th c. Chalukya.
- Lingayat emblems (ishtalinga in a silver box worn at the neck — Fleet p. 477) are attested for the
  sect; the modern Lingayat flag/symbol files on Commons are 20th-century, do not use for 1160.

### Anachronisms to avoid
- No Vijayanagara-style gopuras, no Hoysala star-plans before c. 1060, no Bahmani domes at Kalyana
  (the present Basavakalyana fort is 14th-century and later).
- No firearms (Rice's "musketeers" in EC VII intro p. 28 is a 1902 mistranslation of a 12th-century
  term — do not use), no stirrup-era plate armour, no Persianate dress.
- Vikramaditya VI's era, not Saka years, on any depicted document after 1076.
- Basavanna's statue iconography (18th–21st c.) and the modern 108-ft statue; "Anubhava Mantapa"
  buildings now under construction are modern.
- Rajadhiraja did not die at Kalyana (Koppam is on a river far from the capital); Kalyana was sacked
  by the Cholas c. 1045 per Sastri, but no inscription describes the city's buildings.
- Coins: punch-marked gold, not struck portrait coins.

## 7. Key people beyond the kings (one line each)

- **Ranna (ರನ್ನ)** — Jain Kannada poet; Gadayuddha (982/later) for Taila II or Satyashraya;
  Ajitapurana (993) for Attimabbe; Rannakanda (990), the oldest Kannada word-list; title
  Kavichakravarti from Satyashraya (Sastri 2002 p. 356 via Wikipedia). `scholarship`
- **Attimabbe (ಅತ್ತಿಮಬ್ಬೆ)** — daughter of Mallapa, wife of Nagadeva (son of Dhalla, Vaji family),
  "Danachintamani"; built the Jain temple at Lokkigundi and endowed it in Saka 929 = 1007 (SII XI
  no. 52). `inscription`
- **Akkadevi (ಅಕ್ಕಾದೇವಿ)** — Jayasimha II's elder sister, governor of Kisukad-70 (1021–1053),
  besieged Gokage 1047–48, mother of Kadamba Toyimadeva (Fleet p. 435). `inscription`
- **Chavundaraya / Chamunda-Rayarasa (ಚಾವುಂಡರಾಯ)** — Someshvara I's governor of Banavasi, "lord of
  Banavasi-pura, a brave at the courts of three kings", raised the ganda-bherunda pillar at
  Balligave 1047 (EC VII Sk 151, 160). `inscription` (not the Ganga minister of Shravanabelagola).
- **Lakshma / Lakshmarasa (ಲಕ್ಷ್ಮರಸ)** — Someshvara II's governor of Banavasi and Belvola, "like a
  long bar to the south", restored the Jain temples the Cholas burnt; his Jain minister Somanatha,
  poet of Sukumara-charita (Sk 136; Annigeri 1071). `inscription`
- **Bachaladevi (ಬಾಚಲದೇವಿ)** — queen of Someshvara I, mother of Someshvara II, Vikramaditya VI and
  Jayasimha III (Gadag 1098, 1102). `inscription`
- **Mailaladevi, Liladevi, Ketaladevi, Chandrikadevi, Hoysaladevi** — other queens of Someshvara I
  (SII XI nos. 83, 85; Fleet p. 438; EC VII Hl 1). `inscription`
- **Jayasimha III (ಮೂರನೇ ಜಯಸಿಂಹ)** — youngest brother, yuvaraja at Banavasi 1077–82, rebelled and
  vanished from records (Fleet pp. 453–454). `inscription`
- **Vishnuvardhana-Vijayaditya (ವಿಷ್ಣುವರ್ಧನ ವಿಜಯಾದಿತ್ಯ)** — fourth son of Someshvara I, "lord of
  Vengi", ruling Nolambavadi from Kampili 1064–66 (Fleet p. 454). `inscription`
- **Bilhana (ಬಿಲ್ಹಣ)** — Kashmiri Brahman of Khonamukha, travelled via Mathura, Kanauj, Prayaga,
  Banaras, Karna of Dahala's court, Anhilwad, Somnath, by sea to the Konkan, Rameshvaram; Vidyapati
  at Kalyana; wrote the Vikramankadevacharita c. 1085 (Bühler pp. 16–22). `scholarship`
- **Vijnaneshvara (ವಿಜ್ಞಾನೇಶ್ವರ)** — author of the Mitakshara at Kalyana under Vikramaditya VI;
  cites Bhoja ("Dharesvara"); the text governed Hindu inheritance law in British India outside
  Bengal (Bhandarkar 1896 pp. 219, 229; Lingat 1973 p. 113 via Wikipedia). `scholarship`
- **Chandaladevi / Chandralekha (ಚಂದಲದೇವಿ)** — Silahara princess of Karahata, Vikramaditya VI's
  piriyarasi, mother of Jayakarna; heroine of Bilhana's svayamvara cantos; object of Harsha of
  Kashmir's portrait-infatuation (Fleet p. 449; Rajatarangini VII). `inscription`/literary
- **Lakshmadevi (ಲಕ್ಷ್ಮಾದೇವಿ)** — chief queen "ruling at the capital of Kalyana" 1084–85, governing
  the eighteen agraharas and Dambal 1095–96, alive 1125–26 (Fleet p. 448). `inscription`
- **Mailalamahadevi (ಮೈಳಲಮಹಾದೇವಿ)** — Vikramaditya VI's daughter, married to Kadamba Jayakesin II of
  Goa (Fleet p. 449). `inscription`
- **Achugi II (ಆಚುಗಿ), Sinda of Yelburga** — the general who stopped the Hoysala Vishnuvardhana's
  c. 1117 invasion, burnt Goa and Uppinakatte (Fleet p. 453). `inscription`
- **Gangaraja (ಗಂಗರಾಜ)** — Hoysala dandanayaka who claimed a night attack on Vikramaditya's camp at
  Kannegala (Fleet p. 453). `inscription`
- **Anantapalayya, Govindarasa, Nagavarmayya, Bhivanayya** — Vikramaditya VI's great tax and
  provincial officers (vaddaravula, perjunka, pannaya taxes) (Fleet pp. 451–452). `inscription`
- **Someshvara III as author**; **Jagadekamalla II** author of Sangitachudamani on music (Upadhye,
  Desai via Wikipedia). `scholarship`
- **Taila III (ಮೂರನೇ ತೈಲ)** — captured by Kakatiya Prola II (Kamath 2001 p. 112), overshadowed by
  Bijjala from 1156, fled to Annigeri (Wikipedia, uncited). `scholarship`/unknown
- **Kasapayya-nayaka and Kesiraja/Kesimayya** — Bijjala's great minister and Banavasi governor
  (1159–62), patrons of the Kodiya-matha at Balligave, described as a free hospital, school of the
  six darshanas and shelter for the destitute (EC VII intro pp. 27–28, Sk 102, 123). `inscription`
- **Recharasa (ರೇಚರಸ)** — "a principal part is claimed" for him in winning the kingdom for Bijjala
  (EC VII Sk 197, intro p. 28). `inscription`
- **Bijjala's sons Sovideva, Sankama, Ahavamalla, Singhana; brother Mailugi/Mallugi** (Fleet pp. 477,
  484–489; EC VII Hl 50). `inscription`
- **Brahma / Bamma / Bommarasa (ಬಮ್ಮರಸ)** — Kalachurya senapati who defected and restored Someshvara
  IV, "establisher of the Chalukya sovereignty" (Annigere 1184–85, Fleet pp. 464–465). `inscription`
- **Ekantada Ramayya (ಏಕಾಂತದ ರಾಮಯ್ಯ)** — Shaiva Brahman of Alande, son of Purushottamabhatta and
  Padmambike, founder of Vira-Somanatha at Ablur; later enshrined there as a linga (EI V no. 25 E;
  Desai EI XXIX). `inscription`
- **Basavanna (ಬಸವಣ್ಣ)** — Shaiva reformer, vachana poet (ankita Kudalasangamadeva); tradition:
  born Bagevadi to Madiraja and Madalambike, Bijjala's treasurer/minister, left Kalyana for
  Kudala-Sangama. Contemporary epigraphic mention: none certain (Managoli 1161 possible).
  `legend`/`scholarship`
- **Allama Prabhu (ಅಲ್ಲಮಪ್ರಭು)** — vachana mystic (ankita Guheshvara), temple drummer of
  Balligavi per Harihara; c. 1,300 vachanas attributed; tradition makes him president of the
  Anubhava Mantapa and locates his end at Srisailam (Ramanujan 1973; Shiva Prakash 1997 via
  Wikipedia). `legend` for life, `contemporary text` for vachanas.
- **Akka Mahadevi (ಅಕ್ಕಮಹಾದೇವಿ)** — vachana poet of Udutadi (ankita Chennamallikarjuna), c. 350–430
  vachanas; the Kaushika marriage and renunciation are from Harihara and later; "little is known
  about her life" (Tharu & Lalita 1991 p. 78 via Wikipedia). `legend`/`contemporary text`
- **Channabasava (ಚೆನ್ನಬಸವಣ್ಣ)** — Basava's sister's son in the Puranas; Fleet suggests the sect was
  "actually established, in somewhat later times, by a person named Channabasava" (EI V p. 244).
  `legend`
- **Harihara (ಹರಿಹರ)** — 13th-century Hampi poet, Basavarajadevara Ragale, first biography of
  Basava. `scholarship`
- **Jagaddeva (ಜಗದ್ದೇವ)**, Mallaya, Bommaya — the assassins of Bijjala in the Basava Purana (Bhandarkar
  p. 226). `legend`
- **Halleyaga/Haralayya and Madhuveyya/Madhuvarasa** — the blinded devotees whose treatment triggers
  the murder in the Puranas; the inter-caste wedding of their children is a later elaboration not
  found in the 1896 summaries (source for its first appearance not established here). `legend`
- **Munja (Vakpati Munja) of Malwa** — Taila II's enemy, captured and killed in the Deccan 994–998
  (Fleet p. 432; Wikipedia "Vakpati Munja" citing Nilakanta Sastri 1960 p. 322). `inscription`/`legend`
- **Rajadhiraja, Rajendradeva, Virarajendra, Kulottunga I (Rajiga)** — the Chola adversaries
  (SII III). `inscription`
- **Jayakesin I and II of Goa; Vishnuvardhana Hoysala; Vira-Ballala II; Bhillama V Seuna; Kakatiya
  Prola II** — feudatories who outgrew the empire (Fleet chs. IV–VI). `inscription`

## 8. Sources to avoid (or use only with the corrections noted)

- **Wikipedia "Mallikarjuna Temple, Kuruvatti"** — says Someshvara I died 1062; the inscription says
  29 March 1068.
- **Wikipedia "Battle of Koppam"** — infobox "28 May 1052" is Rajendradeva's accession date, not the
  battle; gives two dates and two sites without resolving them; "Jayasimha killed" is uncited there
  (it is in SII III no. 29).
- **Wikipedia "Akkadevi"** — makes her Jayasimha II's sister (correct) but the dates 1010–1064 and
  the "Bidar, Bagalkot and Bijapur" province are not from inscriptions; "Sudi, India" article calls
  her Satyashraya's daughter (wrong per Fleet). Zenodo 2023 paper calls her a "Kadamba queen" (she
  married a Kadamba; she was Chalukya by birth).
- **Wikipedia "Vikramaditya VI"** — "abolition of the Saka era" is unfootnoted and overstated (see
  Lakshmeshwar no. 52); the Cousens date is cited as both 1921 and 1926.
- **Wikipedia "Someshvara I"** — calls Jayasimha both brother and son; Malwa raids paragraph has no
  footnote.
- **Wikipedia "Bijjala II"** — "By 1162 CE Bijjala II had managed to drive Taila III out of Kalyani"
  is tagged citation-needed; reign "1130–1167" is unfootnoted (his own reckoning starts 1156–57);
  "Mahamandaleshwara over Karhada-4000" unfootnoted.
- **Wikipedia "Kalachuris of Kalyani"** — cites scribd and kamat.com; no footnote for assassination,
  dates or emblem; does not mention Someshvara IV.
- **Wikipedia "Anubhava Mantapa"** — essentially unfootnoted; "first religious parliament in the
  world" is promotional.
- **Wikipedia "Someshvara IV"** — three different reign spans (1181–1189, 1183–1200, 1184–1200) and
  "exile to Banavasi" without source; Britannica-level only.
- **Wikipedia "Basava"** — death 1196 (Olson) conflicts with every Kannada tradition and with
  Bijjala's chronology; Arjunavada inscription appears only as an uncited caption.
- **Wikipedia "Tailapa III"** — death "killed by Hoysala Vira Narasimha in 1162" uncited.
- **Bharatpedia, classicistranieri Wikipedia-2007 mirrors, prepp.in, ebsco "research starters",
  indianetzone, hindupedia, jainheritagecentres, shastriyakannada.org** — uncited popular rewrites;
  use none as a citation.
- **kamat.com "Chalukyas of Kalyana"** — popular, no references.
- **Lingayatreligion.com** — devotional; cited by Wikipedia for Bhima Kavi's date only.
- **Rice's EC VII introduction** — excellent for records but "musketeers" (p. 28) and "Tagiri" are
  translation slips Fleet corrects; Rice's "Ganga mother"/"Eastern Chalukya mother" for the princes
  is contradicted by Gadag 1098.
- **Sir Walter Elliot's Carnatic-Desa transcripts** (as quoted in Fleet) — Fleet repeatedly notes
  copyist errors (wrong samvatsaras, "Trailokyamalla" for "Tribhuvanamalla"); always prefer Fleet's
  ink-impression readings.
- **The Basava Purana, Channabasava Purana, Bijjalarayacharitra** — not sources for events: the
  Channabasava Purana dates the murder to 785 CE; the Jain poem puts it before 1156; they contradict
  each other on method (stabbing vs poison) and place (Kalyana vs the Bhima camp) (Fleet p. 481).
- **Merutunga's Prabandhachintamani** (1304) for Munja — Jain prabandha three centuries late; the
  inscriptions only say captured/killed.
- **Any page stating Rajendra II "crowned himself on the battlefield" as an inscription's words** —
  it is Sastri's gloss; the inscriptions say he "performed the anointment of victory".
