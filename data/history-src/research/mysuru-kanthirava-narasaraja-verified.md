## K§1. The land and the house

**K§1a. The political situation, 1636–1638.** HR I pp. 117–118:
> In May-June 1636, Shah Jahan had concluded a partition treaty with Adil Shah and Qutb Shah, defining
> the boundaries of the respective powers. As a result of this, the advance of the Shahi kingdoms further
> northwards was arrested, leaving them, however, unrestricted scope for expansion in the south and the
> east ... while the Empire of Vijayanagar, under Venkata II, was already in the throes of dissolution
`scholarship`.

**K§1b. Srirangapatna, 1610** (from mysuru-verified.md §1b, HR I pp. 59–60):
> he entered and formally took possession of the city of Seringapatam on February 8, 1610
`scholarship`.

**K§1c. Lineage.** HR I p. 113:
> Bettada-Chamaraja Wodeyar was, as we have seen, a younger brother of Raja Wodeyar ... Bettada-Chamaraja
> is said to have had two sons, Timmaraja Wodeyar, by Lingajamma of Bagali, and Kanthirava-Narasaraja
> Wodeyar, by Guruvajamma (Guruvamba) of Hura ... Timmaraja Wodeyar, the first son of Bettada-Chamaraja
> Wodeyar, had, however, it would seem, predeceased his father, so that the next immediate claimant in
> the line of Bettada-Chamaraja was his second son, Kanthirava-Narasaraja Wodeyar.
HR I p. 106 (reign of Chamaraja V): "Bettada-Chamaraja Wodeyar and Muppina-Devaraja Wodeyar, the two
surviving younger brothers of Raja Wodeyar, seem to have been living in peace and quiet, with their sons,
in Gundlu-Terakanambi and Yeleyur". HR I p. 75: in 1604 "some differences arose between Bettada-Chamaraja
Wodeyar and Raja Wodeyar". WILKS I p. 48: "While living in obscurity in a remote village". `scholarship`.

**K§1d. Birth on Narasimha's day.** HR I p. 114:
> Kanthirava-Narasaraja Wodeyar was born on May 2, 1615, on the Nrsimha-Jayanti day, under the
> constellation Svati.
EC III Introduction p. 27, on Nj. 198 (the Gajjiganahalli plates, 1639): "From him sprung Betta-Chama, to
whom, on the day of maha-jayanti, was born Narasa, a destroyer of the race of Yavanas. This is the king
who makes the grant." `inscription` (the plate) / `scholarship` (the date reading).

**K§1e. The name.** MW p. 246: "kaṇṭhī-rava m. 'roaring from the throat', a lion". `scholarship`.

**K§1f. Training with the Hura cousins.** HR I p. 114 (from K.N.V. IV):
> In his boyhood he was, we learn, brought up along with Nanjarajaiya (Nanjendra) and Lingarajaiya
> (Lingendra), sons of his maternal uncle, Kemparajaiya (Kempa-Bhupa) of Hura, and was trained with them
> in horse-riding, elephant-riding, archery ... and the use of various kinds of weapons, such as the
> spear, lance, dagger, club, sword and discus
"Kanthirava is said to have passed his youth in Gundlu-Terakanambi with his father". `scholarship`.

## K§2. The legend of Trichinopoly (`legend`)

HR I pp. 114–115 (Annals I. 66–67; "compare Wilks"):
> A pious Brahman, while on his return journey from a pilgrimage to Rameswaram, it is said, casually
> mentioned to him that there was a champion at the court of the chief of Trichinopoly, who, over-confident
> of his strength, had proclaimed a general challenge against all his antagonists. Kanthirava, with all
> the ardour of a young man, at once secretly proceeded thither, accompanied by the Brahman. Disguised as
> a professional wrestler, he effected his entrance into the fort of Trichinopoly, whose chief welcomed
> him to an open combat with his champion. ... pierce his neck through with his broad-sword (named
> Vijaya-Narasimha) which he had secreted in his waist. ... the head of the local champion roll down on
> its being merely touched by Kanthirava at the point of his staff. The chief of Trichinopoly was about to
> reward the victor of the day but Kanthirava, disdaining all honours, quietly left the place for
> Terakanambi the very same night, leaving a placard on the fort-wall to the effect that the feat of arms
> had been performed by some one from Mysore. On his return journey, Kanthirava is said to have come across
> a pillar of slate-stone and so dexterously cut it asunder that the sharpness of the blade of his
> broad-sword was by no means lost in the attempt.
WILKS I p. 48: "An instance is preserved of his chivalrous spirit, which seems to be well authenticated.
... the broad sword having been determined as the weapon, he defeated and slew the champion, in presence
of the whole court ... he absconded in the night, and returned to his humble habitation". RICE 1897 p. 364
repeats it ("a celebrated champion athlete at Trichinopoly"). No contemporary record. Tier `legend`.

**K§2b. The twenty-five hirelings** (`legend`). HR I p. 209 fn 190: "The Annals (I. 77–78), for instance,
records how, shortly after Ranadulla Khan's unsuccessful siege of Seringapatam in 1639, Kanthirava was, by
the might of his arms, able, single-handed, to overcome an organised attack on his person (in the
Seringapatam Palace) by twenty-five hirelings sent by the chief of Trichinopoly".

## K§3. Accession, 1638

**K§3a.** HR I p. 113: "ON THE death of Immadi-Raja Wodeyar without issue, direct descent in the line of
Raja Wodeyar came to an end." HR I p. 115:
> Kanthirava-Narasaraja Wodeyar ascended the throne of Mysore on November 22, 1638, in his twenty-fourth
> year, a month and a half after the death of Immadi-Raja Wodeyar. During the intervening period Dalavai
> Vikrama-Raya is said to have actually usurped the State.
`scholarship`.

**K§3b. The poisoning tradition** (`legend`). WILKS I p. 48: Immadi Raja "was poisoned at the expiration
of a year and a half by his Dulwoy (general and minister) Veecrama Raj". RICE 1897 p. 364: "He was shortly
poisoned by the dalavayi". HR I p. 116: "Neither of these traditions has, however, so far been
corroborated."

**K§3c. "I have not yet installed him."** WILKS I pp. 48–49 (`legend`, Wilks's manuscripts):
> On his arrival at Mysoor, where it was still the practice to instal the Rajas, the minister ordered that
> he should be lodged in an exterior apartment : and assigning to him a few personal attendants ...
> departing on a tour of the neighbouring districts, without going through the form of installing him
> ... "Let him take care," said the minister, negligently, "and remember that I have not yet installed
> him."
HR I p. 538 (Appendix IV (2), from Mys. Dho. Pur.): "Kanthirava-Narasaraja Wodeyar proceeded from Nallur
to Mysore, with a view to being installed; and took up his residence at the local gymnasium
(garadi-mane)."

**K§3d. The dalavai's fate (disputed).** WILKS I pp. 49–50 and HR I pp. 538–539: two attendants killed
him in the dark ("Are you uppermost or undermost?"). Against it, HR I p. 116: a Nanjangud inscription of
January 11, 1644 was set up by "Dalavai Vikrama-Raya, a son of Bettada-Chamaraja Wodeyar"; HR I pp. 116–117:
> It appears, therefore, probable that Vikrama-Raya was dismissed from service on the accession of
> Kanthirava in November 1638 and allowed to reside in some part of Mysore during the rest of his life
HR I p. 116 fn 14: the inscription "would corroborate the Annals that Vikrama-Raya was a natural son
(gandharva-putra) of Bettada-Chamaraja". `scholarship` (the dismissal) / `legend` (the killing).

**K§3e. New Dalavai.** HR I p. 117: "Timmarajaiya was appointed Dalavai on November 26, 1638".
Nanjarajaiya "seems to have played an active part in the events of the period, as the king's relation and
right-hand man, till his own appointment as Dalavai in 1640" (p. 117 fn 15). `scholarship`.

## K§4. The Bijapur advance, 1637–1638

HR I pp. 118–119 (`scholarship`):
> the Bijapur army, under Ranadulla Khan (Rustam-i-Zaman), laid siege to and destroyed Ikkeri. ...
> Sira was taken by Afzal Khan and its chief, Kasturi-Ranga, put to death during an interview. Turuvekere
> and Tumkur were ravaged, the chief of the latter place taking to flight panic-stricken. ... The fort of
> Bangalore was taken from Immadi-Kempe-Gauda who retreated to Magadi leaving his son a hostage in the
> hands of Ranadulla Khan. Placing Shahji : father of Sivaji : a second in command, in charge of Bangalore,
> Ranadulla Khan proceeded further south. ... he next took Ramagiri-durga where he held a review of his
> forces. About the end of 1638, he advanced towards Channapatna with eventual designs on Seringapatam.
WILKS I p. 50 and footnote: "a general of reputation, named Rend Dhoola Khan *" ; "* The bridegroom of the
field of battle." `scholarship`.

## K§5. The demand and the refusal

HR I p. 120 (K.N.V. XII; `scholarship`):
> Before commencing operations, however, Ranadulla Khan sent word to Kanthirava-Narasaraja Wodeyar,
> demanding payment of tribute to Bijapur. Kanthirava stoutly refused to accede to the demand, sent back
> the Khan's messengers and hastened the preparations for the defence of the capital. On this, Ranadulla
> Khan resolved upon the siege of Seringapatam. In vain did Kenge-Hanumappa Nayaka remonstrate with him to
> give up his project and make peace. Heedless of the advice, Ranadulla Khan persisted ... While
> Kanthirava ordered a general mobilisation of his forces and endeavoured to obstruct the passage of the
> enemy.

## K§6. The armies and the siege

**K§6a. Armies.** HR I pp. 120–121:
> The investing army consisted of a miscellaneous rabble ranging from 40 to 50 thousand horse, 3 to 4
> lakhs foot and between 500 to 1,000 elephants. ... two divisions : the original Bijapur army, under the
> direct command of Ranadulla Khan himself, and the levies of Karnatak chieftains led by
> Kenge-Hanumappa Nayaka (of Basavapatna). The Mysore army was mostly composed of the levies of the
> tributary chiefs of Hura, Channapatna, Maddur, Satyagala, Heggaddevankote, Channarayapatna, Kikkeri,
> Bukankere, Piriyapatna, Talakad, Malavalli and Nagamangala
p. 121 fn 28: "As a poet, however, he delineates the heroic and other sentiments and his accounts are not
altogether free from exaggeration." `scholarship` (numbers from the poet: disputed).

**K§6b. Positions.** HR I pp. 121–122: "The major portion of the Karnatak forces halted on the southern
bank of the Cauvery, with Hanumappa Nayaka at their head; the Morasas and the chief of Belur, in
particular, took up a convenient position on the northern bank of the river; while the main army of
Ranadulla Khan encamped in the other directions."

**K§6c. Date and length.** HR I p. 122: "On January 18, 1639, Ranadulla Khan laid siege to Seringapatam.
The siege lasted only three days". p. 120 fn 19: the week-days in the chronicle "correspond to January
18-21, 1639". `scholarship`.

**K§6d. Night attacks.** HR I p. 122:
> Kanthirava, personally leading his troops, directed the defence operations ... A section of his army
> delivered a surprise night-attack on the Bijapuris encamped at Arakere ; another fell upon those who had
> halted at Hosaholalu ; a third obstructed the passage of the enemy near Melkote (Yadavadri) ; and a
> fourth one surprised the vast array of the investing forces in the neighbourhood of the Chamundi Hills,
> Mysore : putting them to rout amidst great slaughter and cutting off the noses of several of their
> opponents.
The Khan's deputies "had, in the very beginning, complained to him of the reverses they had sustained".

**K§6e. The breach and the stand (the turning point).** HR I pp. 122–123:
> Despite the calamity which had thus attended his army, and the steady opposition his men met with from
> the besieged, Ranadulla Khan persisted in his resolve to take the fort. By successive attacks he had
> effected a breach and almost scaled the walls, when Kanthirava brought together all the scattered
> elements of his army and offered stout resistance. A severe struggle followed, in which the besiegers
> were thoroughly beaten and repulsed with considerable loss (in killed and wounded) in their ranks.
> Ranadulla Khan was obliged to beat a hasty retreat from Seringapatam and Kanthirava's troops returned
> to the capital with immense spoils.
WILKS I p. 50: "having effected a practicable breach, made a general assault, in which he was repulsed
with great slaughter; and not only compelled to raise the siege, but harassed in his retreat by successive
attacks, in which the Raja obtained considerable booty." RICE 1897 p. 365: "succeeded in effectually
repelling the invader". `scholarship`.

**K§6f. The truce.** HR I p. 123:
> On the fourth day (January 21), Ranadulla Khan found it expedient to sue for peace. He had already
> received an express message from Bijapur urging his return ... Following the advice of
> Kenge-Hanumappa Nayaka, he arranged for a truce with Kanthirava through two of the latter's agents
> (named Kaveri-Hebbaruva and Minchu-Hebbaruva), stipulating that the territory to the south of the
> Cauvery was to remain in the undisturbed possession of the king of Mysore while the right to the
> revenues of the territory north of the river was to belong exclusively to the Adil Shah of Bijapur ...
> The truce was agreed to by Kanthirava in view of its ensuring him the territorial integrity of Mysore
> and of its relieving him from the necessity of withstanding another possible siege
"about the middle of February, Ranadulla Khan raised the siege of Seringapatam and retraced his steps to
Bijapur, placing some of his forces under Hanumappa Nayaka". `scholarship`.

**K§6g. The Bijapur version** (`inscription`, contemporary official chronicle, via Sarkar). HR I
pp. 539–540, Appendix IV (3), quoting the *Muhammad-Namah* of Zahur bin Zahuri:
> After fighting and exertion on both sides had been protracted for nearly a month, the Rajah sent his
> envoy to Rustam Khan, saying 'Please leave the fort of Srirangapatan to me, as you have done to other
> (Rajahs) ... and lay before the throne the five lakhs of hun in cash and presents of various kinds which
> I am offering.'
HR I pp. 540–541: "Its version seems to be based on reports compiled at a place remote from the scene of
action and is, further, not corroborated by independent evidence so far. ... The submission may not have
been an actual fact".

**K§6h. Importance.** HR I p. 124: "There is little doubt that Kanthirava-Narasaraja Wodeyar was able to
win a complete victory over the invaders. Two causes appear to have accounted for his success : firstly,
the efficiency of his army, though small in numbers, and its knowledge of local conditions ... secondly,
the special features of the Mysorean warfare of the time, namely, surprise night-attacks and the 'cutting
off noses.'" `scholarship`.

## K§7. The father's sword, the father's death, the first plate

**K§7a.** HR I p. 124 (Mys. Dho. Vam.; `legend`, "is said"):
> At the news of this victory, Bettada-Chamaraja Wodeyar, father of Kanthirava-Narasaraja Wodeyar, is
> said to have sent him from Gundlu-Terakanambi his (Kanthirava's) broad-sword, Vijaya-Narasimha, as a
> mark of appreciation of his prowess.

**K§7b.** HR I p. 201 fn 163 (`scholarship` from `inscription`):
> from the Gajjiganahalli copper-plate grant [E. C. III (1) Nj. 198], issued on Pramathi, Chaitra su. 13
> (April 7, 1639), we note that Bettada-Chamaraja Wodeyar had passed away before that date, and the grant
> itself was made for the eternal merit of Bettada-Chamaraja Wodeyar ... Accordingly we have to fix his
> death on or before ... (March 26, 1639), two months after Ranadulla Khan's siege of Seringapatam and his
> repulse.
HR I p. 117: "the earliest record referring to him as king being the Gajjiganahalli copper-plate grant
(April 1639)". HR I p. 124 fn 38 quotes Nj. 198: "Atmodbhavo Naraharir-Narasa-kshitindrah ... yavananvaya
daitya bhedi". EC III Intro p. 27 (above, K§1d). `inscription`.

**K§7c. The fort rebuilt.** HR I p. 160: "The fort of Seringapatam having sustained serious damages during
the siege of 1639, Kanthirava took a keen personal interest in the work of improving and extending it ...
Huge flat stones were made use of in enlarging and strengthening the ramparts, bastions and ditches."
WILKS I p. 51: "He improved and enlarged the fortifications of Seringapatam". `scholarship`.

## K§8. The later reign

**K§8a. Bijapur again, 1640–1641.** HR I pp. 125–128: renewed demands refused; Ramagiri-durga taken by
Mysore c. July–August 1640; Mustafa Khan defeated December 1640; Vedoji routed March 1641: "her campaigns
(of 1640-1641) against Seringapatam thus ended in failure." `scholarship`.

**K§8b. Piriyapatna, 1645.** HR I pp. 133–137: "On October 7, 1645, Piriyapatna, after a long siege of
nearly nine months, was annexed to Mysore." `scholarship`.

**K§8c. Nanjarajaiya falls.** HR I p. 139: "It was a complete victory for Mysore but her loss was equally
great, for, in the confusion which followed the attack, Dalavai Nanjarajaiya, fighting desperately against
heavy odds, was himself slain on the field of battle (early in January 1647)." fn 87: the Annals instead
speak of his "removal from service ... in January 1647". `scholarship`, disputed.

**K§8d. The coins.** HR I p. 161: "The first coins were, accordingly, struck in Kanthirava's name, on the
26th of April 1645. These are gold ones, variously known as Kanthiraya-hana ... and are impressed with the
figure of God Lakshmi-Narasimha on the obverse". HR I pp. 541–542 (Appendix IV (4), M. H. Krishna): obverse
"the figure of four-armed Narasimha seated to the front holding conch and discus"; reverse a Nagari legend
"Sri / Kamthi / rava". WILKS I p. 51: "He was the first Raja of Mysoor who established a mint. The cantyrai
boons and fanams, called after his name, continued to be the sole national coin until the Mohammedan
usurpation". RICE 1897 p. 803: "the accounts continued to be kept in Kanthiraya varaha and hana, the
Canteroy pagodas and fanams of the English treaties with Mysore and of the official accounts down to the
time of the British assumption." `inscription` (the coins) / `scholarship`.

**K§8e. "Taken for Nrsimha."** HR I p. 165, translating E.C. V Ag. 64 (April 1647): "The king Kanthirava
was taken by the people for God Nrsimha. Seeing that from love of money the people had forgotten Vishnu,
the wise king Kanthirava made from that money Nrhari and preserved the people". `inscription`.

**K§8f. The temple.** HR I p. 165: "the construction by him (between 1645-1648) of a temple to God
Lakshmi-Narasimha, to the right of his Palace at Seringapatam, for the spiritual benefit of his people".
HR I p. 209: "The most enduring monuments of his rule extant are the Narasimhasvami temple at
Seringapatam and the Bangaradoddi canal in its neighbourhood." `scholarship`.

**K§8g. The Madura war and its cruelty.** HR I pp. 152–154, quoting the Jesuit Proenza (letter of 1659):
"The king of Mysore had ordered to cut off the nose of all the prisoners ... The Nayak, resenting this
procedure ... ordered reprisals; and his troops burst out into the provinces of Mysore, seeking not
enemies to fight, but noses to cut. It is this which has given to this inhuman war the name of 'hunt for
noses.' The king of Mysore ... himself lost his own nose". Mackenzie manuscripts: Madura forces "hotly
pursued the retiring Mysore army into its own territories, as far as Nanjangud". Rao (p. 154): Proenza
"does seem to exaggerate"; "Nor is it likely that Kanthirava-Narasaraja Wodeyar himself ever took part in
the war and sustained loss of his own nose ... this part of the account cannot be literally accepted".
`scholarship`, disputed.

**K§8h. The last years and death.** HR I p. 203:
> The last days of Kanthirava-Narasaraja Wodeyar (particularly during the period 1653-1659) seem to have
> been rather unhappy. His domestic felicity suffered considerably by the death of his only son (c.
> 1653-1654), and his political position itself was seriously threatened by the calamitous invasion of
> his territories by Khan Muhammad of Bijapur, by the war with Madura and by the rise to political
> prominence of Sivappa Nayaka I of Ikkeri.
> On July 31, 1659 passed away Kanthirava-Narasaraja Wodeyar, in his forty-fifth year, his queens, it is
> said, observing sati.
EC III Intro p. 29 notes Nj. 81 "as if Kanthirava died in A.D. 1662"; Rao follows the palace chronicle.
`scholarship`.

**K§8i. Sri-Ranga sheltered.** HR I p. 206: "during the troublous years of Sri-Ranga (c. 1650-1653), afforded
him shelter and hospitality, denied to him by the other feudatories". `scholarship`.

**K§8j. Dasara.** WILKS I pp. 51–52: "for having first celebrated with suitable splendour the feast of the
Maha-noumi, or Dessara". HR I pp. 186–195 describe the Mahanavami of September 1647 from K.N.V. `scholarship`.

**K§8k. Prosperity verse.** EC III Intro p. 29 (Sr. 103, 1646): "The lord of the gods sent good rains, the
earth brought forth full fruit ... all the people were free from disease". `inscription`.

**K§8l. Tradition.** HR I p. 210: "his is a household name in Mysore whenever there is talk of chivalry,
exploit or piety." `scholarship`.

## K§9. His face and dress (for the sheet)

**K§9a. The statue** (`inscription`; the statue with its pedestal label). HR I p. 173 (citing M.A.R. 1919
p. 56 para 195):
> An authentic statue of Kanthirava, a Bhakta-vigraha : with his name in Kannada (Kanthirava-Narasaraja
> Wadayaravaru) inscribed on the pedestal : is to be seen in a room to the left of the Ranga-mantapa of
> the temple of Narasimhasvami at Seringapatam. It is a magnificent figure of Kanthirava-Narasaraja
> Wodeyar, about three and a half feet high, standing on a high pedestal with folded hands. Kanthirava is
> represented as wearing a long robe, girt with a sword, shield and dagger on the left side, and with
> large ear-rings and Vira-pendeya (hero's insignia) on the right foot. ... presenting in life-like
> fashion the majestic bearing of Kanthirava-Narasaraja Wodeyar as a warrior-devotee. A similar statue of
> his is to be seen in a pavilion of the temple of Trinayanesvara in the Mysore fort.
Plate XIX (HR I, facing p. 173), looked at in the page image: the figure in left profile, a tall rounded
head-dress with a knob curving forward at the top, a large round shield standing out at the left side,
a long robe falling to the ankles, the sword hanging at the left. The photograph is too dark for the face.

**K§9b. The miniature.** HR I Plate XVI (Commons File:Kanthirava.jpg, public domain; probably a later palace
copy): seated on a lion-footed throne-platform with a bolster, a broad deep-chested man with large wide
eyes, a thick black moustache swept out and up at the ends, no beard, a rounded turban bound with jewelled
bands and a tall plume or crest at the top, a large ring ear ornament, several pearl strings, a dark long
coat; an attendant with a fly-whisk behind.

**K§9c. Contemporary words.** HR I p. 204 (K.N.V. IX, X, XV):
