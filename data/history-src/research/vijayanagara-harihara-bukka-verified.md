and Story 5, §5, and on `vijayanagara-verified.md` §10 to §11. Everything below was read this session from
the editions named; nothing is from memory. Quotations are verbatim from the archive.org / Gutenberg OCR or
the ambuda.org transcription, with obvious OCR breakage silently repaired and diacritics simplified. The
originals' long dashes are rendered as " - " (house rule: no em dash). Each item: source and page or line,

`inscription` tier, a chronicle written two centuries later is `scholarship` at best). Downloaded texts are
kept in the session scratchpad, not in the repo. Anything not reached is marked UNVERIFIED.

## 1. The collapse of the old south (background)

### 1a. Kafur at Dorasamudra and Ma'bar, 1310 to 1311 (Barani, E&D III pp. 203 to 204)
> "Towards the end of the year 710 H. (1310 A.D.) the Sultan sent an army under Malik Naib Kafur against
> Dhur-samundar and Ma'bar ... At the first onslaught Billal Rai fell into the hands of the Muhammadans, and
> Dhur-samundar was captured. Thirty-six elephants, and all the treasures of the place, fell into the hands
> of the victors. ... Malik Naib Kafur marched on to Ma'bar, which he also took. He destroyed the golden idol
> temple (but-khanah-i zarin) of Ma'bar, and the golden idols which for ages had been worshipped by the
> Hindus of that country."
Tier: inscription (Barani, a Delhi courtier writing in the 1350s). Note: the Hoysala capital was not
destroyed for good: ASR 1907-08 p. 242 n.: "The destruction of the Hoysala capital Dhorasamudra in A.D. 1327
... could not have been anything like complete".

### 1b. 1327 and the Hoysala decline (ASR 1907-08 pp. 235 to 236)
> "The Hoysala kingdom ... received a severe blow from the invasion of Malik Kafur in A.D. 1310. Ballala III,
> the then ruling king, was first captured and subsequently released. But in A.D. 1327 Muhammad Tughlak
> appears to have made another attempt to annex the Hoysala dominions. The Hoysala power, in consequence,
> became much enfeebled and practically came to a close with the demolition of the capital Dhorasamudra by
> the Muhammadans in that year. Ballala III is, however, known to have ruled until A.D. 1342-43. In the
> latter part of his reign, Ballala changed his capital to Tiruvannamalai".
Tier: scholarship.

### 1c. Kampili (ಕಂಪಿಲಿ): the last stand (Ibn Battuta)
REHLA pp. 96 to 97 (copyright uncertain: short excerpts and paraphrase only). Paraphrase: the rebel
Baha-ud-din fled to "Rai Kampila", whose dominion was "an inaccessible mountainous territory". The Sultan's army
besieged him until his provisions were spent; the rai sent his guest on safely to another ruler with an escort,
had a great fire made, told his wives and daughters that those who wished might follow him, and they bowed to
him and threw themselves into it. Excerpt: "Then the rai washed himself, rubbed his body with sandal and put on
his arms, but no shield." With those of his people who chose to die with him he charged the royal army and
fought until all were killed. The army took the city; eleven of his sons were taken to the Sultan, "all of them
embraced Islam", and the Sultan made them amirs for their descent and their father's conduct; Ibn Battuta saw
three of them at court and became friends with one.
Public-domain version of the same story (use this wherever it carries the point): Sewell ch. 2, l. 748 to 760:
"He caused a huge fire to be lit on which his wives and the wives of his nobles, ministers, and principal men
immolated themselves, and this done he sallied forth with his followers to meet the invaders, and was slain.
The town was taken, 'and eleven sons of the Rai were made prisoners and carried to the Sultan, who made them
all Mussalmans.'" The sandal and the missing shield are only in REHLA.
The same in Sewell ch. 2, l. 735 to 760, who places Kampli "about eight miles east of Anegundi, which was
the citadel of the predecessors of the kings of Vijayanagar" and dates the raid to 1334 (l. 723). Mahdi
Husain's note (REHLA p. 96 n. 7) identifies the second host as "Ballala III". The rebel nephew's end (flayed,
paraded) is in REHLA p. 97 and Sewell l. 770 to 777: evidence page only.
Tier: inscription (Ibn Battuta was at the Delhi court and knew one of the sons; not an eyewitness of the
siege). **Date disputed**: Sewell 1334; ASR 1907-08 puts Tughluq's southern campaign in 1327. The book
gives no year for Kampili.
**Who the eleven sons were**: Ibn Battuta names three (Nasr, Bakhtiyar, the keeper of the seal) and no
Harihara or Bukka. The theory that the Sangama brothers were among Kampili's captives (or Warangal's) and
were converted and reconverted is disputed (§2c). Kept out of the prose.

### 1d. Ballala III (ಮೂರನೇ ಬಲ್ಲಾಳ) falls at Madurai
EC VI Kd 75 (translation): "the sons (named) of Baira-Gauda of Bidire set up this viragal for Kankaya, who in
the last Chitrabhanu year Asvija-suddha 8th, (= 8th Sept. 1342 A.D.), joining Ballala-Deva in the capture of
Beribi, smiting the army of the Turukas, fought in the field of battle, [and] along with the deva (? the
king) taking by force the world of gods, became a vira and fell fighting in the battle." Tier: inscription
(a Kannada hero stone).
REHLA p. 229 (Ibn Battuta, at Madura in 1343 as the Sultan's guest; short excerpt and paraphrase only):
the Sultan's men fell on the camp at the siesta hour, when the horses were out at pasture. Excerpt: "Their raja
attempted to mount a horse although he was eighty years of age." He was captured; the Sultan treated him with
apparent respect until he had taken all his riches, elephants and horses, then killed and skinned him, and the
skin, stuffed with straw, hung on the wall of Madura, where Ibn Battuta says he saw it.
Tier: inscription (near-contemporary witness).
MV 1957 concluding canto v. 25 (amb. p. 163): Kampana rejoiced in "an opponent like the Suratrana (Sultan)
who had by his valour reduced the Colas and Pandyas and despoiled the wealth of Vira Ballala (the Third)".
**Disputed**: date (Kd 75, 8 Sept 1342; Ibn Battuta's visit and other records point to 1343, see
`hoysala.md` §5) and place (Beribi; Ibn Battuta's Kubban). The book: "fell before Madurai", no year; the skin
on the wall is told in one plain sentence without detail and attributed on the evidence page.

## 2. The brothers

### 2a. Five sons of Sangama (ಸಂಗಮ)
EC VI Sg 1 (1346; §3a below) names "vira-Hariyapodeyar, Kampannodeyar, Bukkannodeyar, Marapodeyar,
Muddapodeyar". EC VI Kp 25 (dated 1404, a grant of Bukka II), translation: "In that line arose the king Yadu.
From whom was descended Vasudeva (Krishna) ... In his line was born the king Sangama; whose sons were
Harihara, Kampa, Bukka, Marapa and Muddapa. Of these five, Bukka was the chief, like Arjuna the middle
Pandava. As the point of his sword danced about, the Turushkas shrank up, the Konkana Sankaparya fled with
haste from the field of battle, the Andhras sought the shelter of caves ... rajadhiraja raja-paramesvara,
punisher of kings who break their word, terrifier of foreign kings, Suratrana of Hindu rayas, he was in
Vijayanagari which he had made his capital. His queen was Gaurambika, and their son was Harihara". Rice's
Introduction p. 22 repeats this. ASR 1907-08 p. 239: "Samgama's sons are stated each in turn to have
conquered the Muhammadans (Turushkas), which shows that in the early stage of Vijayanagara history there
was continuous struggle with the intruders." Tier: inscription (the 1404 praise is the family's own claim,
sixty years on; "like Arjuna" is panegyric). Order of the brothers: Harihara first, Bukka third (Kp 25; EC VI
Intro p. 24: "Hakka and Bukka, the first and third sons of Sangama").

### 2b. Lords, not kings
Sewell ch. 3, l. 952 to 967: "The first rulers of Vijayanagar, however, did not dare to call themselves
kings ... The inscription referred to of Harihara in 1340 calls him 'Hariyappa VODEYA,' ... and the latter
definitely entitling him to rank only as a chieftain. Moreover, the Sanskrit title given him is
MAHAMANDALESVARA, which may be translated 'great lord' - not king. And the same is the case with his
successor, Bukka, in two inscriptions, one of which is dated in 1353." The 1368 edict (§5a) still calls Bukka
"maha-mandalesvara". ASR 1907-08 p. 242: Harihara II "was the first to assume the imperial titles
Maharajadhiraja, Rajaparamesvara, etc., and to establish himself on the throne of Vijayanagara." Tier:
inscription (titles in the records) + scholarship. (Kp 25's "rajadhiraja" for Bukka is a 1404 record looking
back.)

### 2c. Where they came from: disputed (kept out of the prose)
- Hoysala service: EC VI Intro p. 22 (on Mg 25): "there is reason to suppose that the founders of the
  Vijayanagar dynasty may have been chiefs under the Hoysalas." ASR 1907-08 p. 236: "his feudatory chiefs
  Harihara I and Bukka I. It is not ascertained as yet from epigraphical records what definite position these
  two chiefs held under the Hoysalas". Tier: scholarship.
- Warangal, captivity and conversion: Sewell ch. 2, l. 858 to 875: "One has it that two brothers named Bukka
  and Harihara, who had been in the service of the king of Warangal ... escaped ... being accompanied in their
  flight by the Brahman Madhava"; "Another states that the two brothers were officers in the service of the
  Muhammadan governor of Warangal"; Sewell's own blend (l. 920 to 937): "two brothers, Hindus of the Kuruba
  caste ... serving in the treasury of the king of Warangal". The conversion version is in the *Vidyaranya
  Sikka* shown to Buchanan in 1801 (Sewell n. 29, l. 11739 ff.). Tier: legend (texts two to four centuries
  after the events).
- Kuruba shepherd (Couto, Sewell l. 885 to 899); Kadamba descent (Nikitin, Sewell l. 905 to 908): legend.
**Book rule**: the prose says only that they were the five sons of Sangama and lords on the Tungabhadra.
Every origin theory and the captivity-and-conversion story are on the evidence page (f-origin).

### 2d. The first records: Badami 1340 and the coast
ASR 1907-08 p. 240: "Chameya-nayaka built the fort at Badami in Saka 1261 under the orders of Harihara. As
Harihara was only a mahamandalesvara and had not established himself as a sovereign in his newly acquired
kingdom, we do not find in the records of his time any reference to a capital town from which he would have
ruled." p. 236: "about A.D. 1340 they had acquired sufficient importance to build forts and attract the
notice of foreign travellers"; a fort at Barakuru "by Harihara I" (South Canara Manual). Sewell l. 965 to
967: "a fort was built in that year at Badami by permission of Harihara." Tier: inscription (via ASR; Ind.
Ant. X p. 63).
Ibn Battuta's "Haryab" (REHLA p. 179): the Sultan of Hinawr (Honavar) "is subordinate to a heathen raja
named Haryab". ASR 1907-08 p. 236 reads him as "Hariyappa (Harihara I)"; Mahdi Husain (REHLA p. 179 n. 1)
reads "Bilal Deo, the raja of Dwarsamudra". Disputed; not used in the prose.

### 2e. The division of the land (ASR 1907-08 pp. 237 to 239)
> "The joint donation by the five brothers further indicates the undivided interest which they had in the
> building up of the new kingdom. Bukka I was apparently governing the eastern and central divisions of the
> Hoysala country, while Harihara I was in charge of the western and portions at least of the southern
> districts ... Kampana I was ruling over the Nellore and Cuddapah districts, and Marapa, the Shimoga and
> North-Canara districts."
Marapa "conquered the whole of the Kadamba or Banavasi 12,000 country ... and was residing at Chandragutti in
Saka 1268". "A record from the Bangalore district, dated in Saka 1268, is explicit in stating that
Ariyappa-Udaiyar (= Harihara I) and Bukkanna-Udaiyar were ruling together" (p. 240). Tier: scholarship from
inscriptions.

## 3. The two seas: Sringeri, 1346

### 3a. EC VI Sg 1 (Sringeri Jagir 1), translation
> "Obeisance to Vidyatirtha-guru ... Praise of Sambhu.
> Harihara having conquered the earth from the eastern to the western ocean, in order to celebrate the
> festival of his victory, made an excellent grant.
> Be it well. (On the date specified), the maha-mandalesvara, subduer of hostile kings, champion over kings
> who break their word, vira-Hariyapodeyar, Kampannodeyar, Bukannodeyar, Marapodeyar, Muddapodeyar,
> son-in-law Ballappa-dannayaka, Kumara Sovannodeyar and others, made, to Bharati-tirtha-sripada, his
> disciples and others, and the forty Brahmans residing in that tirtha of Sringeri, for the performance of
> rites and service, a grant on a stone sasana as follows: - Nine villages (named) ... we, of our own will,
> have given ... with pouring of water."
Kannada/Sanskrit text: "purvapara-samudrantam jitva Hariharo bhuvam | jayotsavam khyapayitum akarod
dharmmam uttamam ||"; date "jayabhyudaya-Saka-varusha 1268 neya Parthiva-samvatsarada Phalguna-ba 1
Guruvara". The text heading: "In Sringeri, on a stone attached to the southern wall of the [name garbled in
the OCR] temple." Rice's Introduction p. 21: "The earliest grant of the Vijayanagar kings in this volume
(and one of the earliest known) is Sg. 1 of 1346. Harihara, in order to celebrate the festival of the
victorious establishment of his empire from the eastern to the western ocean, made a grant for the matha of
Sringeri, together with his brothers and other relatives, of nine villages". ASR 1907-08 p. 236 dates it
"Saka-Samvat 1268 = A.D. 1346-47" and calls the donors "the five brothers". Tier: inscription.
Notes: (1) Harihara is still *maha-mandalesvara*. (2) The donee is Bharati-tirtha, not Vidyaranya (ASR p. 237
n. 1: "It is not stated who Bharatitirtha was"; he may be Bharati-Krishnatirtha, Vidyaranya's predecessor).
(3) The appended line names a "Pandya-chakravarti ... Kikkayitayi" granting a further village; the
translation omits it; its link to the Hoysala queen claimed in modern books is not checked. Not used.
(4) That the brothers stood at Sringeri in person is not stated: the book's scene is dramatised.

### 3b. Vidyaranya (ವಿದ್ಯಾರಣ್ಯ) and the year 1336
- EC VI Intro p. 24 (Rice): "Madhava or Vidyaranya ... who was instrumental in founding the Vijayanagar
  empire in 1336, was the head of the establishment at that time (see Sg. 11)." Intro p. 25: "Sg. 11 and 13,
  of 1652, contain a summary of the history of the matha". "In Kp. 30 we have a grant made in 1378 by order of
  Vidyaranya-Sripada." So the earliest record in this volume naming Vidyaranya as a Sringeri guru is 1378;
  the founding role rests on a 1652 matha record.
- ASR 1907-08 p. 239: "Some suspicious copper plate records ... call him a chief of Kunjarakona - the modern
  Anegondi - attribute to him the foundation of the city of Vijayanagara with the help and advice of
  Vidyaranya who was then performing penance in the temple at Hampi and give the date Saka-Samvat 1258 for
  his coronation." Footnote: "As the traditional date of the foundation of Vijayanagara is also believed to be
  A.D. 1336 (i.e., Saka 1258), it is not improbable that Harihara I in this year actually made an attempt to
  declare his independence ... though the records on them may not be beyond suspicion."
- Sewell l. 718 to 724: "generally supposed to have been founded in the year 1336, and that that date is not
  far from the truth may be gathered from two facts" (the 1340 record; Ibn Battuta's raid).
Tier: 1336 = scholarship (traditional, disputed); Vidyaranya's founding role = legend.

### 3c. The hare and the hounds (Nuniz, c. 1535; Sewell l. 8768 to 8800)
> "The King going one day a-hunting ... to a mountain on the other side of the river of Nagumdym, where now
> is the city of Bisnaga ... a hare rose up before him, which, instead of fleeing from the dogs, ran towards
> them and bit them all, so that none of them dared go near it ... And arriving at the river, he met a hermit
> who was walking along the bank ... the hermit said that the King ought in that place to erect houses in
> which he could dwell, and build a city, for the prodigy meant that this would be the strongest city in the
> world, and that it would never be captured by his enemies ... And he gave it the name Vydiajuna, for so the
> hermit called himself".
Nuniz's founder is "Deorao", raised by the Sultan, not named Harihara. Sewell n. 27 (l. 11730): "This tale
is told of the rise of almost every kingdom". Sewell n. 28 (l. 11734): the name was "City of Victory", not
"City of Learning". Tier: legend (recorded two centuries after). The book tells it once as a tale the people
told ("ಅಂತೆ"), unnamed hermit, and never pictures it.

### 3d. The site
Sewell l. 742 to 746: Kampli "a fortified place about eight miles east of Anegundi ... backed by the mass of
rocky hills in the centre of which the great city was afterwards situated". l. 822 to 831: the hermit's
rock above the Hampi temple, "nothing but Nature in one of her wildest moods - lofty hills near and far,
formed almost entirely of huge tumbled boulders of granite ... separated by the river from the mere inhabited
country on the farther side, where dwelt the chiefs of Anegundi". l. 976 to 981: the temple at Hampe "is the
only one among the massive shrines erected at the capital in which worship is still carried on". Kp 25 (1404):
a grant "on the bank of the Tungabhadra, in the presence of Virupaksha". Tier: scholarship / inscription.

## 4. Bukka alone (c. 1355 to 1377)

### 4a. Harihara's end and the throne
ASR 1907-08 p. 240: "We do not know when Bukka become the sole ruler; or in other words, when Harihara died.
As, however, the latest date known for the latter is Saka 1276, we may suppose that the event happened some
time after that date". Bukka "is said to have had his capital at Dhorasamudra in Saka-Samvat 1274 and to
have thence changed it to Hosapattana and to Vijayanagara (Vidyanagara)" (p. 240); n.: "A record from the
Pavagada taluka ... dated in Saka 1277 states, however, that Bukka was ruling from his 'jewelled throne' at
Vidyanagara ... Hence the change of capital from Hosapattana to Vidyanagara must have happened about the end
of Saka 1277." p. 237: "the town of Vijayanagara, newly constructed by him". Tier: scholarship from records
(EC XII Pg 74).
**Succession disputed**: Sewell l. 1024 to 1046: Nellore copper plates (ed. Krishna Sastri) make Kampa succeed
Harihara and Kampa's son Sangama II rule in 1356; "The only possible inference is that the succession to
Harihara was disputed, and that somehow Bukka got the upper hand". Sewell's c. 1343 death for Harihara is
superseded by the Saka 1276 record. Kept to the evidence page.

### 4b. "Sultan among Hindu kings" (EC VI Mg 25, 1359)
> "(On the date specified), when the maha-mandalesvara, subduer of hostile kings, punisher of kings who break
> their word, suratana of the Hindu Rayas, master of the eastern and western oceans, vira-Bukkanna-Vodeyar was
> ruling a peaceful kingdom: - that Bukkanna-Vodeyar's senior [councillor] Teppada-Naganna-Vodeyar and
> Tippanna-Vodeyar ... unwilling to give up Sosaviru, situated in the Kali ... Four-thousand".
Rice Intro p. 22: "Sosavur (the birthplace of the Hoysalas)". Tier: inscription (defaced; Rice: "the
inscription is very much defaced").

### 4c. The Bahmani sultanate (Sewell l. 1080 to 1085, from Ferishta)
"on Friday the 24th Rabi-al-akhir A.H. 748, according to Firishtah, a date which corresponds to Friday,
August 3, A.D. 1347, Ala-ud-din Bahmani was crowned sovereign of the Dakhan at Kulbarga". Tier: scholarship
(Ferishta c. 1600).

### 4d. The war of 1366 (Ferishta, via Sewell ch. 3, l. 1166 to 1380)
Ferishta wrote about 230 years later, at a sultan's court, and calls the king "Kishen Roy"; Sewell (l. 1270 to
1274): "there can be no doubt that his real name was Bukka. The historian collected his information more than
two hundred years after these events, and often misnamed the Hindu kings". Key lines:
- l. 1191 to 1205: the Sultan's drunken draft on "the treasury of the roy of Beejanuggur" for his musicians;
  the roy "placed the presenter of the draft on an ass's back, and, parading him through all the quarters of
  Beejanuggur, sent him back"; he marched to "the vicinity of the fortress of Oodnee".
- l. 1207 to 1214: "Mudkal was captured, and all the inhabitants, men, women, and children, put to the sword.
  One man only escaped and carried the news to Kulbarga."
- l. 1222 to 1226: the Sultan's oath "that till he should have put to death one hundred thousand infidels, as
  an expiation for the massacre of the faithful, he would never sheathe the sword".
- l. 1290 to 1300: the battle "On the 14th of Zeekaud (A.H. 767, or Thursday, July 23, A.D. 1366) ... From the
  dawn till four in the afternoon, like the waves of the ocean, they continued in warm conflict"; the Hindu
  commander mortally wounded; the centre broke; the Sultan "gave orders to renew the massacre".
- l. 1338 to 1340: "the Sultan besieged Vijayanagar in vain for a month, and then retreated across the
  Tungabhadra, harassed at every step by masses of the Hindus from the city".
- l. 1343 to 1352: a night attack; "ten thousand of his troops being slain"; the Sultan "commanded the
  inhabitants of every place round Beejanuggur to be massacred without mercy". (Ferishta also blames the
  king's wine and dancers: a hostile detail, omitted from the prose under the dignity rule.)
- l. 1360 to 1376, the peace: the ambassadors "pleaded that no religion ordained that the innocent, and
  particularly helpless women and children, should suffer for the guilty: 'If Kishen Roy had been faulty, the
  poor and wretched had not been partakers in his crimes.' ... 'Mahummud Shaw was struck by their remarks, and
  took an oath that he would not in future put to death a single enemy after victory, and would bind his
  successors to observe the same lenity.'" Sewell: "For some years, no doubt, the promise was fulfilled".
- l. 1378 to 1380: "Thus ended the war, and for some years there was peace between Vijayanagar and Kulbarga."
- l. 1244 and n. 49 (l. 11828): "The Sultan had with him a train of artillery"; n. 49: "Briggs (ii. 312, n.)
  considers it unlikely that the armies could have possessed artillery at so early a date." Not used; no guns
  in any picture.
- n. 51 (l. 11833): the fallen commander is "Bhoj-Mul" in Briggs. Not named in the book.
Tier: scholarship (late, hostile chronicle). Numbers ("half a million") are not counts. The book tells the war
plainly (Mudkal included), with Bukka's loss and the envoys' plea, and omits the wine.

### 4e. 1375 to 1378 (Sewell l. 1386 to 1580): Mujahid's war, Bukka in the Sandur hills, the attack on the city
and its temple, the Raya's brother's relief army, the failed nine-month siege of Adoni; then Bukka invests
Raichur in 1378. Same source; not used in the prose (it would crowd the climax); evidence page only.

## 5. The settlement of 1368 (EC II 1923 No. 344, old No. 136)

### 5a. Translation (pp. 146 to 147)
> "Be it well. Victorious is the possessor of all titles ... Ramanuja, king of the kings of ascetics.
> (On the date specified), during the time that the maha-mandalesvara, punisher of hostile kings, champion
> over kings who break their word, Sri-vira-Bukka-Raya was ruling the earth, - dispute having arisen between
> the Jainas and the bhaktas (Vaishnavas), the blessed people (the Jainas) of all the nadus including
> Aneyagondi, Hosapattana, Penugunde and the city of Kalleha having made petition to Bukka-Raya about the
> injustice done by the bhaktas, - the king, taking the hand of the Jainas and placing it in the hand of the
> Srivaishnavas of the eighteen nadus including all the acharyas of the places the chief of which are
> Kovil, Tirumale, Perumal-kovil and Tirunarayanapura; all the samayis; all the satvikas; mushtikas; those of
> the holy service, of the holy feet and of the (holy) water; the forty-eight people; the savanta-bovas; and
> the Tirukula and Jambavakula, - and declaring (at the same time) that there was no difference between the
> Vaishnava darsana (or faith) and the Jaina darsana, (decreed as follows): -
> This Jaina darsana is, as before, entitled to the five great musical instruments and the kalasa (or vase).
> If loss or advancement should be caused to the Jaina darsana through the bhaktas, the Vaishnavas will
> kindly deem it as loss or advancement caused to their (own darsana). The Srivaishnavas will to this effect
> kindly set up a sasana in all the bastis of the kingdom. For as long as the sun and moon endure the
> Vaishnava creed will continue to protect the Jaina darsana. The Vaishnavas and the Jainas are one (body):
> they must not be viewed as different.
> Tatayya of Tirumale, by consent of the blessed people (the Jainas) of the whole kingdom, will, out of the
> money levied at the rate of one hana a year for every house according to the door from the Jainas
> throughout the whole kingdom for the bodyguard to be appointed by the Vaishnavas at the holy place Belugula,
> appoint twenty servants as a bodyguard for the god, and with the remainder of the money have the
> dilapidated Jinalayas (or Jina temples) whitewashed. In this manner, for as long as the sun and moon last,
> will they without failure pay every year and acquire fame and merit. He who transgresses this rule shall be
> a traitor to the king, a traitor to the sangha and the samudaya. ...
> Busuvi-setti, the good son of Harvi-setti of Kalleha, having made petition to Bukka-Raya, sent for Tatayya
> of Tirumale and had (? the sasana) renovated. And both the samayas (creeds) uniting bestowed the dignity of
> Sangha-nayaka on Busuvi-setti."
Footnotes (p. 146): Kovil = Srirangam; Tirumale = Tirupati; Perumal-kovil = Conjeevaram; Tirunarayanapura =
Melkote; "Tirukula and Jambavakula" = "The Holeyas and Madigas"; Kalleha = "Kalya in the Magadi Taluk of the
Bangalore District where there is another version (Magadi 18) of this inscription". Location: "At
Bhandari-basti, on the first stone on the east side."
Kannada text (text pp. 159 to 160): "Saka-varsha 1290 neya Kilaka-samvatsarada Bhadrapada-su 10 Bri ... Maharayanu
Vaishnava-darsanakkam Jaina-darsanakkam bhedav illav endu Rayanu Vaishnavara kaiyyalu Jainara kai-vididu
kottu ... Vaishnavaru Jainaru vondu bhedavagi kanal agadu".
Introduction pp. 63 to 64: "The earliest of the records relating to Vijayanagar is 344 (136) ... it refers to a
reconciliation brought about by king Bukka-Raya I between the Jainas and the Vaishnavas in 1368."
ASR 1907-08 p. 241 cites the same record from Rice's 1889 edition (Translations p. 180) under "Saka 1289";
the stone says Saka 1290 Kilaka. Tier: inscription. Date: Bhadrapada su. 10 of Kilaka, Saka 1290 = 1368
(August or September; the day is not converted here).
What the record does NOT say: where the king sat, how many came, what the "injustice" was. The book's hall,
crowd and gestures beyond the joined hands are dramatised.

## 6. Kumara Kampana (ಕುಮಾರ ಕಂಪಣ) and Madurai

### 6a. The poem and its author
MV 1957 Preface: "The Madhuravijayam or Vira Kamparaya caritam by Ganga Devi was discovered by accident
amidst a heap of wornout palm-leaf manuscripts ... The original is in grantha characters and is full of
errors. The manuscript, as discovered, was incomplete, with ten leaves missing in the middle". Introduction
(amb. p. 28): "the story of the conquest of Madurai by Kumara Kampana told by his wife Ganga Devi". Canto 1
gives Bukka's queen as "Devayi". Tier: inscription (contemporary court poem by Kampana's wife; poetic
conventions).

### 6b. The father's counsel (Canto III, amb. pp. 118 to 120)
MV 1957 translation (copyright uncertain: short excerpts and paraphrase only). Paraphrase of vv. 32, 41, 43, 45:
subjects are the wealth of kings, so no wise king terrorises them with brutal punishments; excerpt (v. 32):
"Nobody would go up a tree and apply the axe to its roots." Go to Tondaimandalam, overcome the Sambuvaraya,
establish yourself at Kanchi and rule with regard to the people's wishes (v. 41); the Turushka of Madurai acts
like Ravana towards the southern kingdoms (v. 43). Excerpt (v. 45): "Then the king took off invaluable ornaments
from his own person and adorned the prince with them."

### 6c. Kanchi
Canto IV (summary, MV 1957 Intro amb. p. 29 and p. 88): camp at Mulbagal, siege of Padaividu, "the death of the
Sambuvaraya king at the hands of Kampana"; "Having thus killed the Sambuvaraya in the field of battle, King
Kampana received the decree of his father that he should rule the conquered territory ... he inaugurated a
just and prosperous rule over Tondaimandalam". ASR 1907-08 p. 241: Kampana "deputed about Saka 1283 to reduce
the southern dominions and to subdue the Mussalmans of Madura"; n.: "The earliest date for Kampana available
from Tamil records is Saka 1283 ... and the latest, 1296". Tier: inscription (poem) + scholarship.

### 6d. The lament of Madurai (Canto VIII, amb. pp. 155 to 156)
Paraphrase: at Srirangam the serpent shields the sleeping Hari from falling brick with his hood; the Kaveri,
without bunds, has strayed from her course; coconut groves are replaced by iron spikes hung with skulls; the
speaker is grieved more by parrots taught Persian than by owls in ruined groves. Excerpt: "Those temples which
were once resonant with the sounds of mridanga drums are now echoing the fearful howls of jackals."
Spoken by a goddess who gives him a sword (Agastya's); MV 1957 Intro (amb. p. 30): "The appearance of the
goddess was a poetic convention." Tier: inscription (as a poem's picture; hostile to the sultanate; poetic).

### 6e. The battle (Concluding canto vv. 21 to 40, amb. pp. 163 to 166)
Paraphrase: when his forces were routed, the Yavana king strung his bow and met Kampa in single combat (v. 21),
riding a fast horse with a jewelled tuft (v. 23). Kampa destroyed the Sultan's "crow banner" (v. 30), cut his
bowstring with an arrow (v. 31); the Sultan drew the sword hanging at his saddle (v. 32); Kampa, "the glory of
the Karnata race", avoided the blow and cut off his head (v. 35). Kampa was astonished to see the headless trunk
still holding the reins with one hand and raising the other to strike back (v. 37). Excerpt (v. 40): "King
Kamparaya guaranteed the safety of the remaining men in his enemy ranks".
MV 1957 Intro (amb. p. 74): "it is very much to be doubted if there was even a duel at all though the final
result, i.e., victory of Kampana is beyond dispute"; the Ramabhyudayam gives the duel to Saluva Mangu.
Tier: inscription (poem), duel **disputed**.

### 6f. The Delhi report (Afif, E&D III p. 339)
> "Kurbat Hasan Kangu was king in Ma'bar. ... A neighbouring chief, named Bakan, at the head of a body of men
> and elephants, marched into Ma'bar and made Kurbat Hasan Kangu prisoner. He made himself master of all
> Ma'bar, which had belonged to Muhammadans; their women suffered violence and captivity in the hands of
> Hindus, and Bakan established himself as ruler of Ma'bar."
Tier: inscription (Delhi chronicle of Firoz Shah's reign). Note: "prisoner" against the poem's "killed"; the
cruelty to women is told once, plainly, in the prose.

### 6g. Ranganatha comes home (EI VI No. 33, Saka 1293)
> "Hail! Prosperity! In the Saka year (expressed by the chronogram) bandhupriya (i.e. Saka-Samvat 1293).
> (Verse 1.) Having brought (the god) from the Anjanadri (mountain) ... having worshipped (him) at Chenchi for
> some time, then having slain the Tulushkas whose bows were raised, - Goppanarya, the mirror of fame, placing
> Ranganatha together with both Lakshmi and the Earth in his own town, again duly performed excellent worship.
> (V. 2.) Having carried Rangaraja, the lord of the world, from the slope of the Vrishabhagiri (mountain) to his
> capital, having slain by his army the proud Tulushka soldiers, having made the site of Sriranga united with
> the golden age (Kritayuga), and having placed there this (god) ... the Brahmana Gopana duly performs ... the
> worship".
Hultzsch's introduction: "Goppana or Goppanna is known as an officer of Kampana-Udaiyar". Tier: inscription.
Saka 1293 = 1371-72.

### 6h. The end of the Madurai sultanate
MV 1957 Intro (amb. p. 76, printed p. 55): "So the Muslim rule ended for ever in Madhurai in 1371, with its
destruction by Vijayanagar." Appendix (amb. p. 77): Sultans of Madurai 1334 to 1371, the last "Qurbat Hassan
Kangu". Tier: scholarship.

## 7. The end

ASR 1907-08 p. 242: "A copper-plate record from Yedatore gives the date of Bukka's death as Saka 1298, Nala,
with astronomical details. Harihara II, the son of Bukka, must have succeeded in Saka 1298-9. He was the first
to assume the imperial titles Maharajadhiraja, Rajaparamesvara, etc., and to establish himself on the throne of
Vijayanagara. The kingdom in his time seems to have reached the utmost limits". Harihara II's mother:
"Bukka I. was succeeded by Harihara II., his son by his wife Gauri" (Sewell l. 1583 to 1584); Kp 25
"Gaurambika". Tier: scholarship from records (EC IV Yd 46). Saka 1298 Nala = 1376-77.
Kampana's latest record Saka 1296 (1374-75) (§6c); his death year is therefore before or about 1375, before
his father's. The book says only that the son died before the father.

### 7b. Two more centuries (Sewell)
Sewell l. 303 to 304: "when the empire fell in 1565, the prosperity of Portuguese Goa fell with it never to rise
again." l. 6120: "On the following day, Tuesday, January 23; 1565, ... a pitched battle took place". l. 986 to
990: "the great temple at Hampe close to the river, which is still in full preservation and is the only one
among the massive shrines erected at the capital in which worship is still carried on; the others were
remorselessly wrecked and destroyed by the Muhammadans in 1565." (Sewell's claim in the same sentence that
the first king built this temple for the hermit is his conjecture; not used.) Tier: scholarship. The book
says only that the city stood for more than two centuries after its founders.

## 8. Visual record

- **No portrait**: no sculpture, painting or coin portrait of Harihara I or Bukka I is known (Commons
  categories empty, checked 2026-11-08). Both faces are invented and disclosed.
- **Coins**: Commons "HariHara I coin.png" and "Bukkaraya I Coin.png" (PD; attribution by the numista
  catalogue, not checked): tiny thick gold pieces, a striding figure on one face (a standing figure with a
  raised hand on the other coin) and a short Kannada-script legend between lines. No king's face. Use only
  as props (a handful of small gold coins), never as an emblem.
- **Titles in stone** (for set dressing of inscriptions, never legible): Sg 1 is "a grant on a stone sasana",
  Kannada in Old Kannada letters; the 1368 stone at the Bhandari basti, Shravanabelagola.
- **Arms of 1370** (MV concl. canto): bows, swords hung at the saddle, horses, elephants with riders in "coat of
  mail" (v. 13 per `vijayanagara.md` §6); the Madurai Sultan's "crow banner" (v. 30) and "jewelled tuft" (v. 23).
- **Kampili**: the rai "rubbed his body with sandal and put on his arms, but no shield" (REHLA p. 97).
- **Hoysala-era dress and arms for the 1340s** (`hoysala.md` §6, from the Halebidu friezes and hero stones):
  bare torso, dhoti, heavy ear-rings, straight swords, round shields, bows; horsemen with saddles and
  stirrups; elephants. The tall crowns of Hoysala kings are NOT given to the brothers, who were not kings.
- **Delhi armies of 1311 to 1340** (`hoysala.md` §6): early Sultanate mail, conical helmets, composite bows; no
  Mughal dress, no guns.
- **The site**: Sewell l. 822 to 831 (boulder hills, the river, the old shrine). The great Virupaksha gopura,
  the Vitthala temple, the stone chariot, the Mahanavami platform and the Lotus Mahal are all later than this
  story and must not appear.
