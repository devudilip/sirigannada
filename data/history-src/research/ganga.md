## 1. Timeline and rulers (dates with source)

Dating caveat (scholarship): the early Ganga kings are dated by regnal years in copper plates, many
of which Fleet declared spurious (kūṭa-śāsana); documentary evidence becomes reliable only from
Avinita's accession (c. 469) onward. Source: Shastriya Kannada database, "Gangas of Talakadu",
https://shastriyakannada.org/database/english/land%20history%20and%20people/GANGAS%20OF%20TALAKADU%20HTML.htm
(citing Fleet, Krishna Rao 1936, Sheik Ali 1976). Rice's own 1889 table (EC II 1889 pp. 25–27 of
the Introduction) starts Harivarma at "247 A.D.", a chronology now abandoned; use Rice only for the
10th-century kings, where he had dated inscriptions.

Anchor dates that rest directly on dated records (tier: inscription):
- Hebbata copper-plate grant (Srinivaspur taluk, Kolar dt.), "thirty-first regnal year" of Durvinita,
  five plates on a ring with the Ganga elephant seal; described by S. Srikanta Sastri as "one of the
  earliest genuine Ganga grants". Source: Srikanta Sastri, *Early Gangas of Talakad (Hebbata Grant
  of Durvinita)*, 1952, https://www.srikanta-sastri.org/early-gangas-of-talakad
- Penukonda plates of Madhava (II/III): Fleet accepted them as genuine, c. 475; a later edition
  dates them 495. Edition: EI XIV pp. 331–339 (Rice). The Sasanakota plates are argued to be
  earlier still. Source: dharmalekha.info/texts/INSEI14_24 ; ARE 1937–38 report (whatisindia.com).
  Disputed (475 vs 495).
- Salem plates of Sripurusha, dated 771. Source: Ramesh in Adiga 2006 p. 116 (via Wikipedia).
- Javali inscription: Sripurusha "ruled for 62 years". Source: Wikipedia "Sripurusha" (uncited
  there; treat as scholarship pending the EC reference).
- Doddahundi nishidhi (Mysore dt.), undated; Fleet 840 (palaeography 800–860), others 869.
  EI VI pp. 40–45. https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol6_1900-01/fleet2.html
- Begur hero stone of Ereyappa, Fleet dates "c. 934–938", cannot be earlier than 906–907. EI VI
  pp. 45–50. https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol6_1900-01/fleet9.html
- Atakur stone: "Śaka 872 current, Saumya saṁvatsara = A.D. 949–50", and "the seventh year of the
  tying of the fillet (paṭṭa)" of Butuga. EI VI pp. 50–57 (fleet11–18).
- Karya inscription "Śaka 890 … 5th year of the reign" of Satyavakya Permanadi (Marasimha); Melagani
  inscription Śaka 896 says he died then: hence Marasimha reigned Śaka 885–896 = 963–974. Rice,
  EC II 1889 Intro p. 18 (archive text lines ~4206–4216), quoting both Kannada texts in footnotes.
- Kuge Brahmadeva pillar (Rice no. 38; 1923 no. 59): undated, but a later addition says Marasimha
  "lived for one year after it was inscribed"; Rice fixes it at Śaka 895 = 973 and his death at 974.
  Later writers give 975 (Mulgund record, Aug. 975, may imply he was still alive in retirement at
  Bankapur). Disputed 974/975. Sources: Rice ibid.; EI V p. 151 ff. (Mulgund) via Wikipedia
  "Marasimha II Satyavakya".
- Pegga-Ur (Coorg) inscription, Śaka 899 expired, Isvara year = Phalguna 978: "Rakkasa" governing a
  district, called younger brother of Rachamalla (query mark in the edition). Source: EI VI
  (Fleet, Coorg records) via WebSearch summary of EI VII index p. 329 — verify before use.
- Chavundaraya Purana colophon: "Śaka 900, the year Īśvara (A.D. 978)". Rice, EC II 1889 Intro
  p. 22 (archive text line ~4504). Tier: inscription-grade (author's colophon in a dated MS
  tradition).
- Gommateshwara base inscriptions: undated. Rice's reasoning (EC II 1889 p. 22): after 978
  (Purana silent on the statue) and before 984 (end of Rachamalla's reign in Rice's data) → "Śaka
  905 or A.D. 983". Hultzsch (EI VII p. 108) restates Rice's window as "A.D. 977–984". Modern Jain
  scholarship computes the traditional calendar data (Vibhava year, Chaitra śukla 5, Sunday,
  Mrigaśira, Kumbha lagna) to Sunday 13 March 981; "now generally accepted" per jainworld.com
  (Sangave, *The Sacred Sravana Belagola*). Disputed: 981 / 983.
- End of the dynasty: Rajaraja Chola I's Tanjavur inscription lists "Gangapadi" among conquered
  territories; Talakad renamed Rajarajapura; conventional date 1004 (Wikipedia "Gangavadi";
  Wikipedia "Talakadu"). Tier: inscription for the conquest list; scholarship for "1004".

Capitals (scholarship): Kuvalala/Kolar (ಕುವಲಾಲ / ಕೋಲಾರ) first; Talakad = Talavanapura /
Dalavanapura (ತಲಕಾಡು) from Avinita's time or earlier (Harivarma's Kudalur grant names him resident
at Dalavanapura); Manne = Manyapura (ಮಣ್ಣೆ, Nelamangala taluk) regional capital under Sripurusha
and Shivamara II. Sources: Shastriya Kannada db; Wikipedia "Talakadu" (citing older Mysore gazetteer
material); the Atakur inscription itself styles Butuga "Koḷāla-puravarēśvara, Nandagiri-nātha" =
lord of Kolar the best of cities, lord of Nandagiri (Nandi hills) — Rice EC II 1889 p. 19 n.,
quoting the Kannada. Tier: inscription for the titles.

Suzerains: Pallavas (early), Badami Chalukyas (Shivamara I "under the suzerainty of the Badami
Chalukyas"), Rashtrakutas (from Sripurusha/Shivamara II onward until 973), then Cholas. Sources:
Wikipedia "Shivamara I"; Shastriya Kannada db. Tier: scholarship.

## 2. Primary sources: what survives, where to read, licence

### 2a. Stone inscriptions at Shravanabelagola (ಶ್ರವಣಬೆಳಗೊಳ) — the core of the Chavundaraya story
Edition: EC II 1889 (Rice) — PD, archive.org as above; EC II 1923 (Narasimhachar) — PD in India,
archive.org scan. Key records (Rice 1889 numbers; translations are Rice's, PD):
- Nos. 75 and 76 (1923: 175–176). "At foot of the colossus of Gommatesvara", engraved on the rock
  cut as an ant-hill "immediately below its right and left hands". Text as printed by Rice (EC II
  1889 p. 62 text; Intro p. 22):
  - On the left, in Nagari characters: `Śrī-Chāvuṇḍa-Rājeṁ karaviyale` ("Śrī Chāvuṇḍarāja caused
    [it] to be made") and below it `Śrī-Gaṅga-Rājeṁ suttāle karaviyale` ("Śrī Gaṅgarāja caused the
    enclosure to be made").
  - On the right: in Pūrvada Haḷe-Kannada characters `Śrī-Chāmuṇḍa-Rāja madisidaṁ`; in Grantha and
    Tamil characters `Śrī-Chāmuṇḍa-Rājar [seyvitta]`; in Haḷe-Kannada `Śrī-Gaṅga-Rāja suttālayavaṁ
    madisidaṁ`.
  Hultzsch (EI VII pp. 108–109, nos. 52a–b of 1902) re-edited them: lines in Kannada, a Tamil
  translation in Grantha + Vatteluttu, and the Nagari lines in **Marathi** — "the Marathi may have
  been chosen for Jaina pilgrims from the Maratha country"; he suspects both Nagari lines were cut
  in Gangaraja's time (c. 1116) since they share one letter-type. Rice had taken the first Nagari
  line as contemporary with Chavundaraya. Disputed: is the Marathi line 10th- or 12th-century?
  Some popular pages call the Nagari line "the oldest Konkani" — no edition says so; avoid.
  Tier: inscription. Rice's remark (Intro p. 22): "it is impossible not to admire the original
  grandeur of their simplicity … the bold sculptor of which has not so much as left a record of his
  name."
- No. 77: praise-verses on the rim of the lotus pedestal, same period.
- No. 109 (1923: 281?) — Tyagada Brahmadeva pillar (ತ್ಯಾಗದ ಬ್ರಹ್ಮದೇವ ಕಂಬ), Vindhyagiri. Chavundaraya's
  own eulogy, only one face surviving: "The remaining three sides … appear to have been defaced in
  order to inscribe No. 110" (Heggade Kanna's 2½-line record, c. 1180). Rice's translation (EC II
  1889 p. 169–170) gives: born in the Brahma-Kshatra race; "by order of Indra kshitindra" (Rashtrakuta
  Indra IV) conquered Vajvala-deva, younger brother of Patala-malla, before his lord Jagadekavira
  (= Marasimha); praised by his lord in the Nolamba war ("By thee, what kings soever are there that
  will not fall as food to the black serpent my arrow?"); in the war with king Rana-singa; defeated
  Chaladanka-Ganga's attempt "to seize by force of arms the wealth of Ganga's empire"; "caused the
  cups made from the skulls of brave men, decorated with jewels … to brim over with the blood of
  heroes". Tier: inscription. Wikipedia "Tyagada Brahmadeva Pillar" dates it c. 983, height 2.3 m,
  relief on the base showing Chavundaraya seated with his guru Nemichandra and chauri-bearers
  (cites Rice 1889 nos. 109–110; Sarma 1992).
- No. 38 (1923: 59) — Kuge Brahmadeva pillar (ಕೂಗೆ ಬ್ರಹ್ಮದೇವ ಕಂಬ), Chandragiri: 113 lines on three
  sides (Sanskrit) + Kannada fourth side, the nishidhi-eulogy of Marasimha II. Rice's translation
  EC II 1889 pp. 118–121; summary Intro pp. 18–20. Key lines (Rice's rendering): "All the skulls of
  the Pallavas, spoils from their defeat, instead of casting away he collected together, presenting
  the appearance of a Kapalika"; "the fort of Uchchangi, which had formerly been celebrated for being
  surrounded and besieged but abandoned through inability to take it he captured"; places fought in:
  "the regions within the skirts of the Vindhya forests, the chief city of Manyakheta, Gonuru,
  Uchchangi, the Banavasi country, the Parise fort"; titles Ganga-chudamani, Guttiya-Ganga,
  Chalad-uttaranga, Nolamba-kulantaka, mandalika-Trinetra, Ganga-Vidyadhara, Ganga-Kandarpa,
  Ganga-Vajra; "born to be a wild-fire to the lion Rajaditya, the head-jewel of the Chalukyas"; he
  "caused basadis and mana-stambhas to be erected in numerous places". Subsequent addition: "for one
  year more he carried on the kingdom, and then, in the presence of the feet of Ajitasena-bhattaraka
  of Bankapura, in the manner of a faithful worshipper … entered the tomb." Closing verse: "O Chola
  king, your master has gone, without conquering your treasures or yourself — keep quiet; Pandya,
  grin and run not away in haste from your kingdom through fear … the Ganga chief has departed to
  the dwelling of the gods." Tier: inscription. Jain Heritage Centres summary (citing EC II 1923
  p. 13) adds: 3-day sallekhana vow; pillar c. 30 ft with Sarvahna yaksha on top; "first instance of
  a nishidhi inscribed on a Brahma-stambha".
  https://www.jainheritagecentres.com/jainism/jain-epigraphy/marasimhas-inscription-at-shravanabelagola/
- No. 67 (c. 995): on the upper-storey image in the Chavundaraya basadi: "the minister Chamunda's son
  had a (or this) home for Jina made: the lay-disciple of Ajitasena muni." Rice p. 160 (text line
  ~18633). Tier: inscription.
- No. 89 (12th c.): verse naming the three "firm promoters of the Jina doctrine" at Belgola —
  "Raya, the minister of king Racha-Malla; after him Ganga, the minister of king Vishnu; and after
  him Hulla, the minister of king Nrisimha-Deva". Rice Intro p. 34. Tier: inscription.
- Nos. 85, 105, 137, 138: 12th-c. records that identify Gommata with Bahubali/Bhujabali, call the
  Belgola image "the southern Kukkuṭeśvara", and repeat that Chamundaraya's motive was to reproduce
  the legendary Podanapura image. Rice Intro pp. 24–27. Tier: inscription (for the 12th-c. tradition).
- Nos. 60–61: two undated viragals (hero stones) set against the Terina basti, c. Marasimha's time.

### 2b. Government Museum, Bengaluru — the three Fleet records (EI VI, PD)
- A. Doddahundi nishidhi (ದೊಡ್ಡಹುಂಡಿ), T. Narasipur taluk, found "lying in a pond"; c. 6 ft; now in
  the Museum. Sculpture: prince Nitimarga on a couch with two royal umbrellas; eldest son Satyavakya
  standing at his head under an umbrella; follower Agarayya seated holding the prince's legs across
  his knees and "holds a dagger with his right hand, apparently drawing it from the prince's left
  side". Record grants Agarayya a kalnāḍu (land for the fallen) named Guldapāḍi. EI VI pp. 40–45.
  Tier: inscription. Interpretation as sallekhana (Sarma 1992 pp. 17, 204) vs. Fleet's silence —
  see §5.
- B. Begur (ಬೇಗೂರು) hero stone of Ereyappa — "partly a vīrgal … of a hero who was killed in battle
  and partly an ordinary śāsana", archaic Kannada prose, 6 main lines + 6 down each side. Content:
  Ereyapparasa, ruling Gangavadi 96,000 "as one whole under a single umbrella", lent to Ayyapadeva
  (a Nolamba) a force "collected and commanded by the leader of the Nāgattaras" to fight
  Vīramahēndra; the leader "was pierced and died" at Tumbepāḍi (= Tumbadi, Madhugiri taluk, "about
  fifty-four miles N.N.W.½W. from Bēgūr"); Ereyappa "tied the paṭṭa" on Iruga as the new Nagattara
  leader and granted him "the Bempūr twelve" (Bempur = Begur). Fleet on the sculpture: "a horseman
  with a sword, under umbrellas that indicate high rank, is probably Vîramahêndra"; "an elephant with
  arrows in its forehead"; above, a seated central figure (the dead leader) "attended by female
  chauri-bearers"; "a standing man with a plumed headdress is probably Iruga". The paṭṭa "seems to
  have included an upright plume in addition to the fillet". EI VI pp. 45–50.
  Tier: inscription. Commons: File:Old Kannada inscription (908-938 AD) of Western Ganga dynasty
  King Ereyappa on hero stone at Begur.jpg (PD, from EI VI plate).
- C. Atakur (ಆತಕೂರು / Ātukūr), Mandya dt., tablet 5′1″ × 6′8″ found before the Challēśvara temple;
  now in the Museum. 19 main lines + a subsidiary record (lines 20–24) in the upper margins; the
  sculpture shows "a hound and a boar fighting". Content (Fleet's translation, EI VI pp. 50–57):
  Kannaradeva (Krishna III), with birudas Aṅkatriṇētra, Āneveḍeṅga, Vanagajamalla, Kachchega, "came
  upon" the Chola Rājāditya "Mūvaḍi-Chōḷa" and "fought and killed him at Takkōla" and was "going in
  state, going in triumph"; Satyavakya-Būtuga (birudas Nanniya-Gaṅga, Jayad-uttaraṁga,
  Gaṅga-Gāṅgēya, Gaṅga-Nārāyaṇa), in the seventh year of his paṭṭa; Maṇalera of the Sagara-vaṁśa,
  "lord of Valabhī the best of towns", "the champion (aṅkakāra) of Būtuga", asked as a gift for
  Būtuga's "favourite hound Kāḷi, called the one that bays loudly"; at Beḷatūr in the Kelale nāḍu
  (5 miles WSW of Atakur) the hound was loosed at a boar on a hill "in the western quarter of the
  village" and "the boar and hound died together (oḍa-sattuvu)"; a stone was set up before
  Challēśvara at Ātukūr and land below the big tank yielding two kaṇḍugas of grain granted; whoever
  destroys it "incurs the guilt of the hound's sin", and the gorava (temple manager) incurs the same
  "if he fails to worship the stone"; verses (lines 15–19) praise Maṇalera striking "the forehead of
  the elephant that was the Chōḷa's fortress"; Būtuga is "kadan-aika-Śūdraka". Subsidiary record,
  line 20: "Ereyapana magaṁ Rāchamallanaṁ Būtugaṁ kādi kondu" = Butuga "fought and killed
  Rachamalla, son of Ereyapa" and then governed the 96,000; and "Būtugaṁ Rājādityanaṁ bisu-geye
  kaḷḷan=āgi" which Fleet renders as Butuga "stabbed Rajaditya treacherously (as a thief) and killed
  him", for which Kannaradeva gave him Banavase 12,000, Belvola 300, Purigere 300, Kisukad 70,
  Bagenad 70 (these had earlier been Rēvakanimmaḍi's dowry from Amoghavarsha-Vaddiga, fn. 3); Butuga
  gave Maṇalera "Ātukūr twelve and Kādiyūr in Belvola as a bāḷgachchu (sword-washing) grant".
  Earlier editions: Fleet EI II (1892) pp. 167–174; Rice EC III (1894) Md. 41 — both PD.
  Tier: inscription. Commons: File:Atakur memorial stone with inscription in old Kannada (949 C.E.).jpg
  (CC BY-SA 3.0, Holenarasipura 2012); File:Epigraphia Indica Vol 6 0076.jpg (PD plate).

### 2d. Literary sources (contemporary or near)
- *Chāvuṇḍarāya Purāṇa* / *Triṣaṣṭilakṣaṇa Mahāpurāṇa* (ಚಾವುಂಡರಾಯ ಪುರಾಣ), Kannada prose, Śaka 900 =
  978, by Chavundaraya himself; opening and closing chapters give his lord as "Ganga-kula-chudamani
  Jagadekavira Nonamba-kulantaka-deva" (Marasimha), his guru as Ajitasena, and the origin of his
  titles (list in §4). Rice, EC II 1889 Intro pp. 22, 33–34. Lives of the 24 Tirthankaras + other
  worthies = 63, drawn on Jinasena's Ādipurāṇa and Gunabhadra's Uttarapurāṇa and Pampa's
  Ādipurāṇa; "lucid Kannada for the common man"; includes a short Jaina Rāmāyaṇa. Sources:
  Shastriya Kannada db "Chavundaraya"; Sastri 1955 p. 357 and Narasimhacharya 1988 p. 18 via
  Wikipedia. Online edition: UNKNOWN (no Kannada Wikisource copy located). Licence: text is PD;
  modern critical editions are not.
- *Kavirājamārga* (ಕವಿರಾಜಮಾರ್ಗ), c. 850, names Durvinita among early Kannada prose writers. Sastri
  1955 p. 355; Kamath 2001 p. 40 (via Wikipedia "Western Ganga literature"). Kannada Wikisource
  hosts the Kavirajamarga text (PD) — verify the line before quoting.
- Ranna's *Ajitapurāṇa* (993) mentions Attimabbe's visit to the Bahubali image; Ranna says he was
  "formed into a ratna by Chavundaraya". Sources: Shastriya Kannada db "Ranna"; Nagarajaiah,
  *History of Early Ganga Monarchy and Jainism*, p. 86 (jainqq.org/explore/006723/86).
- Nemichandra's *Gommaṭasāra* (ಗೊಮ್ಮಟಸಾರ), composed at Chavundaraya's request and named after his pet
  name Gommaṭa; Chavundaraya's own Kannada commentary *Vīramārtaṇḍī* on it. Sources: Upadhye papers
  1983 p. 237 via Nagarajaiah p. 86; Wikipedia "Nemichandra" (J. L. Jaini 1927 introduction).
- Buchanan 1807 (eyewitness, July 1800): see §4 and §6.
- Later legend texts (not contemporary): *Bhujabali Charitra* (Sanskrit, date "cannot be definitely
  ascertained … composed long after"), *Bhujabaliśataka* of Doddayya c. 1550, *Bhujabalicharite* of
  Pañchabāṇa 1614, *Gommaṭēśvaracharite* of Anantakavi c. 1780, *Rājāvaḷikathe* of Devachandra
  1838, the Belgola *Sthalapurāṇa* (19th c.). Sources: Rice Intro pp. 24–26 (summaries, PD);
  Sangave as quoted on jainworld.com. Tier: legend.

### 2e. Coins

  claim of a Kannada legend "Bhadr" on the coins could not be traced to a numismatic source — mark
  UNKNOWN.

## 3. Candidate stories (3–5)

### Story A — "The arrow and the old woman": Chavundaraya and the Gommata colossus (c. 975–983)
Kannada title idea: ಗೊಮ್ಮಟನ ಕೆತ್ತಿದ ಮಂತ್ರಿ. People: Chavundaraya (ಚಾವುಂಡರಾಯ), his king Rachamalla IV
(ರಾಚಮಲ್ಲ), the dying Marasimha II (ಮಾರಸಿಂಹ), the gurus Ajitasena (ಅಜಿತಸೇನ) and Nemichandra
(ನೇಮಿಚಂದ್ರ), his mother Kalala-devi / Kalikamba (ಕಾಳಲದೇವಿ), the poet Ranna (ರನ್ನ), his son Jinadeva
(ಜಿನದೇವ), the rebel Chaladanka-Ganga, the old woman Gullakayajji (ಗುಳ್ಳಕಾಯಜ್ಜಿ).

Arc (beats):
1. 963–973. The general's war decade: Vajjala-deva routed "before the face of Jagadekavira"; the
   plain of Gonur against the Nolambas; the hill-fort of Uchchangi that had beaten every earlier
   siege; the fort of Bagalur where he kills Tribhuvana-vira; he kills his own younger brother
   Nagavarma "on account of his hatred"; cups of jewelled skulls brim with blood. [inscription:
   EC II no. 109 + Purana titles, Rice pp. 33–34, 169–170]
2. 973. Marasimha crowns Indra IV at Bankapur to keep the Rashtrakuta empire alive; a year later he
   lays down the kingdom and starves to death at Ajitasena's feet. The epitaph taunts the Chola and
   Pandya: "keep quiet … the Ganga chief has departed to the dwelling of the gods." [inscription:
   EC II no. 38]
3. 974/975. Succession crisis: Chaladanka-Ganga tries "to seize by force of arms the wealth of
   Ganga's empire"; Chavundaraya defeats him and Panchaladeva's rebellion, kills Mudu Rachayya at
   Bageyur, and seats Rachamalla IV. [inscription no. 109; scholarship: Krishna Rao 1936 pp. 109–113,
   Kamath 2001 p. 46 via Wikipedia]
4. 978. The warrior sits down to write: the Chavundaraya Purana in plain Kannada "for the common
   man", colophon Śaka 900 — and it does not mention any statue. [Rice p. 22]
5. The vow and the arrow (legend): a merchant at court describes the 525-bow-length Bahubali at
   Podanapura; mother and son vow not to taste milk until they see it; the journey proves
   impossible; at Belgola the goddess Padmavati appears in a dream: shoot an arrow south from the
   smaller hill and the image will rise. He shoots; the hidden Gommata appears "of the height of 13
   men". [legend: Bhujabali Charitra and Rajavali Kathe as summarised by Rice pp. 24–26]
6. 981/983. The real event: a 57-foot nude kayotsarga Bahubali is cut from the living granite of
   Vindhyagiri, facing north toward Chandragiri; three lines are carved under its hands in Kannada,
   Tamil and (Nagari) Marathi: "Śrī Chāvuṇḍarāje karaviyale". The sculptor leaves no name.
   [inscription nos. 75–76; EI VII]
7. The anointing (legend): milk from a hundred pots stops at the navel; an old woman with a drop of
   milk in half a gullakayi shell is mocked, then her offering runs down the whole image and floods
   the hill — the town is named Beḷgoḷa ("white pond"). Chavundaraya's pride is broken.
   [legend: Rajavali Kathe; Mackenzie's version in As. Res. IX 266, both via Rice p. 26]
8. Epilogue: his sister Pullabba dies by sallekhana c. 985; he dies before his Chandragiri basadi is
   consecrated; his son finishes it (no. 67, c. 995). A century later a rival-dynasty minister,
   Gangaraja, adds the cloister and carves his own line beneath Chavundaraya's. [inscription nos. 67,
   75–76; Nagarajaiah p. 86]

Goosebump moment: the three-language line under the hands — the most famous monolith in India
signed only by its patron, "and the bold sculptor … has not so much as left a record of his name"
(Rice). Evidence: the stone itself, legible today; PD photographs; Hultzsch's plate.

Dramatised vs recorded: everything in beats 1–4, 6, 8 is on stone or in the author's own colophon.
Beats 5 and 7 are 16th–19th-century legend; flag them as such in the book (the brief forbids
unsourced invention, but a legend tier is allowed if named). The "golden arrow" and "Gullakayajji" are
not in any 10th-century record. The 981 vs 983 date is a live scholarly dispute — pick one and footnote.

Why it grips adults: a man who collected skulls builds the gentlest image in India; the pride-humbling
old woman; a 1,000-year-old anointing ritual still performed (Mahamastakabhisheka every 12 years —
Wikipedia "Gommateshwara statue", citing The Hindu). Evidence base: excellent (strongest of the five).

### Story B — "The hound Kali": Atakur, Takkolam and the price of a kingdom (938–950)
People: Butuga II (ಬೂತುಗ), his wife Revakanimmadi (ರೇವಕನಿಮ್ಮಡಿ, Rashtrakuta princess), emperor Krishna
III / Kannaradeva (ಕನ್ನರದೇವ), the Chola prince Rajaditya (ರಾಜಾದಿತ್ಯ), the champion Manalera (ಮಣಲೆರ),
the hound Kali (ಕಾಳಿ), the murdered brother Rachamalla III, the boar of Belatur.

Arc:
1. 938. Butuga, son-in-law of Amoghavarsha III, "fought and killed Rachamalla, son of Ereyapa" — his
   own elder brother — and took the 96,000. [inscription: Atakur line 20]
2. Marriage politics: Banavasi 12,000 and the Belvola/Purigere/Kisukad/Bagenad districts are
   Revakanimmadi's dowry. [Fleet fn., EI VI p. 56]
3. 949. Takkolam (North Arcot): Krishna III vs Rajaditya. The main record says Kannaradeva "fought
   and killed him at Takkōla"; the subsidiary record says Butuga killed Rajaditya "bisu-geye kaḷḷan
   āgi" — Fleet: stabbed him treacherously "as a thief"; later readers: embraced him on his elephant
   and stabbed him. The Cholas remember the prince as "he who died on the elephant's back".
   [inscription; Chola plates via Wikipedia "Rajaditya Chola"]
4. The reward: the emperor confirms the northern districts on Butuga. [inscription]
5. Manalera, who "struck the forehead of the elephant that was the Chōḷa's fortress", asks for one
   gift: the king's favourite hound, "the one that bays loudly". [inscription lines 7–10]
6. Belatur: the hound is loosed on a boar on the western hill; "the boar and the hound died
   together". [inscription]
7. 949–50. A stone is raised before Challesvara at Atukur carved with the hound and boar; two
   kandugas of grain-land endowed; a curse — whoever ruins it takes on "the guilt of the hound's
   sin", and the gorava who eats before worshipping the stone the same. [inscription]
8. 963–975 coda: Butuga's son Marasimha collects Pallava skulls, crowns an emperor, starves to death
   at Bankapur; Butuga's widows Padmavati and Revakanimmadi also die by sallekhana at Koppala.
   [EC II no. 38; Nagarajaiah p. 92 citing EC II (R) 65(59)]

Goosebump moment: the king who stabbed a prince in an embrace weeps for a dog — the stone's curse
puts a hound's sin on a man's head. Evidence: one of the best-preserved 10th-century Kannada
inscriptions, in a public museum, edited three times (Fleet 1892, Rice 1894, Fleet 1900), with the
hound-boar relief visible in CC photos.

Dramatised vs recorded: the fratricide, the killing of Rajaditya, the hound's gift, the boar fight,
the grant and curse are all recorded. HOW Rajaditya died (arrow vs dagger-in-embrace) is disputed
between the two parts of the same stone and between editors — do not assert one. Why it grips:
loyalty, betrayal, a dog's death that outlived an empire; the Chola revenge (Rajaraja's conquest of
Gangavadi c. 1000–1004) closes the circle. Evidence base: excellent.

### Story C — "Pierced at Tumbepadi": the Nagattara of Begur and the first "Bengaluru" (c. 900–938)
People: the Nagattara chief (ನಾಗತ್ತರ, name lost on the main stone), king Ereyappa (ಎರೆಯಪ್ಪ), the
Nolamba Ayyapadeva (ಅಯ್ಯಪದೇವ), the enemy Viramahendra (ವೀರಮಹೇಂದ್ರ), the successor Iruga (ಇರುಗ), the
daughter Tondabbe (ತೊಂಡಬ್ಬೆ), the sons who fell "in the Bengaluru battle".

Arc:
1. c. 900. Nagattara, feudatory of the Ganga king, builds the Somanatha (now Nageshwara) temple at
   Begur after a victory over "Beeravarma". [scholarship: Devarakonda Reddy, Itihasa Darshana 3, via
   Wikipedia "Begur inscriptions and hero stones" — that Wikipedia page carries an LLM-content
   warning; verify in Itihasa Darshana before use]
2. Ereyappa, ruling Gangavadi 96,000 "under a single umbrella", orders the Nagattara "with his
   tributary chiefs" to supply an army to Ayyapadeva against Viramahendra (probably E. Chalukya
   Bhima II, per Hultzsch's note). [inscription: EI VI B]
3. Tumbepadi, 54 miles north-west: the leader "was pierced and died" — the stone shows the elephant
   with arrows in its forehead and the horseman under umbrellas. [inscription + Fleet's sculpture
   description]
4. Ereyappa ties the paṭṭa with its upright plume on Iruga and grants him the Bempur twelve: the
   stone is at once a hero's memorial and a title deed. [inscription]
5. The women: Tondabbe, "Nagatara's daughter", "ending her time in the manner appointed, died" —
   a Jain sallekhana recorded on a stone with a seated woman carved above; her husband's stone shows
   a neck wound. [EC IX Bn. 94 via Wikipedia; verify in EC IX (1905), PD]
6. The sons: a separate hero stone (found 1914, R. Narasimhachar) records the deaths of Nagatara's
   sons in a battle at "Bengulooru" — the earliest written form of the city's name. [EC IX supplement,
   Bangalore no. 174, via Wikipedia; scholarship: Manjunath, Karthik, Padigar]
7. 949. Thirty years later the same ruling house is murdered by Butuga (Atakur line 20); the
   Nagattara line continues under new masters.

Goosebump moment: the first written "Bengaluru" is on a 10th-century hero stone recording two
brothers' deaths — a city's name born in grief. Evidence: good for the main stone (EI VI, in the
Government Museum); the "Bengaluru" stone is well known locally but this pass could not open EC IX
or the Padigar study — read before writing. Dramatised vs recorded: names of the fallen leader and
the battle's course are not recorded; the arrows-in-elephant image is on the stone. Why it grips:
Bengaluru readers' own origin story, told from a stone 14 km from the airport road. Evidence base:
good, with one unverified link.

### Story D — "The scholar-king": Durvinita, the disputed throne and the Kiratarjuniya (c. 529–579)
People: Durvinita (ದುರ್ವಿನೀತ), his father Avinita (ಅವಿನೀತ), the preferred younger brother (unnamed),
the Pallava and Kadamba backers, the Jain grammarian Pujyapada (ಪೂಜ್ಯಪಾದ), the Sanskrit poet Bharavi
(ಭಾರವಿ), his mother the Punnata princess, his daughter married to a Chalukya.

Arc:
1. Avinita favours a younger son; the Pallavas and Kadambas back him; the Nallala and Kadagattur
   plates refer to the dispute; Durvinita wins "by his valour". [scholarship: Kamath 2001 p. 40;
   Sheik Ali and Ramesh in Adiga 2006 p. 100, via Wikipedia "Durvinita"]
2. Battles at Andari, Alattur, Porulare, Pernagara (Gummareddipura plates); the kingdom reaches
   from Mysore to Kolar and Anantapur (Hebbata grant, year 31). [inscription via Srikanta Sastri 1952;
   Wikipedia]
3. The Jain tutor: tradition makes Pujyapada his preceptor; Pujyapada's Maleyur lies near Kittur,
   capital of his mother's Punnata. [scholarship: Nagarajaiah p. 63, citing Krishna Rao 1936]
4. The library: he is "Śabdāvatāra-kāra", author of a Sanskrit Vaḍḍakathā (a rendering of the
   Bṛhatkathā / Vasudevahiṇḍi), and of a commentary on canto 15 of Bharavi's Kirātārjunīya; the
   Nallala grant of year 40 calls him "skilled in poetry, stories, dramas and commentaries".
   [inscription (plates) as reported in MAR 1912 pp. 57, 63; Muddachari, "Durvinita — a man of
   letters", Proc. IHC 33 (1971) pp. 126–130, JSTOR 44145322]
5. Bharavi at court: the Avantisundarīkathāsāra (attributed to Dandin) says Bharavi stayed with
   Durvinita. [scholarship: Muddachari 1971 pp. 126–127]
6. 850: Kavirajamarga remembers him as a Kannada prose writer — nothing survives. [literary]
7. Disputes to dramatise honestly: Śabdāvatāra is also credited to Pujyapada (Coward & Kunjunni
   Raja 1990 p. 175); whether the Kiratarjuniya commentary was in Kannada or Sanskrit is unsettled;
   whether his daughter married Vijayaditya or Pulakeshi II is unsettled (Moraes 1931 p. 55).

Goosebump moment: a 6th-century king whose only surviving words are a copper-plate boast that he
wrote a commentary on canto 15 of the hardest poem in Sanskrit — and the poem's own tradition
(Aihole inscription of 634 naming Bharavi) confirms the poet was already famous. Evidence base:
moderate — all on plates of uncertain date, with no quoted Kannada line located online in this
pass. Needs MAR 1912 and Muddachari 1971 opened before writing.

### Story E — "Sand and a curse": Talakadu from Ganga capital to buried town (c. 400–1610–today)
People: Harivarma at Dalavanapura; Rajaraja Chola; Hoysala Vishnuvardhana; Madhava-mantri (dam);
Rani Alamelamma (ಅಲಮೇಲಮ್ಮ) and Raja Wodeyar (ರಾಜ ಒಡೆಯರ್).
Arc: capital of the Gangas "for nearly six centuries" → Chola Rajarajapura (c. 1004) → Hoysala
capture → 30-plus temples under sand advancing "9 or 10 feet a year" → 1610 legend: the drowning
rani's three-fold curse "ತಲಕಾಡು ಮರಳಾಗಿ, ಮಾಲಂಗಿ ಮಡುವಾಗಿ, ಮೈಸೂರು ದೊರೆಗೆ ಮಕ್ಕಳಾಗದೆ ಹೋಗಲಿ" → ISRO-guided
excavations find Ganga-period sanctums under Chola towers.
Sources: Wikipedia "Talakadu" (citing Sashi Sivaramakrishna, *The Curse of Talakadu: a legend in
history*, ISBN 81-291-0836-4; Deccan Herald 2003); Shastriya Kannada db "Talakadu"; Krishnamurthy,
Gopal & Gangadhar excavation report (Karnataka Itihasa Academy, not opened).
Caution: the curse is legend; its earliest written record was NOT established in this pass (UNKNOWN);
the sand is explained by the Kaveri's shallowing after a dam and south-west monsoon winds
(livehistoryindia.com; Wikipedia). The Ganga content is thin — this is a frame story, not a Ganga
story. Evidence base: weak for the Ganga period; strong for the geography.

## 4. Little-known facts (one line + source + tier)

1. The Gommata colossus is signed in three scripts and languages — Kannada, Tamil (Grantha +
   Vatteluttu) and Nagari-Marathi — but the sculptor is nameless. EC II 1889 nos. 75–76; EI VII
   pp. 108–109. Tier: inscription.
2. Rice's 983 date rests on an argument from silence: the 978 Purana "makes no mention of the statue
   … It is incredible that so great a work should have been unnoticed if then in existence." EC II
   1889 Intro p. 22. Tier: scholarship.
3. Buchanan (1800) never saw the statue: "an inflammation that attacked my eyes" kept him below; his
   painter's sketch gave a height of "seventy feet 3 inches", and Sir Arthur Wellesley (the later
   Duke of Wellington) "thinks the drawing rather more clumsy than the image" and believed "the rock
   has been cut until nothing but the image remained". Buchanan 1807 vol. III p. 410–411
   (archive text lines ~22513–22546). Tier: eyewitness.
4. Rice measured it at 57 ft against Wellesley's 60 ft 3 in and Buchanan's 70 ft 3 in, using
   Scanlon's 1871 scaffolding measurements (Ind. Ant. II 129); the sculptor cut a scale on the
   lotus pedestal "which corresponds almost exactly with 3 feet 3½ inches English, or with the French
   metre". EC II 1889 Intro pp. 30–31. Tier: scholarship.
5. The Jains of Belgola told Buchanan the colossus was made by "a certain Chamunda Raya, descended
   from whom were the nine Belalla Rajas" — i.e. by 1800 local memory had merged the Ganga minister
   into the Hoysala line. Buchanan III p. 411. Tier: legend (as recorded 1800).
6. Chavundaraya's own list of titles, from the Purana: Samara-dhurandhara (Khedaga war),
   Vira-martanda (Gonur), Rana-ranga-singa (Uchchangi), Vairikula-kaladanda (Bagalur),
   Bhuja-vikrama, Chaladanka-Ganga (for killing his brother Nagavarma), Samara-Parasurama and
   Pratipaksha-rakshasa (for killing Mudu Rachayya), Bhata-mari, Satya-Yudhishthira ("never telling an
   untruth even in jest"), Kavi-jana-sekhara. EC II 1889 Intro p. 34. Tier: literary/contemporary.
7. Rice on the same list: "among so many distinguished actions no mention is made of a single work
   of religious merit … little more than a record of warfare and bloodshed from beginning to end."
   EC II 1889 Intro p. 34. Tier: scholarship.
8. Marasimha's epitaph says he kept the skulls of the Pallavas "presenting the appearance of a
   Kapalika" — a Jain king boasting like a Shaiva ascetic. EC II 1889 no. 38 (p. 120). Tier:
   inscription.
9. The Atakur stone makes a temple priest liable for a dog's sin if he eats before worshipping the
   stone; Fleet notes memorial stones for animals are otherwise unknown and compares a 16th-century
   Tekal hound-and-tiger fight. EI VI p. 55 n. Tier: inscription + scholarship.
10. Butuga II married into the Rashtrakutas; the five districts he "won" at Takkolam had already
    been his wife Revakanimmadi's dowry. EI VI p. 56 fn. 3. Tier: scholarship (Fleet).
11. The Begur paṭṭa (fillet of office) carried an upright plume — Fleet reads it off the four main
    figures on the stone. EI VI p. 46. Tier: scholarship from sculpture.
12. The Doddahundi stone shows a king dying on a couch with a servant drawing a dagger from his
    side while his son stands by — read by Sarma as a sallekhana memorial, but Fleet's description is
    literally of a dagger being withdrawn. EI VI p. 40; Sarma 1992 p. 204. Tier: inscription;
    interpretation disputed.
13. Nemichandra named his Gommaṭasāra after Chavundaraya's pet name Gommaṭa, and the statue took the
    name from the patron, not the other way round (Rice canvasses Gomata = "Gomates the Magian" of
    the Behistun inscription and rejects it). EC II 1889 Intro pp. 24–25; Nagarajaiah p. 86. Tier:
    scholarship.
14. Chavundaraya's sister Pullabba died by sallekhana at the Chandranatha basadi, Vijayamangalam,
    c. 985. Nagarajaiah p. 86 (citing ARE 597 of 1905). Tier: inscription via scholarship.
15. Butuga's widows Padmavati and Revakanimmadi, his daughters Kundanasamidevi and Bijjambika,
    and Rachamalla IV's wife Kanchabbarasi all died by sallekhana at Koppala. Nagarajaiah p. 92.
    Tier: scholarship citing inscriptions.
16. Sripurusha earned the title Permanadi after storming Penkulikottai (Kulidikki, Salem dt.) and
    the Pallava Nandivarman had to take it back — the Pallava "Kulidikki inscription" records a Ganga
    chief's death there. EI (Kulidikki inscription of Vijaya Nandivarman) via WebSearch summary of
    jainqq EI pages. Tier: inscription via summary — verify.
17. Shivamara II was imprisoned twice by the Rashtrakutas (Dhruva after Mudugundur; Govinda III)
    and still wrote the Gajāṣṭaka on elephant management. Kamath 2001 p. 50; Ashwathappa 2015 p. 91
    via Wikipedia "Shivamara II". Tier: scholarship.

## 5. Disputed or uncertain points

- Date of the colossus: 983 (Rice 1889, argument from the Purana's silence + Rachamalla's reign
  ending "984" in Rice's data) vs 13 March 981 (computation of the traditional Vibhava-year horoscope,
  accepted by Sangave and most Jain writers) vs 982 (various). Rice himself noted Vibhava fits
  neither 968 nor 1028, so the traditional year-name is suspect (Intro p. 22 fn.). Also the 1981
  "millennium" celebration assumed 981.
- Language of the Nagari line: Rice assumed Jain Prakrit/Maharashtri and dated it to Chavundaraya;
  Hultzsch (EI VII) called it Marathi and suspected both Nagari lines are Gangaraja-era (c. 1116).
  Popular claims that it is "the oldest Konkani" have no edition behind them.
- Numbering of Rachamalla: Rice "Racha Malla (974–984)" + "Rakkasa (984–999)"; Wikipedia Rachamalla
  IV (975–986) + V (986–999); Shastriya Kannada db "Rachamalla-3 (974–999)"; Hultzsch calls
  Chavundaraya minister of "Marasimha II and Rachamalla II". Choose one scheme and say so.
- Marasimha's death: 974 (Rice from Melagani Śaka 896; Nagarajaiah) vs 975 (Mulgund record of
  Aug. 975; Wikipedia; Gazetteer).
- How Rajaditya died: arrow while on his elephant (popular; Wikipedia main text) vs Fleet's
  "stabbed treacherously, as a thief" from the subsidiary record vs "embraced and stabbed with a
  dagger" (EI VI no. 6c p. 57 as cited on Wikipedia "Rajaditya Chola"). The main record credits
  Krishna III; the subsidiary record credits Butuga. The Chola plates call him "died on the elephant".
- Whether Butuga killed his elder brother Rachamalla III: the Atakur subsidiary record says so
  plainly ("kādi kondu"); Wikipedia "Butuga II" softens it to "overthrow". Use the stone.
- Begur "Bengaluru" inscription: widely cited as the oldest written "Bengaluru" (c. 890/900), but the
  Fleet-edited Begur stone (EI VI B) does not contain the name; it is a DIFFERENT stone (EC IX
  supplement no. 174, found 1914). Verify in EC IX before claiming.
- Doddahundi: sallekhana scene (Sarma) vs Fleet's literal description (dagger being drawn from the
  prince's side); date 840 (Fleet) vs 869 (Rice/Sarma); Fleet thinks Nitimarga/Satyavakya are titles,
  identifies Nitimarga with Ranavikrama.
- Simhanandi origin legend: appears in full only in later plates (Kudlur 962; "twelfth-century"
  records per Adiga 2006 p. 88); "no Jaina teacher is connected with this feat" in the earliest
  plates; a damaged plate of Shivamara I is the earliest to mention Jaina help. Treat as dynastic
  myth. Sources: Adiga via Wikipedia "Origin of the Western Ganga Dynasty"; jainqq summaries.
- Ganga origin: northern (Kanvayana gotra, Ikshvaku, Jahnaveya kula — in the plates) vs native
  south-Karnataka chiefs (Adiga, Sheik Ali) vs Kongu (Sewell). Sarma: the "Konganipattam" epithet
  first appears only in Avinita's Serugunda record.
- Durvinita's works: Śabdāvatāra (Pāṇini commentary) is by Pujyapada per Coward & Kunjunni Raja;
  Kiratarjuniya commentary language (Kannada per Muddachari; unstated elsewhere); daughter's
  Chalukya husband (Vijayaditya vs Pulakeshi II).
- Who wrote the first Kannada prose: Chavundaraya Purana (978) is the earliest DATED prose work;
  Shivakotyacharya's Vaḍḍārādhane is usually called earlier but is undated (9th–10th c. estimates;
  some say pre-6th). Shastriya Kannada db "Vaddaradhane"; "Literary prose: ancient and medieval".
  Do not call the Purana "the first Kannada prose" without this qualifier.
- Coins: the elephant pagodas attributed to the "Western Gangas" by dealers are dated c. 1080–1400,
  after the dynasty fell; attribution is uncertain (Museums Victoria: alternative Kongu Chera). Use
  only with a caveat, and do not put one in a 10th-century scene.
- Talakadu curse: 1610 date and the whole Alamelamma story rest on Mysore-court tradition; first
  written record not located (UNKNOWN). Sand advance figure "9 or 10 feet a year" is uncited on
  Wikipedia.
- Marathi/Konkani line "oldest evidence of written Konkani" (jainworld/other sites) — unsupported.
- Butuga II "defeated a Buddhist scholar in debate" and Durvinita "defeated Buddhists in debate":
  both appear only as uncited or loosely-cited lines (Rice 1905; Settar 1989 p. 78) — leave out or
  mark legend.
- Chavundaraya "and Butuga erected the Gommateshwara in 943" (Kamat's Potpourri via Wikipedia
  "Butuga II") is an error; ignore.

### Dynastic emblem
Elephant (ಆನೆ): seal on copper plates (Hebbata; tier inscription), "heraldic animal" per numismatic
catalogues; the Shastriya Kannada db links it to the Talakadu jungle. Titles on stone that an
artist can letter: "Satyavākya Konguṇivarma dharma-mahārājādhirāja, Koḷāla-puravarēśvara,
Nandagiri-nātha" (Atakur, Rice's quotation).

### Dress, armour, weapons, animals (from the stones themselves — tier: sculpture; all else
is inference and should be checked against Settar/Sontheimer, *Memorial Stones* 1982, not opened)
- Chiefs: fillet/paṭṭa of authority with an upright plume (Begur); royal umbrellas (two for a king,
  one for an heir — Doddahundi; Begur); female chauri-bearers; dagger worn at the side (Doddahundi);
  sword-armed horseman (Begur).
- Weapons recorded in text: arrows (Begur elephant; Marasimha's "black serpent my arrow"; the Atakur
  main text's elephant-forehead strike), dagger (Atakur subsidiary record "bisu-geye"; Doddahundi),
  sword (bāḷgachchu "sword-washing" grant), elephant-goad metaphor (no. 109), chaturaṅga-bala "the
  complete army of elephants, chariots, cavalry and infantry" (Atakur line, Fleet fn. 8).
- Elephants: war elephants as royal mounts and "fortresses" (Rajaditya died on his; Chavundaraya "an
  elephant by whose tusks … the temples of the enemy's elephants were split"); the Ganga seal.
- Dogs: a royal hunting hound that "bays loudly" set on wild boar in a trench/hill (Atakur).
- Jain monks: Digambara (nude) ascetics; nishidhi pillars with a yaksha on top; manastambhas
  (Marasimha "caused basadis and mana-stambhas to be erected"; Rice Intro p. 19 n. 2 citing
  Fergusson p. 276).
- Scripts for props: Pūrvada Haḷe-Kannada (10th c.), Grantha/Vatteluttu Tamil, Nagari — all on one
  rock; Kannada "boldly formed" letters ⅞ to 2¼ inches high on Begur (Fleet).
- Architecture: single-cell Ganga shrines with later Chola towers (Talakadu); the Chavundaraya basadi
  (10th-c. core, Dravida-style tower); Chandragiri's cluster of bastis; the hills Vindhyagiri
  ("3,347 feet above the level of the sea and about 470 feet above the plain", Rice) and Chandragiri;
  the Belgola tank "built by a Jain merchant of Seringapatam" (Buchanan, so 18th c. — do NOT show
  the present tank in a 10th-c. scene without checking its date).

### Anachronisms to avoid
- Hoysala-style star-plan temples and lathe-turned pillars (12th c.) in 10th-c. Gangavadi.
- Vijayanagara gopurams at Talakadu or Belgola.
- The cloister (suttālaya) round the colossus and the present enclosure walls — Gangaraja's, 1116.
- The enclosure around the Tyagada pillar — c. 1700.
- The Belgola tank — 18th century (Buchanan).
- Konkani or Marathi as the language of the court — the Marathi line is for pilgrims and probably 12th c.
- Gold elephant pagodas in a 950s scene (post-1080 coins).
- Muslim-era arms (talwars, chain mail), Mughal turbans, horses with stirrups in European style.
- A named sculptor for the colossus.
- "Bahubali" as the statue's name in 983: the records say Gommaṭa / Gommaṭēśvara; Bahubali/Bhujabali
  identification is stated in 12th-c. records (nos. 85, 105).
- The curse/Alamelamma in any Ganga-era Talakadu scene (1610).

## 7. Key people beyond the kings (one line each)

- Chavundaraya / Chamundaraya (ಚಾವುಂಡರಾಯ, c. 940–c. 989+): Brahma-Kshatra general, minister and
  Kannada/Sanskrit author under Marasimha II and Rachamalla IV; built the colossus. EC II nos. 75–76,
  109; Purana colophon.
- Ajitasena Bhattaraka (ಅಜಿತಸೇನ ಭಟ್ಟಾರಕ): Jain teacher at Bankapura; Marasimha died at his feet;
  Chavundaraya's guru per the Purana; Chavundaraya's son his lay-disciple (no. 67). Tier: inscription.
- Nemichandra Siddhanta-chakravarti (ನೇಮಿಚಂದ್ರ): author of Gommaṭasāra, Trilokasāra, Labdhisāra; carved
  with Chavundaraya on the Tyagada pillar base. Tier: scholarship (Upadhye; Sarma).
- Kalala-devi / Kalikamba / Kalika-devi (ಕಾಳಲದೇವಿ): Chavundaraya's mother in the legend texts; not in
  any 10th-c. record located. Tier: legend (Bhujabali Charitra; Rajavali Kathe via Rice).
- Jinadeva / Jinadevana (ಜಿನದೇವ): Chavundaraya's son, built/consecrated the Chandragiri basadi
  (no. 67, c. 995). Tier: inscription.
- Pullabba (ಪುಲ್ಲಬ್ಬೆ): Chavundaraya's sister, sallekhana c. 985 at Vijayamangalam. Nagarajaiah p. 86.
- Ranna (ರನ್ನ, b. 949): Kannada poet first sheltered by Chavundaraya, later laureate of Chalukya
  Tailapa II/Satyashraya; Ajitapurana 993, Sahasabhimavijaya/Gadayuddha. Shastriya Kannada db;
  Kamath 2001 p. 45.
- Attimabbe (ಅತ್ತಿಮಬ್ಬೆ, c. 950–1016): Jain patroness, "Dānachintāmaṇi", commissioned Ranna's
  Ajitapurana; visited the Bahubali image per that work. Shastriya Kannada db.
- Nagavarma I (ನಾಗವರ್ಮ): Brahmin scholar from Vengi patronised by Chavundaraya; Chandombudhi (earliest
  Kannada prosody), Karnataka Kadambari. Narasimhacharya 1988 pp. 12, 18 via Wikipedia.
- Pujyapada (ಪೂಜ್ಯಪಾದ): Jain grammarian (Jainendra vyākaraṇa; probably the Śabdāvatāra), traditional
  tutor of Durvinita. Nagarajaiah p. 63; Krishna Rao 1936.
- Bharavi (ಭಾರವಿ): Sanskrit poet of the Kirātārjunīya, said to have stayed at Durvinita's court
  (Avantisundarīkathāsāra). Muddachari 1971.
- Simhanandi (ಸಿಂಹನಂದಿ): Jain acharya of the origin legend who gives Dadiga and Madhava a kingdom
  (sword that cuts the stone pillar). Kudlur plates 962; tier legend.
- Revakanimmadi (ರೇವಕನಿಮ್ಮಡಿ): Rashtrakuta princess, wife of Butuga II, whose dowry was the Banavasi
  12,000 etc.; died by sallekhana at Koppala. EI VI p. 56 fn.; Nagarajaiah p. 92.
- Manalera (ಮಣಲೆರ): Sagara-vaṁśa champion of Butuga, "lord of Valabhī", owner of the hound Kali,
  grantee of Atakur-12. EI VI.
- Nagattara (ನಾಗತ್ತರ): Begur feudatory house (Fleet: a family name); its leader died at Tumbepadi;
  Iruga succeeded. EI VI; EC IX.
- Tondabbe (ತೊಂಡಬ್ಬೆ): Nagatara's daughter, sallekhana inscription at Begur. EC IX Bn. 94 (verify).
- Panchaladeva (ಪಂಚಾಳದೇವ): Ganga mahasamanta/rebel of 975 who briefly held the north (Mulgund
  inscription; EI V). Scholarship.
- Rajaditya Chola (ರಾಜಾದಿತ್ಯ): Parantaka I's son, killed at Takkolam 949, "he who died on the
  elephant's back". Atakur; Leiden plates.
- Krishna III / Kannaradeva (ಕನ್ನರದೇವ): Rashtrakuta emperor, Butuga's overlord and brother-in-law.
  Atakur.
- Gangaraja (ಗಂಗರಾಜ): Hoysala Vishnuvardhana's minister who built the cloister (c. 1116) and signed
  beneath Chavundaraya; d. 1133. EC II nos. 75–76, 90, 137.
- Heggade Kanna (ಹೆಗ್ಗಡೆ ಕಣ್ಣ): c. 1180 chief who erased three faces of Chavundaraya's pillar
  inscription to add his own 2½ lines (no. 110). EC II.
- Gullakayajji (ಗುಳ್ಳಕಾಯಜ್ಜಿ) / Kushmandini / Padmavati: the old woman of the anointing legend; a
  small image of her stands opposite the statue (jainworld; not verified in an edition). Tier: legend.
- Francis Buchanan (1800) and Arthur Wellesley (1799–1800): earliest European eyewitnesses on record;
  Colin Mackenzie's collection (As. Res. IX 266) recorded the Gullakayajji story c. 1800.
- B. Lewis Rice (1889), R. Narasimhachar (1923), J. F. Fleet (1892, 1900), E. Hultzsch (1902):
  the editors whose public-domain texts this file rests on.

## 8. Sources to avoid (or use with care)

- Wikipedia "Begur inscriptions and hero stones": carries a Feb-2026 banner that parts may be
  LLM-generated with unverified claims; internally inconsistent (1988 vs mid-1990s discovery). Use
  only as a finding aid to EC IX and Itihasa Darshana.
- Wikipedia "Butuga II": states Butuga and Chavundaraya erected the colossus "in 943" (impossible).
- Kamat's Potpourri (kamat.com) "History of Ganga Dynasty": popular, undated claims; the 943 error
  comes from here.
- newworldencyclopedia.org, bharatpedia.org, classicistranieri mirrors: stale Wikipedia copies.
- jainworld.com / herenow4u.net reprints of Sangave: useful for the 981 computation and the legends,
  but assert "oldest Konkani", give unsourced dates (978–993) and conflate legend with record.
- Travel blogs on Talakadu (amusingplanet, lakshmisharath, ghumakkar, kupi.com) and the Open/Homegrown/
  Quartz curse articles: entertaining, mutually contradictory on who Alamelamma was and when; no
  primary source.
- Numista/CNG/legends.com.tw coin pages: the "Western Ganga" label on 11th–14th-c. pagodas is a
  dealer convention; the legends.com.tw page adds an unrelated Kashmir claim.
- South Indian Inscriptions vol. XI introduction (whatisindia.com) dates Takkolam "c. 975" — an
  outlier against the dated Atakur stone (949–50).
- Britannica "Ganga dynasty": says Talakad fell to "the Chola ruler Vishnuvardhana" in 1004 —
  Vishnuvardhana was the Hoysala who took it a century later.
- Any page calling the Chavundaraya Purana "the first Kannada prose work" without the Vaddaradhane
  caveat, or the colossus "Bahubali" as a 10th-c. name.
- Rice's 1889 early-Ganga chronology (Harivarma "247 A.D.", Madhava "425") — superseded by Fleet's
  spurious-plate critique and later work; use Rice for the 10th century only.
- Mudiraja Weebly and similar caste-history sites cited on Wikipedia for Chavundaraya.
