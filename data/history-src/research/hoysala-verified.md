## 1. The Hoysala rise, before Vishnuvardhana

### 1a. Hill chiefs under a Chalukya overlord
RICE 1909 p. 96 (text lines 6020–6040):
> The Hoysalas at first acknowledged the supremacy of the Western Chalukyas, the chosen enemies of the
> Cholas, but the bond was a loose and friendly one, and in the time of Vishnuvarddhana they became
> independent. Their capital was Dorasamudra (now Halebid in Belur taluq), which appears in Sanskrit as
> Dvarasamudra and Dvaravatipura. But while this was under preparation and being adorned with splendid
> buildings by Vinayaditya (see SB 53), the capital was at first at Sosevur and then at Beluhur,
> Belapura or Velapura (Belur).
Same page, titles: "Malepayol-ganda (champion among the Malepas or hill chiefs)". Tier: scholarship
(from inscriptions).

RICE 1909 p. 98: Vinayaditya "is styled Tribhuvanamalla-Poysala-Deva, from the Western Chalukya king
Tribhuvanamalla Vikramaditya (reigned 1076-1126) being his overlord"; "had the six letters Ra-kka-sa
Po-ysa-la inscribed on his flag"; "The earliest date we have for him is 1047 (Ng 32, Cm 160)"; "The
latest date we have for him is 1100 (Bl 141)." Ereyanga "is described as a powerful right arm to the
Chalukya king. He trampled down the Malava army, burnt Dhara and laid it in ruins, dragged down Chola
and plundered his camp, broke and ruined Kalinga (Sh 64, etc.). By his wife Echala-Devi he had three
sons - Ballala, Bitti-Deva, and Udayaditya." Tier: inscription (as summarised by Rice).

EC II 1923 Intro p. 49 (n110), on No. 132 (56) of 1123, verbatim from the record's prologue:
> "From (Brahma) the offspring of the lotus navel of Vishnu was born Atri; his son was the Moon; his son
> Budha; his son Pururava; his son Ayu; his son Nahusha; his son Yayati; his son Yadu: and in the line
> of Yadu arose many kings. Among those famous ones was a certain king, Sala, to whom, on a certain
> occasion, a great sage in a forest said with reference to a fierce tiger poy sala (strike, Sala); and
> from this circumstance he adopted the sage's words as his name, as also the tiger crest. Thence the
> lords of Dvaravati became Poysalas and possessors of the tiger crest in Sasapura. Among them was king
> Vinayaditya".
Same page, on Vinayaditya in No. 143: "The pits dug for bricks became tanks, the mountains quarried for
stone became level with the ground, the roads by which the mortar-carts passed became ravines; - thus
did king Poysala cause Jina temples to be built." And: "No. 118 (44), of 1120, however, mentions him
[Nripa-Kama] as the patron of Echa, the father of Vishnuvardhana's general Ganga-Raja." Tier:
inscription (the prologue) / legend (the content of the Sala story).

EC V Intro p. x (text lines 670–700), Nripa-Kama: "Mj. 43 of 1022 where he appears repelling an attack
by the Kongalva king ... In Ag. 76 we find the Kongalva king again attacking Poysala, here called the
base (manda) Poysala, in 1026". Tier: inscription.

### 1b. The first "Poysala"
RICE 1909 p. 95: "Of the time of Sala no records have been found, but a name Poysala occurs in an
inscription of 1006 at Kaliyur, on the opposite side of the river to Talakad (TN 44)."
EC III Intro p. 14 (text line 1172): "The occurrence of the name Poysala in S'aka 929 (A.D. 1007) see TN. 44
above (p. 9) is the earliest mention of it that I am able to quote at present."
DISPUTE (minor): the same record converted as 1006 (1909) and 1007 (1894) by the same editor.

### 1c. The Sala legend: the earliest dated telling and the variants
**Bl 58 (1117)**, EC V translation p. 56: "Obeisance to Kesava and Siva. Usual account, in verse, of
the rise of the Yadu-kula and the Poysalas. Sala slew the tiger and became Poysala". (Rice abridges the
legend here; the 1123 Belgola wording above is the full text of the same verse.) Tier: inscription
(that the court told it in 1117) / legend (the event).

**Bl 171 (Hale-Belavadi)**, EC V translation p. 100, headed "Date about 1160. A.D.":
> Among those Yadu kings, a king named Sala, hunting along the slopes of the Sahya mountains, was
> astonished to see a hare pursuing a tiger. While coming along saying this is heroic soil, a holy
> rishi, fearing that the tiger was coming to kill him, called out adam poy Sala (hit it, Sala), on
> which that valiant one, before it could step a span (gen) forward, slew it with his dagger (gen). The
> great muni thereupon rejoicing granted him the boon of this tiger as a victorious crest, and this
> exclamation as a victorious name. Thenceforward the Yadu-vamsa was known as the Poysala-vamsa, and
> that place became Sasakapura (the town of the hare).
EC V Intro p. ix calls Bl 171 "the earliest" telling. DISPUTE: Bl 171 is dated "about 1160" in the
same volume, so the earliest DATED telling is Bl 58 of 1117 (and EC II No. 132 of 1123 gives it in
full). Rice's "earliest" may mean the earliest form of the story, not the earliest stone.

**Bl 124 (1133)**, EC V translation p. 81 (page image n767): "When, for the increase of the wealth of
that king Sala's kingdom, a certain Jaina-bratisa by his mantras was bringing the goddess Padmavati of
Sasakapura into subjection, a tiger sprang forth upon them to break the spell, when the yogisvara,
holding out the handle of his chamara (or fan), said poy Sala (hit him, Sala): on which he fearlessly
smote it; from which time the name Poysala came to the Yadu kings, and the flag of a tiger waving on a
rod."

EC V Intro pp. ix–x lists the other weapons: cane (betta, Ak 71), "cane rod (bettada sele)" (Bl 142),
"kunchada sele, the rod of the yogi's fan or whisk" (Ak 108), "saláki" (Bl 74); and: "The seals of
some of the copper plates give the crest as the tiger and the rod, and this also appears on the
coins". RICE 1909 p. 95: "their crest on temples exhibits a free standing group of Sala stabbing the
tiger (see frontispiece, vol. v.), while the seal of copper-plate grants shows a dead tiger and the
rod (as in Bn 6)." Footnote: "According to Sb 28 his name [the yati's] was Sudatta ... Nr 46 calls him
Vardhamana-munindra." Tier: inscription for what the records say; legend for Sala.

Scholarship note (from `hoysala.md`, not re-read): Foekema 1996 p. 67 and Kamath 2001 p. 123 (via
Wikipedia) suggest the legend "may have arisen or gained popularity" after the Talakad victory over
the Chola, whose emblem was the tiger. UNVERIFIED in the original works. The CSMVS (Mumbai) Commons
photo labels a Sala-and-tiger sculpture from Balligame "circa 1070 CE" (museum label, not checked).

### 1d. Ballala I and the two princes
RICE 1909 pp. 98–99: Ballala I "succeeded his grandfather on the throne in 1100 (Bl 199) ... there are
inscriptions of his up to 1106 (Cn 169)"; "made Beluhur (Belur) his capital"; "marrying in one day in
1103 the three beautiful and accomplished daughters of Mariyane-dandanayaka"; "together with his
brothers repulsed an attack made by Jagaddeva (Santara king) on Dorasamudra, capturing his treasury and
the central ornament of his necklace (Bl 58, Ng 30)."
Bl 58, EC V translation p. 57: "With their swords beating down without mercy the pride of Pandya ...
the princes Vishnu and Ballala seized the wealth of his kingdom; - were they ordinary men? In
Dorasamudra they defeated the army of Jagadeva, painted the goddess of Victory with the blood of his
elephants for vermilion, and captured his treasury together with the central ornament of his
necklace". Tier: inscription.
DERRETT p. 42 (page image): "Ballala's last inscription was set up in 1108. Until his death, which must
have been in or about that year, he followed a cautious and temporizing policy, acknowledging from time
to time Calukya supremacy". DISPUTE: Ballala I's end 1106 (Rice) vs c. 1108 (Derrett).

## 2. Vishnuvardhana: accession, name, Chalukya overlordship

RICE 1909 p. 99 (text line 6130 ff.):
> Bitti-Deva, Ballala's brother, next came to the throne, and is celebrated as the rescuer of his
> country from the Cholas and the establisher of the independence of the Hoysalas, whose kingdom he
> greatly extended. In what year his reign began has not been discovered. ... The earliest actual date
> that can be cited for him is 1111 in Sh 89, but Kd 164 represents him as ruling in 1100: this must
> have been in association with Ballala, his elder brother.
DERRETT p. 43–44 (page images n65–n66): "It is even a matter of conjecture whether he became king in
1108 or 1109. His earliest inscription, referred to below, belongs to the year 1109. He died in 1142,
and thus reigned approximately 34 years." Section heading p. 43: "§2. 1108-1128. The first great
victories and the beginnings of large foreign commitments."
SASTRI 1958 p. 216 (dynasty table): "Ballala I 1100-1110 5. Vishnuvardhana 1110-1152".
EC V Intro p. xi: "With Cn. 169 of 1106, probably begin the inscriptions of Vishnuvarddhana, though
here only named Tribhuvanamalla-Poysala."
DISPUTE: accession 1106 (EC V, tentative), 1108/1109 (Derrett), 1110 (Sastri); first sure date 1111
(Rice). Joint rule from 1100 (Kd 164).

Overlordship, as the records phrase it:
- EC II 1923 Intro pp. 49–50: "The titles applied to him are the maha-mandalesvara who has acquired the
  band of five great instruments, Tribhuvanamalla, lord of the excellent city of Dvaravati, sun in the
  sky of the Yadava family, crest-jewel of rectitude, punisher of the Malepas, capturer of Talakadu
  Kongu Nangali Koyatur Uchchangi Nolambavadi and Hanungal, and Bhujabala-Vira-Ganga."
  (maha-mandalesvara = great feudatory; Tribhuvanamalla = the Chalukya emperor's own title.)
- EC II 1923 Intro p. 54: "No. 384 (144), of about 1135 ... Opening with an acknowledgment of Chalukya
  suzerainty, it gives the Hoysala genealogy from Vinayaditya to Vishnuvardhana".
- EC V Intro p. ix: early Hoysala inscriptions "have in the introduction an acknowledgment of Chalukya
  supremacy, dating from 1073 to 1174."
- DERRETT p. 55: "we have a reference to the time when Bitti-deva was on his return journey 'from the
  North, when he had gone to pay homage to (or attend the durbar of) the senior Hemmadi-raya of
  Kalyana', that is, Vikramaditya."
- SASTRI 1958 pp. 184–185: after taking Talakad he "turned against Vikramaditya himself and at first
  attained much success ... But Vikramaditya dealt firmly with the situation with the assistance of
  loyal feudatories, particularly Sinda Acugi II of Yelburgu ... in the end (1122-3) Vishnuvardhana made
  his submission and returned to his allegiance." p. 189: "in his reign [Somesvara III] Hoysala
  Vishnuvardhana not only threw off his allegiance to the Chalukyan power, but sought to extend his
  dominions at their expense".
DISPUTE: "independence" was a process, not a date: Rice ties it to 1116 ("caused ... to stand erect");
Sastri has a submission in 1122–3 and a final break under Somesvara III (1126–1138); the Chalukya
acknowledgment still opens records c. 1135.

The name. DERRETT, Chapter Three, Note 3 (p. 43), OCR only (text lines 11818–11845), cleaned:
> ... a further embellishment of the tradition, which had previously gained credence. It was to the
> effect that Visnuvardhana had been known as Bitti-deva before his conversion, had adopted the former
> name after it ... An inscription found at a place to the south of Mysore city published in M.A.R.
> 1944, no. 34 is dated 1098 and speaks of an agrahara founded by or named after Visnuvardhana Hoysala,
> undoubtedly our Visnuvardhana. Bitti is a pet name for Visnu ...
Tier: scholarship. The MAR 1944 record itself: UNVERIFIED (not reached).
RICE 1909 p. 99 holds the opposite: "This change was signalised by Bitti-Deva calling himself
thenceforward Vishnuvarddhana". DISPUTE.

## 3. Talakadu, 1116

### 3a. Before: Talakad under the Chola
EC III Intro p. 10 (line 880): "That it took place in the time of Rajaraja-Chola there is evidence in
the fact that Talakad, the Ganga capital, received the new name of Rajarajapura." Intro (on Ml 99, text
line 1362): "Rajarajapura or Talaikkadu belonged to the Vadakarai-nad ... of the Rajendra-Chola-valanad in
the Mudikonda-Chola-mandala." EC III Tamil records (e.g. text line 12149) still write
"Talaikkadina-Rajarajapurattil sri-Kirtti-Narayana-pperumal" under the Hoysalas. RICE 1909, Ganga chapter
(text line 3452): "His son Rajendra-Chola captured Talakad by 1004". Tier: inscription (the name) /
scholarship (the date).

### 3b. Gangaraja's family
EC II 1923 Intro p. 51 (n114):
> Nos. 73 (59), of 1118, 125 (45), 240 (90), 251, 384 (144) and 397, of 1119, all of which refer
> themselves to the reign of Vishnuvardhana, give Ganga-Raja's pedigree and an account of his exploits
> and pious acts. The pedigree is as follows: Nagavarma of the Kaundinya-gotra; his son Mara, his wife
> Makanabbe; their son Echa, also known as Budhamitra, whose patron was king Nripa-Kama-Hoysala; his wife
> Pochikabbe; their sons Bamma-chamupa and Ganga-Raja.
EC II 1889 Intro p. 39: "Echa, who was a Brahman (dvija) of the Kaundinya gotra. He became a devoted
Jaina, and his wife was Pochikabbe." Bl 124 (EC V p. 82) calls Gangaraja "chief of the Karnnata
Brahmans". Tier: inscription.
EC II 1923 Intro p. 55 pedigree table: Ganga-Raja m. Nagaladevi (and Lakshmi); son "Boppa alias Echa".
Intro p. 53: his wife Lakshmi "the lady of Policy in business and the lady of Victory in battle to
Ganga-Raja" (No. 130 (63)); she died by samadhi in 1121 (No. 128 (48)); his mother Pochikabbe died by
sallekhana in 1120 (No. 118 (44)).
EC III Intro p. 14: Ganga-Raja "was undoubtedly a descendant and representative of the old Ganga
dynasty" (Rice's inference; scholarship).

### 3c. His titles
EC II 1923 Intro p. 51: "The titles of Ganga-Raja as given in 118 (44), of 1120, are: obtainer of the
band of five great instruments, maha-samantadhipati, maha-prachanda-dandanayaka, terrifier of his
enemies, purifier of his family, friend of the learned, a moon in raising the volume of the milk ocean
the Jaina religion ... a purna-kumbha (vessel filled with water) for the coronation of king
Vishnuvardhana, a foundation pillar for supporting the mansion of dharma, a hero who keeps his word,
chaser of his enemies, and drohagharatta." Also p. 51 (No. 388): "svamidroha-gharatta (a millstone to
traitors to his lord)". EC II 1889 translation p. 125 (No. 44): "the full vessel for the
coronation-anointing of the Poysala raja Vishnu-varddhana ... a mill stone to traitors".
Bl 124 (EC V p. 83) gives his "seven hells": "To be false in speech, one; to show fear in battle, two;
to be addicted to others' wives, three; to give up refugees, four; to leave suppliants unsatisfied,
five; to forsake those to whom he is bound, six; to live in treachery to his lord, seven: - these are
the seven narakas (or hells) says Ganga." Tier: inscription.

### 3d. The fight at Talakad (EC II No. 240 (90); same words in Ml 31 at Tippur, 1117)
EC II 1923 Intro pp. 51–52 (n114–n115), verbatim:
> "Seizing Talakadu, taking possession similarly of Kongu, chasing away Jam...., pulling out Chengiri by
> the strength of his arm, sending Narasinga to the abode of Yama, - the general Ganga took
> Ganga-mandala and made it subject to the orders of king Vishnu. When Chola's samanta Adiyama,
> stationed in the camp of Talakadu, refused to surrender the nadu which Chola had given, saying 'Fight
> and take it', Ganga-Raja marched against him; and the two armies met. O Ganga-Raja, why do we require
> others for describing the greatness of your prowess? Is not Dama who, while the point of your sharp
> sword was lifting up the skin of his back, fled in the direction of Kanchi enough? O Ganga, unable to
> face you in battle, the Tigula (Tamil) Dama escaped and took refuge in the forest. Having remained
> till now in Talakadu astonishing people by his valour, the samanta Damodara, turning now his back on
> the fight through fear of Ganga-Raja's sword, lives like a Saiva ascetic eating from a skull.
> Ganga-Raja put to flight Narasingavarma and all the other samantas of Chola above the ghats and
> brought the whole nadu under the dominion of his lord's single umbrella."
EC II 1889 translation pp. 157–158 (No. 90), Rice's rendering of the same lines:
> when Chola's feudatory Adiyama, being with an army in the camp of Talakadu, the frontier (gadi) of
> Gangavadi nad above the Ghats, refused to surrender the country which Chola had given him, saying
> 'Fight and take it (if you can)' - filled with the desire of victory, caused the two armies to
> approach one another. ... When, eager for victory, the point of the sharp sword upraised in your hand
> was lifting the belt at his back, he fled - Daman, and ran in the direction of Kanchi, as if he would
> reach it, did he not? ... he took refuge night and day in the forest, more frightened than the
> frightened deer, the Tigula Daman. ... there still remained in Talakadu, dreading the blows of the
> sword of Ganga Raja, turning his back on the fight, carrying some food that a dog would not eat in a
> basket, (disguised) like a S'aiva ascetic, the feudatory Damodara. Him, approaching alone and on foot,
> taunting and showing up his cowardice, he made to flee. Moreover, having put to flight Narasinga-
> varmma and all the other feudatories of Chola above the Ghats, and brought all that had become nads
> under the dominion of one umbrella, king Vishnu was highly delighted, and said 'Ask (what you will).'
Tier: inscription (court eulogy). Note the two editions' readings: "skin of his back" (1923) vs "belt at
his back" (1889); "eating from a skull" (1923) vs "food that a dog would not eat in a basket" (1889).

Cn 209 (EC V Intro p. x n.): "terrified at hearing the sound of Hattagara Kete-Nayaka's glittering
shield, taking it for a rumbling of the earth or the rolling of thunder, Adiyama, crossing the river
from the south side, fled, and at the same moment Kete-Nayaka entered the fort of Talakadu behind king
Vishnuvarddhana." Bl 171 (same note): "On Pandya flourishing his sword, Hoysalesa cut him down with his
own sword, and left only half a man to look on in the Tigula's army." Tier: inscription.

EC III Intro p. 14: "Of the Hoysala inscriptions in this volume the first is Ml 31, which is dated in
S'aka 1039 (A.D. 1117) ... it contains an account of the recovery of Talakad from the Cholas by his
minister and general Ganga-Raja ... It was on these events that the latter took the title of Vira-Ganga,
as well as that of 'capturer of Talakad' (Talakadu-gonda) which appears on his coins, and is in evident
retaliation of the Gangaikonda of the Cholas." p. 15: "When the king offered Ganga-Raja a boon for these
exploits, he merely asked for Tippur, where this inscription is, and made a grant of it to
Megha[chandra]". Tier: inscription (Ml 31) / scholarship (the "retaliation").

The year. RICE 1909 p. 99: "Talekad was captured by his general Ganga-Raja in 1116, and this was
immediately followed up by the expulsion of the Cholas from Mysore". SASTRI 1958 p. 184: "after
defeating Adigaiman, the Chola governor of Talakad, annexed the province (1116)". EC II 1889 Intro
p. 37: the enclosure lines date "A.D. 1116". Tier: scholarship (the year is inferred: Bl 58 and Ml 31 of
1117 already presuppose it; no record dates the battle itself).

DERRETT pp. 49–50 (page images n71–n72), reconstruction (scholarship, copyrighted, short quotes):
the Chola governor "was a member of the well-known Adigaiman family of Tagadur, otherwise Dharmapuri";
"he crossed the Kaveri and set his forces with their backs to the river"; "Ganga-raja surveyed the
scene at leisure, and asked the king for a boon; he was readily granted several villages, most of
which he seems to have granted immediately for religious purposes"; then drove the enemy "until they
fled before him into the fortress of Talakad itself, which lay in the bend of the river immediately
upon its north bank"; "Without wasting time Visnuvardhana assailed the fort, and took it. The fate of
Adiyama is unknown". Derrett dates the verses he quotes to "an inscription of about 1178".
DISPUTE: Adiyama and Dama/Damodara: one man or two? 1923 treats "Dama" and "Damodara" in successive
verses; Derrett (p. 50) and EC V treat Adiyama and Damodara as two governors ("A certain Damodara fled
in the direction of Kanci; another, by name Narasinga-varma, fled and was put to death in the
neighbourhood of Bengiri"). The brief's "Adiyama/Dama line" is safest told as: Adiyama refused; a Tamil
samanta Dama/Damodara fled toward Kanchi; Narasingavarma fled.

### 3e. The corpses in the Kaveri (Bl 58, 1117)
EC V translation p. 57 (Bl 58), verbatim:
> First taking into his arms the wealth of the Poysala kingdom which was his inheritance, as his power
> increased, he brought all the points of the compass under his command, and capturing Talakadu, became
> the first to the Ganga kingdom, - this promoter of the Yadu-vamsa, the king Vishnu. ... he burnt the
> chief city of the Gangas, - the mighty Vishnu, named Bhuja-bala-Ganga. Behold, in order that
> Rajendra-Chola, disgusted at the water of the Kaveri suddenly becoming polluted, should be driven to
> the use of water from wells in the vicinity, Vishnu by the power of his arm threw all the corpses of
> his army into the stream of the river, and caused his valour to shine forth. Kubera, why is the south
> wind delayed? why has it not set in yet? It has been stopped by filling the nostrils of the skulls of
> the enemies slain in king Vishnu's expeditions of victory on the banks of the Kaveri. ... the victorious
> king Vishnu's Java (Yama) was afraid to straighten his moustaches, the sign of his manhood
Same, epithets: "causing the earth to tremble with the tramp of his Kambhoja horse ... a powerful lion
in devouring the fierce elephant Somesvara ... skilled with his bow in reducing the pride of Adiyama ...
having made his own Talakad, having taken the Kongu country ... having made Kanchi-pura tremble".
EC V Intro p. x gives the same as "polluted the water of the Kaveri by throwing the corpses of the
enemy into it". DERRETT p. 51 renders the line "mingled ... in the flood of the Kaveri river the corpses
of the army of Rajendra-Cola". Note: "his army" in the EC V translation is the enemy's army (Intro
p. x and Derrett agree). Derrett p. 50: the poet calls Kulottunga "somewhat slightingly, by the name

## 6. Shantala (ಶಾಂತಲಾದೇವಿ)

### 6a. Crown and council

### 6b. The basti of 1123 and her titles (EC II No. 132 (56); No. 131 (62))
EC II 1923 Intro pp. 55–56 (n122–n123), verbatim:
> No. 132 (56) records the erection in 1123 of the Savatigandhavarana-basti by Santala-Devi, the queen
> of Vishnuvardhana. ... It then eulogises his queen Santala-Devi and applies to her a long string of
> epithets. The eldest daughter of Marasinga and Machikabbe, she was to king Vishnu the goddess of
> Victory in battle, the goddess of Wealth always resting on his breast and the goddess of Fame
> spreading to the remote points of the compass the greatness of his valour. Among the epithets applied
> to her are a Brihaspati in discrimination, a Vachaspati in ready wit, the cause of the elevation of
> the four samayas or creeds, expert in singing instrumental music and dancing, a rampart to the Jaina
> faith, and a rutting elephant to ill-mannered co-wives (udvritta-savati-gandhavarane). The last
> epithet is interesting; and the temple founded by her was named after it. She endowed the temple with
> a village and certain lands, which she presented to her guru Prabhachandra after washing his feet.
> No. 131 (62), which also mentions the foundation of the temple by the queen, says that the god set up
> was Santi-Jina. In two fine verses it describes the beauty of Santala-Devi.
EC II 1889 Intro p. 41: "She also, with the permission of Vishnu-varddhana, endowed it with various
lands". EC II 1889 translation p. 131 (No. 53): "the dweller at his lotus feet, the senior queen and
crowned consort S'antala Devi". Tier: inscription.

### 6c. Her religion and family
EC II 1889 translation p. 132 (No. 53): "Her guru being Prabhachandra-siddhanta-deva; the mother who
bore her, the mine of good qualities, Machikabbe; the senior perggade Marasingayya, her father; her
uncle, the perggade Singimayya; her king Vishnu-varddhana; her favourite, Jinanatha; Vishnu her god: -

### 6d. Her death, 1131 (EC II No. 143 (53))
EC II 1889 translation p. 132, verbatim: "In the S'aka year 1053, the year Virodhikrit, the 5th of the
bright fortnight of Chaitra, on Monday, in the holy place of S'ivaganga she expired and went to
svarga." Then: "Joyfully the incomparable S'antala Devi, her father Marasingayya, and her mother
Machikabbe, so many expiring together, attained to svarga." Of her father: "the king went from this
world performing a vow, did he not?" West face: "'The queen has attained to godhead; it has fallen to
me to remain', thus saying, she came and in Belagula by severe penance this mature Machikabbe herself
quitted (her body.) With eyes half closed, repeating the five words (or phrases), glorious with
meditating on Jinendra, magnanimous in parting from relatives, absorbed in the vow of a sannyasi,
fasting for one month, Machikabbe herself attained godhead". Writer "Bokimayya".
EC II 1923 Intro p. 56: "Inscription 143 (53) records the death in 1131 of queen Santala-Devi at
Sivaganga ... Sivaganga where she is stated to have ended her life is no doubt the Saiva holy place of
that name some thirty miles to the north-west of Bangalore. Her parents also died soon after.
Resolving not to survive the loss of her daughter, Machikabbe went to Belgola and fasting for one month
died by the rite of samadhi in the presence of her guru Prabhachandra, Vardhamana and Ravichandra."
EC II 1889 Intro p. 49: "No particulars are given as to the circumstances of the queen's death, nor how
she came to be in such a place at such a time. The death may therefore have been sudden and unexpected
while she was on a visit to her father." Rice 1889 p. 49 places Sivaganga "some 30 miles north-east of
Bangalore"; 1923 says north-west.
DISPUTES: (1) Manner: 1889 translation "expired"; 1923 "ended her life"; sallekhana (ritual fast) is a

reading resolves it. (3) Heir: Rice 1889 Intro p. 49: "it is evident that no heir to the throne had
been born to them"; DERRETT p. 56: "Ballala, Visnuvardhana's son, probably by Santala ... until an
untimely death deprived Visnuvardhana of his most valuable subordinate". (4) Sivaganga direction NE vs
NW of Bangalore.
LEGEND layer (not in any record): Shantala as a dancer-queen ("Natyarani"), as the model of the Belur
madanikas, grief over a dead son, sallekhana in protest at the king's conversion. Earliest source not
traced. UNVERIFIED; keep off the page or label ಐತಿಹ್ಯ.

## 7. Gangaraja the Jain patron, and his end

EC II 1923 Intro p. 52 (No. 125 (45) and others), verbatim:
> "As the thunderbolt to Indra, as the plough to Balarama, as the discus to Vishnu, as the spear to
> Subrahmanya, as the bow Gandiva to Arjuna, even so, does Ganga conduct the affairs of king Vishnu."
> ... "The Kondakunda line of the Mula-sangha is the most ancient in the Jaina creed; and the promoter
> of that line is undoubtedly the general Ganga, lay disciple of Subhachandra-siddhanta-deva ... He
> renovated all the Jina temples of Gangavadi; he had the enclosure built around Gommatadeva; driving
> out the Tigulas, he restored Gangavadi to Vira-Ganga; - was not Ganga-Raja a hundred-fold more
> fortunate than that former Raya (Chamunda-Raya) of the Gangas? Wherever he marched, wherever he was
> encamped, wherever his eyes rested, wherever his mind was attracted, there he had Jina temples made;
> and thus the country was everywhere brought through Ganga-Raja to the condition in which it had been
> in days of yore. After Chamunda-Raya he was the chief promoter of the Jaina doctrine."
Same page: "Supernatural power was also attributed to him; for, it is stated that just as the Godavari
stopped flowing on account of the Jaina devotee Attimabbarasi, the Kaveri, though it swelled and
surrounded him, did not touch him". EC II 1889 Intro p. 39 (No. 59): "for now the Kaveri, coming down in
flood, surrounded him and touched his feet as if in obeisance. There is no information to explain
either of these allusions."
DISPUTE: "the former Raya of the Gangas": Narasimhachar 1923 reads Chamunda-Raya; Rice 1889 Intro p. 39
and p. 56, and EC III Intro p. 14, read "the last of the Ganga kings ... Ganga Raja ... under whom the
Ganga sovereignty had been overthrown by the Cholas". The Chavundaraya story already uses the 1923
reading (fact f-gangaraja); keep consistent and disclose.
EC II 1923 Intro p. 51: Jinanathapura "about a mile to the north of Belgola" made by Ganga-Raja; "The
arrow shot by Drohagharatta" ends No. 388. EC II 1889 Intro p. 37: the enclosure lines under the
colossus "fixed as A.D. 1116"; 1923 dates them "about A.D. 1117" (see ganga-verified.md §1a).

Death and memorial. EC II 1923 Intro p. 55: "From E C, V, Belur 124 we learn that Ganga-Raja died in
1133 and that his son Boppa erected the Parsvanatha-basti, naming it Drohagharatta-Jinalaya after one
of the titles of his father, to his memory at Halebid." Bl 124, EC V translation pp. 81–83 (page image
of p. 81 checked: "Date 1133 A. D."), verbatim from p. 83:
> This Jina temple ... did the lord Boppa-Deva erect in the middle of Dorasamudra, the greatest of royal
> cities. As a memorial of Ganga-Raja's death, Boppa set up the god (on the date specified) ... when the
> priests (narar) took the consecrated food (seshe) to Vishnuvarddhana-Deva at Bankapura, - at that
> time, the king Vishnu having defeated and slain Masana, who fell upon him in battle with unequalled
> forces, and seized the whole of his empire, - and to (the queen) Lakshmi-mahadevi a son having been

> forehead, and took the sandal water and consecrated food, saying, "By the merit of the consecration of

> therefore gave to the god the name of Vijaya-Parsva, and to his son the name of Vijaya-Narasimha-Deva.

## 9. Ramanuja and the conversion: what each source says

- No Hoysala inscription read this session names Ramanuja or records a conversion. EC III Intro p. 15
  (above) notes the silence even at Melukote in 1128.
- RICE 1909 p. 99: "An important event in his career was his exchanging the Jain faith for that of
  Vishnu, which took place (before 1116) under the influence of the reformer Ramanuja, who had fled from
  persecution by the Chola king, a rigid Saiva, and taken refuge in the Hoysala country." (scholarship,
  resting on tradition)
- EC II 1889 Intro p. 37: "with the conversion to the Vaishnava faith of the Hoysala king Bitti Deva
  (subsequently called Vishnu-varddhana), in probably this very year [1116], by the reformer
  Ramanujachari, great animosity was excited against the Jainas, albeit they were too powerful to be
  altogether set aside. There are even stories of Ramanujachari's having mutilated the image [the
  colossus] ... This particular form of revenge would easily have suggested itself if it be true, as one
  tradition states, that the change of the king's religion was in some degree brought about by the
  refusal of his Jaina guru to take food with him because the king was mutilated, having lost one of his
  fingers." (legend, as reported by Rice; source of the tradition not named)
- DERRETT Chapter Three, Note 3 (p. 43), OCR (page image not reached), cleaned: "It was long believed
  that the Vaisnava saint Ramanuja ... was driven from the Tamil country by Saivite persecution and took
  refuge with the Hoysala Visnuvardhana, whom he converted to Vaisnavism from Jainism. This tradition is
  in no sense contrary to possibility or even probability. But much of the embroidery upon the
  tradition, namely that Ramanuja converted Visnuvardhana by means of curing his daughter's insanity or
  adverting to certain superstitions of the Jainas which affected the king himself, and that he
  destroyed Jainas and built a tank with the stones, and ground Jainas in oil-mills, such accumulations
  of legend cannot now be substantiated by evidence, and have about them an improbable ring." ...
  "However, the tradition concerning Ramanuja is of respectable antiquity and is mentioned in several
  Kannada works collected by Colonel Mackenzie". DERRETT p. 43 (page image): "Visnuvardhana made a
  corresponding movement towards Vaisnavism, and spent huge sums on the construction of Vaisnava
  temples, such as the famous Cenna-Kesava temple at Belur".
- SASTRI 1958 p. 420: "We may not trust the legends of the persecution to which Ramanuja and his
  followers were subjected in all their details, but the fact remains that he had to withdraw into
  Mysore about 1098 and could not return to Srirangam till 1122. During this time Ramanuja won over the
  Hoysala king Vishnuvardhana from Jainism and established a well-organized matha at Melkote."
- EC III Intro (Sr 80, text line 1620): Melukote clay "discovered by Emberumanar, or Ramanujacharya".
- Ramanuja hagiographies (Guruparampara-prabhavam etc.), their dates, and the earliest text of the
  "daughter cured" story: UNVERIFIED (not reached).
- Depiction: the Belur durbar panel. Commons "KABEL 05 Belur King Vishnuvardana.jpg" (VasuVR, CC BY-SA
  4.0) describes "Queen Shantalu Devi seated to his left, Rajaguru Ramanujachar seated to his right".
  DERRETT Plate II caption (OCR): "Visnuvardhana in Durbar. On an exterior screen of the Cenna-Kesava
  temple at Belur. A queen, possibly Santaladevi, is on the king's left and a minister on his right.
  Whisk-bearers are in the background ... The whole panel ... is described in M.A.R. 1943, pp. 15-19."
  DISPUTE: Ramanuja (modern label) vs a minister (Derrett). Identification of the king himself is
  traditional, not inscribed.
Conclusion for the story: the conversion is `legend` (tradition of respectable antiquity, accepted as
possible by Derrett and as fact by Rice and Sastri); the date (before 1116 / 1116 / 1098–1122) is
disputed; the name-change story is contradicted by MAR 1944 no. 34 (1098) per Derrett; the daughter's
cure, the finger, the persecution and oil-mills are embroidery. Keep Ramanuja off-stage or labelled.

## 11. Disputes, in one list

1. Accession: 1106 (EC V, tentative) / 1108–1109 (Derrett) / 1110 (Sastri); joint rule from 1100.
2. Death: 1141 (Rice, Cm 96) / March 1142 (Derrett) / c. 1152 (Sastri, Aiyangar, Desai).
3. Talakad: 1116 is an inference from 1117 records; no record dates the battle.
4. Kannegal: 1116 (Rice 1909 narrative) vs early 1118 (Derrett); place "near Hassan" vs "north of the
   Hoysala-nad"; mounted (1889 tr.) vs dismounted (Derrett).
5. Adiyama vs Dama/Damodara: one man or two governors.
6. Independence: 1116 "stand erect" (Rice) vs submission 1122–3 and break after 1126 (Sastri); Chalukya
   acknowledgment in No. 384 c. 1135.
7. Conversion: reality, date (before 1116 / 1116 / 1098–1122) and agent; the name Vishnuvardhana in
   1098 (MAR 1944 no. 34, per Derrett) vs name-change story (Rice 1909).
8. Belur 1117: consecration and grant are dated; completion and madanikas later (Derrett); Saturday vs
   Sunday (Bl 58 vs Bl 71); "on renouncing the Jain faith" is Rice's inference.
9. Shantala's death: "expired" (1889) / "ended her life" (1923); sallekhana not stated; reason unknown.
10. Heir by Shantala: none (Rice 1889) vs Ballala "probably by Santala" (Derrett).
11. "former Raya of the Gangas": Chamunda-Raya (1923) vs the last Ganga king (Rice 1889, EC III).
12. The Belur durbar panel: Ramanuja (modern label) vs a minister (Derrett).
13. Sala legend earliest: Bl 58 (1117, dated) vs Bl 171 (Rice "earliest", but dated c. 1160).
14. Six Narayana temples: "is said" (EC V); list not verified.
15. The daughter-cured legend: embroidery per Derrett; earliest text UNVERIFIED.
