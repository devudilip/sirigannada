## 1. Birth, the Tuluva house, Narasa Nayaka, the Saluva usurpation, the accession legend

### 1a. The Tuluva pedigree in the king's own coronation record (EI I pp. 361 to 362, 368)
URL: EI I OCR (above), lines c. 29038 to 29120 and 29600 to 29620.
> "The historical part begins with verse 5: - 'In his (viz., Turvasu's) race shone king Timma, who was
> famous among the princes of Tuluva, just as Krishna shone in the race of Yadu.' From this verse we
> learn, first, that the founder of the second Vijayanagara dynasty was a native of Tulu or Northern
> Malayalam, the country of the Tuluvas. Secondly, he must have been a usurper, as he claims only a
> mythical relationship to the princes of the first dynasty of Vijayanagara."
Pedigree as tabled by Hultzsch: Timma m. Devaki; Isvara m. Bukkama; Narasa or Nrisimha (Saka 1404 and
1418); by Tippaji, Vira-Nrisimha; by Nagala, Krishnaraya.
> (Vv. 13 and 14.) "Just as Rama and Lakshmana (were born) to Dasaratha by his queens Kausalya and the
> glorious Sumitra, (thus) two brave (but) modest sons, prince Vira-Nrisimha and prince Krishnaraya,
> were born to king Nrisimha by his queens Tippaji and Nagala (respectively)."
Tier: inscription (the record), scholarship (Hultzsch's "usurper" inference). Note: Hultzsch writes
"Tulu or Northern Malayalam"; the Tulu country is coastal Karnataka. The dynasty name ತುಳುವ comes from
this verse.

### 1b. Mother's name and the father as "king Narasa" (EI I p. 370; EI IV p. 267)
> "who was the son of Nagambika and of king Narasa" (EI I p. 370, Sanskrit prose of the Hampe record).
> "the son of Nagambika and of king Nrihari" (EI IV p. 267, Sankalapura record, 1513).
TTD 1930 p. 151 reads the Tirumala grant of 13 June 1513 (No. 459 T.T.) as made "for the merit of his
father Narasa-Nayaka-Vodayalu and his mother Nagajammamgaru". Tier: inscription.
Kannada: ತಂದೆ ನರಸ ನಾಯಕ, ತಾಯಿ ನಾಗಲಾದೇವಿ / ನಾಗಾಂಬಿಕೆ.

### 1c. Birth year: tradition only
ASR 1908-09 p. 174:
> "According to Telugu tradition Krishnaraya is stated to have been born on Friday the 12th of the dark
> fortnight of Pushya in the cyclic year Vikriti which corresponded to Saka 1387. This would make him
> about 45 years old when he was crowned, which is very unlikely. ... There is still another tradition
> which says that he was born in Saka 1409 (=A.D. 1487). This latter appears to be nearer the truth as it
> agrees with the statement of Nuniz that the king was over twenty years when he succeeded".
SEXC 1936 p. 182: "An anonymous verse in Telugu which gives the Saka year 1409 or 1487 A.D. as the year
of his birth". Nuniz (l. 9206): "Crisnarao was a man of over twenty years". Tier: legend (birth year
1487 rests on an anonymous verse), consistent with Nuniz. Write "born about 1487 (tradition)".

### 1d. Saluva Narasimha's usurpation (ಸಾಳುವ ನರಸಿಂಹ)
ASR 1908-09 p. 164: "the Saluva usurpation, which put an end to the Samgama line (or, the First dynasty
of the Vijayanagara kings), took place probably about Saka 1408 (= A.D. 1486-87)". Sewell l. 3440 to
3449: an inscription of 1472-73 "speaks of Narasimha as a great lord, but a great lord ONLY, and so does
another of A.D. 1482 - 83. In one of A.D. 1495 - 96, however, he is called 'MAHA-RAYA,' or the 'king.'"
Nuniz (l. 8990 to 9007) gives him a 44-year reign and a deathbed testament naming three unconquered
fortresses: "Rachol, and Odegary and Conadolgi" (Raichur, Udayagiri, Kondavidu: Sewell n. 506).
Tier: scholarship (date) / inscription (titles).

### 1e. Narasa Nayaka (ನರಸ ನಾಯಕ) and the end of the Saluvas
Nuniz (l. 9000 to 9080): Narsymga on his deathbed "sent to call Narsenaque his minister ... he would by
testament leave him to govern the kingdom until the princes should be of an age to rule"; one prince is
murdered by a captain's plot, the other ("Tamarao") is kept at Penukonda; later Narasa, "pondering on
the treason", has the king killed in a garden and "was raised to be king over all the land of Narsymga."
Sewell n. 507 (l. 13611): "This account of the second Narasa and the family relationship differs
altogether from the results obtained from epigraphical study".
ASR 1908-09 pp. 168 to 172: "About Saka 1424 Tamarao appears to have been deprived of even this nominal
power either by Narasa-Nayaka himself or by his son Vira-Narasimha"; Narasa "is referred to as still
living in Saka 1424, Durmati, the month Bhadrapada, whereas in a record from Barakuru, dated in the same
Saka year Durmati, but in the month Magha, we are informed that Vira-Narasingaraya was ruling ...
Consequently, we have perhaps to infer that Narasa died in the latter part of Saka 1424"; and "in Saka
1427 ... we find Immadi-Nrisimha still living".
Tier: Nuniz = inscription (chronicle hearsay, c. 1535); ASR = scholarship. The garden murder is
Nuniz alone and Nuniz himself confuses Narasa with Vira Narasimha. **Dignity note**: Krishnadevaraya
was a teenager or child at the time; nothing in any source makes him party to it. If told at all, tell
it as "the throne passed from the Saluvas to Narasa's family" (ತುಳುವ ವಂಶ) and keep the murder on the
evidence page as Nuniz's report.

### 1f. The accession legend: the brother's order and the she-goat's eyes (Nuniz ch. 4, l. 9188 to 9213)
> "This King died of his sickness in the city of Bisnaga; and before he died he sent for Salvatimya, his
> minister, and commanded to be brought to him his (the King's) son, eight years old, and said to
> Sallvatina that as soon as he was dead he must raise up this son to be king ... and that he must put
> out the eyes of the latter and must bring them to show him ... When he heard this, Crisnarao said that
> he did not seek to be king, nor to be anything in the kingdom, even though it should come to him by
> right; that his desire was to pass through this world as a JOGI (ascetic, recluse), and that he should
> not put his eyes out, seeing that he had not deserved that of his brother. Sallvatina, hearing this,
> and seeing that Crisnarao was a man of over twenty years and therefore more fit to be king ... than
> the son of Busbalrao who was only eight years old, commanded to bring a she-goat, and he put out its
> eyes, and took them to show the King".
ASR 1908-09 p. 173 accepts it as Nuniz's report ("he issued the cruel order that the eyes of his
step-brother Krishnaraya should be put out"). SEXC 1936 p. 185 notes "the chronicle refers to an
attempt on his life by his brother Busbal Rao or Vira Narasimha".
Tier: **Nuniz only** (written c. 1535, about 26 years later, from hearsay at the capital; no Indian
inscription or poem tells it). Treat as `inscription` tier for "Nuniz reports", but tell it in the book
as what the Portuguese chronicler heard. **Dignity**: the episode honours Krishna (he answers without
fear and asks only for justice) and Timmarasu (mercy). Tell it; disclose on the last page that only
Nuniz records it.

The "the king must not see the baby" story (astrologers warning that the infant Krishna endangers the
king): **UNVERIFIED**. Not found in Nuniz, Paes, Sewell, ASR 1908-09, TTD 1930, SVH 1919 (Rayavachakamu
abstract) or SEXC 1936. Do not use until a written source is located (likely 19th to 20th-century
retelling).

### 1g. Timmarasu "Appaji" (ಸಾಳುವ ತಿಮ್ಮರಸು, ಅಪ್ಪಾಜಿ): who he was
ASR 1908-09 p. 183:
> "His able minister and general throughout these victorious campaigns was Saluva-Timma (popularly known
> as Appaji). He was a Brahmana of the Kaundinya-gotra ... It is even supposed that it was in consequence
> of this regard which the king bore for him that Saluva-Timma received the surname Appaji which
> literally means 'the respectful father.' In a record from Bapatla (Guntur district) Saluva-Timma is
> described as Krishnaraya's 'own body' and holds the biruda Dharanivaraha."
Footnote: "a popular Telugu verse on Timmarasa speaks of him thus: 'The best of kings, Krishnaraya called
you "Ayya," father.'"
Paes (l. 7930 to 7936), eyewitness:
> "Salvatinica, who is the principal person that enters the building, supervises the whole, for he
> brought up the king and made him king, and so the king looks on him like a father. Whenever the king
> calls to him he addresses him as 'Lord (SENHOR) Salvatinica,' and all the captains and nobles of the
> realm make salaam to him."
Also Paes l. 7446: "The greatest favourite is an old man called Temersea; he commands the whole
household, and to him all the great lords act as to the king." Sewell n. 404: "TEMERSEA = TIMMARASA".
Tier: inscription (Paes, Bapatla record) / scholarship (etymology of Appaji).

## 2. Accession and coronation

### 2a. Accession: July 1509 (TTD 1930 p. 183 n. 3, from Madras epigraphy records)
> "Krishnaraya's accession to the Vijayanagara throne must have taken place about the 26th July 1509
> A.C., in view of this earliest date furnished for him by the Gulya inscription No. 703 of 1919 and in
> relation to the date of No. 289 of 1915 from Vikravandi ... supplied for Vira-Narasimha's reign, viz.,
> Saka 1431, Sukla, Karkataka Su. 5, ... corresponding to 22nd July 1509 A.C. ... Krishnaraya must
> therefore have ascended the throne within the three days' interval between these two dates ...
> Krishnaraya's formal coronation, however, happened, after the lapse of six months, on the 23rd January
> 1510, as recorded in his Hampe inscriptions."
Tier: scholarship (from inscriptions). Sewell l. 3784: "Probably we shall not be wrong if we consider that
the new king succeeded to the throne in A.D. 1509."

### 2b. The coronation festival record at Hampi (EI I p. 363, p. 370)
> "Lines 27f. of the north face contain the date of the grant, Salivahana-Saka-varsha 1430 expired, the
> Sukla-samvatsara current, Magha Su 14, on which day was the festival of the king's coronation. Though
> no earlier inscriptions of Krishnaraya are known, it is at present impossible to say, whether the real
> day of the coronation or an anniversary of it is intended."
Kannada portion, translation (p. 370): "Hail! On the 14th day of the bright half (of the month) of Magha
of the Sukla-samvatsara, which was current, after the 1430th year of the illustrious ... Salivahana-Saka
had passed, the illustrious maharajadhiraja and rajaparamesvara, the illustrious Virapratapa, the
illustrious Vira-Krishnaraya-maharaya, gave, at the auspicious time of the festival of his coronation
(pattabhisheka) ..."
Gift: the village "Singenayakanahalli" to Virupaksha and "an assembly-hall (ranga-mandapa)". ASR
1908-09 p. 174 adds from the same record that he "built in front of the shrine (of Virupaksha) a large
assembly-hall (ranga-mandapa) and a gopura before it, caused to be repaired the great gopura in front of
that, and gave to the holy Virupakshadeva a golden lotus, set with the nine (kinds of) gems".
Sewell l. 3770 to 3784 explains the cyclic-year mismatch (Sukla = Saka 1431 expired): "then the date
corresponds to 23rd or 24th January A.D. 1510". ASR 1908-09 p. 175 argues the actual crowning was
"some time between the months of Vaisakha and Karttika in the cyclic year Sukla (corresponding to A.D.
1509-10)" and the gifts were recorded later. Tier: inscription. **The record is in Kannada and
Sanskrit** (ಕನ್ನಡ ಭಾಗ), on a stone tablet at the Virupaksha temple, Hampi.

**"Coronation on Krishnajayanti 1509"**: NOT SUPPORTED. No opened source gives Krishna Janmashtami. The
coronation record names Magha Su 14 (January 1510); the accession was July 1509. Do not use.

### 2c. Kannada birudas in the coronation record (EI I p. 363, p. 369)
Hultzsch: "In the Sanskrit portion of the inscription occur two Kanarese birudas ... (bhashege tappuva
rayara ganda ... and maru-rayara ganda)". The verse list (vv. 25 to 27) includes "the Sultan among
Hindu kings" (Hindu-raya-suratrana) and "the double-headed eagle (gandabherunda) which splits the temples
of troops of elephants". The prose calls him one "who, (like) a second Bhoja, knew the mysteries of
poetry, of the drama and of rhetoric". Tier: inscription. Kannada: ಭಾಷೆಗೆ ತಪ್ಪುವ ರಾಯರ ಗಂಡ (champion
over kings who break their word), ಮೂರು ರಾಯರ ಗಂಡ.

### 2d. First acts (Nuniz ch. 5, l. 9217 to 9232)
> "he without delay sent his nephew, son of Busbalrao his brother, together with his own three brothers,
> to a fortress called Chaodegary [Chandragiri] ... he stayed in the city of Bisnaga for a year and a half
> without going outside of it, learning the affairs of the kingdom and looking at the testaments of past
> kings."
Tier: inscription (Nuniz). The "testament of Narsymga" naming Raichur, Mudkal, Udayagiri is Nuniz's
frame for the whole reign (ASR 1908-09 p. 175 repeats it).

## 3. Wars: Ummattur, Udayagiri, Kondavidu, Orissa, Raichur

### 3a. Ummattur and Sivasamudram first
Sewell l. 4041 to 4045: "about this time Krishna Deva Raya was engaged with a refractory vassal in the
Maisur country, the Ganga Rajah of Ummatur ... He captured the strong fortress of Sivasamudra and the
fortress of Srirangapattana". TTD 1930 p. 160 f.: Mysore subdued "by 22nd September 1512 A.C. (No. 180 of
1913 from Bukkapatnam)". SVH 1919 p. 111 (Rayavachakamu): "He then proceeded against the fort of
Sivasamudram which belonged to the chieftainship of Ummattur and fought with Ganga Raja, the chief of
the place. The place was reduced after a single day's fighting." Tier: scholarship (Sewell, TTD) /
legend (Rayavachakamu detail).

### 3b. Udayagiri (ಉದಯಗಿರಿ) taken 9 June 1514; the Balakrishna image
TTD 1930 pp. 170 to 171: the capture of Udayagiri "occurred on the 9th June 1514 A.C. and only twenty-six days
elapsed between Krishnaraya's capture of Udayagiri and his visit to Tirumala." The Tirumala trilingual
records of 6 July 1514 (Nos. 201, 254, 256 T.T.), as quoted in ASR 1908-09 p. 176:
> "started on a military expedition against Prataparudra Gajapati, crushed and pierced (i.e., drove) him
> as far as Kondavidu, took possession of the fortress of Udayagiri, and on his way back to the capital of
> Vijayanagara went up to the top of the Tirumalai hill, paid homage to the lord Venkatanatha, had him
> bathed in gold (kanakabhisheka) with 30,000 gold pieces (varahans) and presented a triple-stringed
> necklace and a pair of gold-bangles of very high value set with pearls, diamonds, rubies and topaz."
ASR 1908-09 p. 176 on the two Hampi records (ARE Nos. 25 and 26 of 1889):
> "The two other records which are engraved on a deserted shrine in the Krishnasvamin temple at
> Krishnapuram refer to the same subject, and speak of Krishnaraya, as having subdued Udayadri (Udayagiri)
> and having thence brought with great care the image of the god Balakrishna which he set up in a jewelled
> [shrine?] (at Krishnapura) on Friday, the 3rd of the bright half of the month of Phalguna in the cyclic
> year Bhava, which corresponded to Saka 1436."
TTD 1930 p. 171 gives the equivalent: "consecrating and installing in the Krishna temple at Hampe the
image of Bala-Krishna which he had very carefully brought from his victorious expedition against
Udayagiri, at the end of the same Saka year 1436, Bhava, on the 16th of February 1515 A.C., when he made a
present of valuable jewels and a gift of nine villages".
Tier: inscription (as reported by two epigraphists; text not opened). **Correction to `vijayanagara.md`**:
Sewell's "built ... in 1513" (l. 4955) is superseded; the image was installed 16 Feb 1515. LONGHURST
1917 p. 20 n.: "This image is now in the Madras Museum." (Government Museum, Chennai; current display
not verified.)
Nuniz (l. 9246 to 9258) says he besieged Udayagiri "for a year and a half", cut roads through rock, and
"captured an aunt of the King of Orya, who was taken captive and carried off with all the courtesy that
he could show her". TTD 1930 p. 172 to 173 rejects the year-and-a-half (the road-cutting belongs to
Kondavidu in the Telugu records). Tier: inscription (Nuniz) / scholarship (TTD correction).
Kumara Dhurjati's *Krishnaraya-Vijayamu* (later poem) has the king escalade the wall himself (TTD 1930
p. 159 n. 1): **legend tier**.

### 3c. Kondavidu (ಕೊಂಡವೀಡು) June 1515 and the Gajapati's son
Sewell n. 206 (l. 12457): "An inscription at Kondavid glorifying Saluva Timma states that he took the
fortress on Saturday, June 23, A.D. 1515". TTD 1930 pp. 162 to 163 (records Nos. 576 to 580 T.T.) summarised: he "alighted
on the Kondavidu fortress, captured the citadel after escalading the walls"; earlier records name the
captives "Prataparudra Gajapatiraya's son Virabhadraraya ... and other Patra-Samantulu ... and granted
them their lives." TTD 1930 p. 157 n.: the Gajapati's son Virabhadraraya accepted service "as governor
of 'the Maleya-Bennur country' ... in ... October 1515 A.C., i.e., within four months after his capture
at Kondavidu (No. 744 of 1917 of the Mad. Ep. Coll.)." Tier: inscription.
After Kondavidu, at Amaravati: "performed the Tulapurusha-mahadana in the temple of Amaresvara and had
the Ratnadhenu-mahadana performed through his favourite queen Chinnadevi and the Saptasagara-mahadana
through his senior queen Tirumaladevi on the 8th July 1515" (TTD 1930 p. 157 n.). LONGHURST 1917 p. 43
says the two queens "appear to have accompanied him during his military campaigns". Tier: inscription
(as reported).

### 3d. Nuniz's Kondavidu battle and the fencing story (Nuniz ch. 5, l. 9270 to 9350)
Nuniz: the Gajapati came with "one thousand three hundred elephants, and twenty thousand horsemen, and
... five hundred thousand foot-soldiers"; Krishna offered to draw back two leagues so the enemy could
cross the river and fight; then forced the crossing and won. Then the captive prince, asked to fence with
a skilled commoner, cried "God forbid that I should soil my hands by touching a man not of the blood
royal," and saying this he slew himself" (l. 9338).
**Conflict**: TTD 1930 p. 163 n. 1: "Only one son of the Gajapati is known, viz., Prince Virabhadra, and,
as already noticed, he was captured at Kondavidu", and Virabhadra was serving as a governor in October
1515. The suicide story is therefore doubtful. **Dignity**: a story that humiliates a captive prince and
rests on Nuniz alone; do not dramatise. What can be told: the king offered the Gajapati an open field
(Nuniz), and spared the captured princes' lives (inscriptions).

### 3e. The victory pillar and the boast inscription
Nuniz (l. 9312 to 9322): at "Symamdary" (Sewell: Rajahmundry) "he commanded to engrave on it an
inscription which says: - 'Perhaps when these letters are decayed, the king of Orya will give battle to
the King of Bisnaga. If the King of Orya erases them, his wife shall be given to the smiths who shoe the
horses of the King of Bisnaga.'" TTD 1930 p. 163: the Telugu-country campaign ended with "a pillar of
victory" at "Simhadri-Potnuru"; AMUKTA I v. 40 names "the pillar planted near Pottunuru" (పొట్టునూరికడ నిల్పిన
కంబము). Tier: inscription (pillar, as reported) / Nuniz for the wording (the actual Potnuru text not
opened; **the insulting clause is Nuniz's report only and should not be quoted as the pillar's text**).

### 3f. Marriage to the Gajapati's daughter; "Annapurna", "Tukka", "Jaganmohini"
Paes (l. 7372 to 7375), eyewitness: "in order to make a treaty and (preserve) peace, the king of Orya gave
him a daughter whom the king of Bisnaga married and has as his wife." Paes l. 7381: "One of these
principal wives is the daughter of the king of Orya". Nuniz (l. 9340 to 9352): Timmarasu advised the
Gajapati to "arrange the marriage of his daughter with the King"; "and with the coming of her they were
friends. And Crisnarao restored the lands on the other side of the river". Tier: inscription (Paes).
Names: AMUKTA I v. 33 (dedicatory verse): "తిరుమల్దేవియును నన్నపూర్ణాదేవియుఁ గమలాబ్జముఖియు దేవేరులుగాన్"
(our rendering: "with Tirumaladevi and Annapurnadevi, lotus-faced, as your queens"; SEXC 1936 reads a
third name "Kamala"; ambiguous). TTD 1930 p. 186 to 190: the king changed Peddana's verse, substituting
Annapurnadevi for Chinnadevi; the Orissa princess is "Tukka" in Kumara Dhurjati's *Krishnaraya-Vijayamu*
(late) and "Jaganmohini" in *Rayavachakamu* (late); whether Annapurna = the Gajapati's daughter is
undecided: "Further evidence is needed to settle the point of time."
The "Tukka Panchakam" (five verses of a neglected wife, said to be hers): TTD 1930 p. 189 n. (note by M.
Ramakrishnakavi): "One of these verses is found in Vallabhadeva's 'Subhashitavali' whose compilation
cannot be dated later than 1400 A.D. One more verse ... is also found quoted by Mukula Bhatta ... a work
of 900 A.D." and "It is also doubtful if Tukka was the name of the daughter of the Gajapati". SEXC 1936
p. 184 records the Andhra tradition that "she plotted against his life" and was abandoned: **legend,
undermined by the verse-borrowing evidence; do not use**.
Tell: the war ended in a marriage alliance with the Gajapati's daughter (Paes). Her name: UNVERIFIED.

### 3g. Raichur (ರಾಯಚೂರು), 1520: the date
Sewell l. 4259 to 4260: "On Saturday morning, May 19, in the year A.D. 1520, according to my
deductions". Basis (l. 4320 to 4360): Paes saw the Mahanavami begin on 12 September and New Year on 12
October; "I find that in no year except A.D. 1520 did this occur", and Paes calls Raichur a city "THIS
KING took it from the Ydallcao" (so Raichur fell before September 1520). Nuniz had written 1522;
Ferishta AH 927 (= 1521). Tier: scholarship (Sewell's date), **disputed**. TTD 1930 p. 157 to 158
records that *Rayavachakamu* and *Krishnaraya-Vijayamu* place a Krishna-river battle **before** the
Udayagiri campaign; Sewell l. 3937 to 3940 (from Ferishta) says Krishna took Raichur "about the year 1512".
Write: "In 1520 (Sewell's reckoning; some place it in 1521)".

### 3h. Why the war began (Nuniz ch. 7, l. 9440 ff.)
The king "sent Cide Mercar with forty thousand PARDAOS to Goa to buy horses"; Cide fled to the Adil Shah
with the money; the Adil Shah would not return him; the council warned "for such a small sum of money as
this it was not well so to act". Tier: inscription (Nuniz).

### 3i. The host (Nuniz ch. 8 to 9; Sewell's verdict)
Nuniz's columns total (Sewell l. 4524) "703,000 foot, 32,600 horse, and 551 elephants". Sewell
l. 4526: "It certainly demands a large strain on our credulity." Nuniz l. 9700: "thirty-two thousand
four hundred horses and five hundred and fifty-one elephants". Nuniz on the camp (l. 9697 to 9712): "no
one who did not understand the meaning of what he saw would ever dream that a war was going on, but would
think that he was in a prosperous city"; the war-drums so loud that birds "used to come down through
terror ... and so they would catch it in their hands; principally kites". The Bijapur muster (l. 9761):
"one hundred and twenty thousand men on foot ... eighteen thousand cavalry, and one hundred and fifty
elephants". Raichur's garrison (l. 9645 ff.): "eight thousand men as garrison and four hundred horse and
twenty elephants, and thirty catapults (TRABUCOS) ... two hundred heavy pieces". Tier: inscription
(Nuniz's figures; chroniclers' numbers, not counts).

### 3j. The battle (Nuniz ch. 9, l. 9810 to 9890), told with dignity
> "Seeing that the dawn of Saturday was now breaking, the drums and trumpets and other music in the King's
> camp began to sound and the men to shout, so that it seemed as if the sky would fall to the earth ...
> it was already two hours after sunrise, and the King ordered an advance of his two forward divisions".
The Bijapur guns opened fire, "it compelled the King's troops to retire", the Bijapur horse pursued
"for about half a league".
> "When the King saw the way in which his troops fled he began to cry out that they were traitors, and
> that he would see who was his side; and that since they all had to die they should meet their fate
> boldly according to custom. 'Who ranges himself with me?' he cried. Immediately there thronged about him
> all those lords and captains that were ready to side with him, and the King said that the day had
> arrived in which the Ydallcao would boast that he had slain in it the greatest lord in the world, but
> that he should never boast that he had vanquished him. Then he took a ring from his finger and gave it
> to one of his pages, so that he might show it to his queens in token of his death ... Then he mounted a
> horse and moved forward with all his remaining divisions".
The counter-charge broke the Bijapur army: "like lost men they leaped into the river".
> "Seeing what passed, I say, the King out of compassion commanded the troops to retire, saying that
> numbers had died who did not deserve death nor were at all in fault" (l. 9874 to 9876).
> "To whom the King answered that many had died who were not to blame; that if the Ydallcao had done him
> wrong, he had already suffered enough" (l. 9885 to 9887).
The Adil Shah "had not even entered into the fight" and fled on an elephant with Asad Khan
("Sefallarym ... he who now calls himself Acadacao and is lord of Belgaum"): "Sire, if thou seekest to
live follow me!" (l. 9895 to 9905).
Tier: inscription (Nuniz; Sewell l. 4731 to 4735 argues the Raichur chapters read "like the account of
an eye-witness"). **Dignity**: Sewell's paraphrase "began to despair of victory" (l. 4273) is Sewell,
not Nuniz; use Nuniz's own words (the rally, the ring). Leave out Nuniz's line that he ordered the
fleeing men slain (l. 9850 to 9851: "commanding to slay without mercy every man of those who had fled"), or
keep it only on the evidence page.

### 3k. Salabat Khan, the spoil, the dead (Nuniz ch. 10, l. 9927 to 9962)
Salabat Khan with "five hundred Portuguese of the renegades" cut to the king's guard; "he seemed like
nothing but a furious wolf amongst sheep"; taken prisoner. Spoil: "four thousand horses of Ormuz, and a
hundred elephants, and four hundred heavy cannon, besides small ones; the number of gun-carriages for
them was nine hundred"; "nor yet of some women, whom the King ordered to be released." "Here the King
stayed till all the dead had been burned, and the customary honours had been paid to them; and here he
gave much alms for the souls of those who had been killed in battle on his side. These numbered sixteen
thousand and odd." Tier: inscription (Nuniz).

### 3l. Figueiredo's muskets and the fall of the fort (Nuniz ch. 10 to 12)
Cristovao de Figueiredo "took with him twenty Portuguese musqueteers"; the defenders were "careless and
free from fear, as men who up to then had never seen men killed with firearms"; the fort's captain
"reached over with his body in front one of the embrasures and was killed with a musquet-shot that struck
him in the middle of his forehead" (l. 10026 to 10028). Surrender after "twenty days since the battle"
(l. 10045). The king to the citizens (l. 10052 to 10055): "he would save all their persons and property";
then, of Figueiredo: "The King, casting his eyes on Christovao de Figueiredo, nodded his head, and turned
to the people telling them to observe what great things could be effected by one good man" (l. 10069 to
10071). On entry (l. 10092 ff.): looting by his troops was stopped, "everything was returned to its
owner", and those who robbed "were soundly chastised". Ch. 13 (l. 10147): "to many who had nothing
wherewith to depart the king commanded to give all that was required for their journey." His letter to
the Deccan lords (l. 10128 to 10136): "I pray you do not take the trouble to come hither, for I will
myself go to seek ye if ye dare to await me in your lands". Tier: inscription (Nuniz).

### 3m. The Adil Shah episode after Raichur (Nuniz ch. 13 to 17, l. 10157 to 10418)
- The envoy "Matucotam" kept waiting a month, then received; the king's terms (l. 10241 to 10245):
  "he would be content to restore everything to the Ydallcao according to his wish, and would be pleased
  at once to release Satabetacao, provided the Ydallcao would come and kiss his foot."
- The king marched to Mudgal, then into Bijapur territory "so desirous was he to meet the Ydallcao; but
  the Ydallcao, after all, dared not meet the King", as far as Bijapur, which his troops stripped of
  wood for cooking fires; to the Adil Shah's protest: "it was not he who had done it, but that he could
  not control his people" (l. 10288).
- Asad Khan, to destroy his rival, persuaded the king that Salabat Khan was the obstacle; the king
  "angrily commanded that Salebatacao ... should be beheaded"; Asad Khan then fled (ch. 16).
- At "Culbergura" (Gulbarga) "the King took three sons of the King of Daquem. He made the eldest King of
  the kingdom of Daquem ... The other two brothers he took with him, and gave them each one an allowance
  ... fifty thousand gold PARDAOS; and he holds them and treats them as princes" (ch. 17, l. 10398 ff.).
  The epithet "Yavanarajya-sthapanacharya" (establisher of the Yavana kingdom) in his records of 1515
  onward (TTD 1930 p. 157) is read by TTD as referring to this; the dates conflict (records of 1515 vs
  Nuniz's post-1520 sequence): **disputed**.
Sewell's judgement (l. 3810 to 3812): "The only blot on his scutcheon is, that after his great success
over the Muhammadan king he grew to be haughty and insolent in his demands" (Sewell's opinion,
scholarship 1900). **Dignity**: tell the "kiss his foot" demand plainly as the price of peace he set;
drop Sewell's moralising; the Salabat Khan execution is a story of the king being deceived by Asad Khan
(tell it that way, or leave it out).

### 3n. Goa: the mainlands and the horses
Castanheda via Sewell (l. 4389 to 4397): the king "wanted that all the horses that came to Goa should come
to him and none to the HIDALCAO, he sent to say to Ruy de Mello, captain of Goa, that he had taken Belgaum
... and that the captain of Goa could enter and take possession of the TANADARIS." Sewell (l. 3950 to
3961): "It appears that in 1514 A.D. Krishna Deva offered Albuquerque [pound sterling] 20,000 for the
exclusive right to trade in horses"; Albuquerque answered "30,000 cruzados per annum". Sewell l. 3929 to 3931:
"Krishna Deva's anxiety was to secure horses ... horses, and again horses". Nuniz ch. 19 (l. 10488):
"On the road King Crisnarao bought six hundred horses from the Portuguese". Nuniz (Achyuta's reign,
l. 11022): "The King every year buys thirteen thousand horses of Ormuz, and country-breds". Nuniz on
Narsymga (l. 9000 to 9002): horses bought "dead or alive at three for a thousand PARDAOS, and of those that died
at sea they brought him the tail only, and he paid for it just as if it had been alive." Federici (1567)
via Sewell l. 6333: "The Merchandize that went every yeere from Goa to Bezeneger were Arabian Horses,
Velvets, Damaskes, and Sattens". Tier: scholarship (Sewell from Portuguese chronicles) / inscription
(Nuniz, Federici).

### 3o. Battles he lost (for "never lost a battle")
Sewell ch. 10 (l. 4110 to 4160), summarising a Golkonda history in Briggs' Ferishta vol. III: at Pangul
"a battle took place in which the Qutb Shah was victorious"; near Bellamkonda "the Sultan counter-marched,
and suddenly appeared in rear of the Hindu army. In the battle which ensued he was victorious and the
siege was raised". Sewell: "We are left, therefore, entirely in the dark as to the exact years". Against
this, ASR 1908-09 p. 183: "Krishnaraya's rule was one of continuous warfare in which as we have seen, he
was ever successful." Tier: Golkonda chronicle (late, partisan) vs scholarship (1912). Plus Nuniz's own
Raichur account: the first line broke. Write "he won every great campaign he led" only with care; never
"never lost a battle".

## 4. The city and the court as Paes saw it (c. 1520 to 1522)

### 4a. The king's person (Paes, l. 7356 to 7368)
> "This king is of medium height, and of fair complexion and good figure, rather fat than thin, he has on
> his face signs of small-pox. He is the most feared and perfect king that could possibly be, cheerful of
> disposition and very merry; he is one that seeks to honour foreigners, and receives them kindly, asking
> about all their affairs whatever their condition may be. He is a great ruler and a man of much justice,
> but subject to sudden fits of rage, and this is his title - 'Crisnarao Macacao, king of kings, lord of
> the greater lords of India, lord of the three seas and of the land.' ... so gallant and perfect is he in
> all things."
Tier: inscription (eyewitness). TTD 1930 p. 185 n. 2 argues against "fat": "he could not obviously have
put on 'fat' with his daily consumption of gingelly oil and immediate recourse to physical exercises",
citing Peddana's address "Vyayama-sthira-sandhibandha" (*Manucharitramu* VI.121: firm-jointed by
exercise). TTD 1930 p. 154 n. 2 notes *Manucharitramu* IV.121 may imply a dark complexion (poetic).
**Owner rule (handsome, strong, never fat)**: the 1518 bronze shows a slim, strong figure; follow the
bronze. The small-pox marks: Paes only; may be left out or rendered as faint marks, never as
disfigurement.

### 4b. The daily routine (Paes, l. 7428 to 7440)
> "This king is accustomed every day to drink QUARTILHO (three-quarter pint) of oil of GINGELLY before
> daylight, and anoints himself all over with the said oil; he covers his loins with a small cloth, and
> takes in his arms great weights made of earthenware, and then, taking a sword, he exercises himself with
> it till he has sweated out all the oil, and then he wrestles with one of his wrestlers. After this labour
> he mounts a horse and gallops about the plain in one direction and another till dawn, for he does all
> this before daybreak. Then he goes to wash himself, and a Brahman washes him whom he holds sacred ...
> and after he is washed he goes to where his pagoda is inside the palace, and makes his orisons".
Then business in a pillared hall "hung with cloths right up to the top". Tier: inscription. Gingelly =
sesame oil (Sewell n. 403). Kannada: ಎಳ್ಳೆಣ್ಣೆ.

### 4c. Audience and dress (Paes, l. 7452 to 7492)
Nobles "place their hands in the sleeves of their tunics (CABAYAS) and cast their eyes on the ground";
"The salaam ... is that they put their hands joined above their head as high as they can."
Figueiredo's reception: "We were so close to the king that he touched us all and could not have enough
of looking at us." The king "was clothed in certain white cloths embroidered with many roses in gold,
and with a PATECA of diamonds on his neck of very great value, and on his head he had a cap of brocade in
fashion like a Galician helmet, covered with a piece of fine stuff all of fine silk, and he was
barefooted". The parting gift to Figueiredo: "a CABAYA (tunic) of brocade, with a cap of the same fashion
as the king wore". At the festival (l. 7956 to 7959): "dressed in white clothes all covered with
(embroidery of) golden roses and wearing his jewels - he wears a quantity of these white garments, and I
always saw him so dressed". Tier: inscription.

### 4d. The city (Paes, l. 7605 to 7630)
> "What I saw from thence seemed to me as large as Rome, and very beautiful to the sight; there are many
> groves of trees within it, in the gardens of the houses, and many conduits of water which flow into the
> midst of it, and in places there are lakes (TAMQUES)".
> "The people in this city are countless in number, so much so that I do not wish to write it down for
> fear it should be thought fabulous".
> "This is the best provided city in the world".
Also (l. 7600 to 7603): the Moorish quarter "at the very end of the city, and of these Moors there are
many who are natives of the country and who are paid by the king and belong to his guard. In this city
you will find men belonging to every nation and people". The palace enclosure "encloses a greater space
... than all the castle of Lisbon" (l. 7554). Tier: inscription.

### 4e. Nagalapura (ನಾಗಲಾಪುರ, Hospet)
Paes (l. 7280 to 7300): at the western entrance "this king has made within it a very strong city fortified
with walls and towers"; (l. 7339) "This new city that the king made bears the name of his wife for love of
whom he made it". Paes (l. 7381 to 7387): "another wife is a courtezan whom in his youth he had for
mistress before he became king ... For love of her he built this new city". Nuniz (l. 10540 to 10556):
"called its name Nagallapor ... one principal street, of length four thousand and seven hundred paces and
of breadth forty, which is certainly the most beautiful street it is possible to see".
**Dating**: Nagalapuri already existed on 20 September 1513 (EI IV p. 267, Sankalapura record: the
granted village lay "on the east of Nagalapuri"; Hultzsch: "The site of the western boundary, Nagalapuri,
is now occupied by the town of Hosapete"). **Name**: SEXC 1936 p. 185: "the mother of Krishnaraya was
Nagambika and the city should be presumed to be so called in her memory rather in honour of the courtesan
princess." **Disputed**: Paes and Nuniz say his wife; the king's mother was Nagala (§1a). Tell: "the new
town of Nagalapura" without choosing; disclose both readings on the evidence page.

### 4f. The House of Victory and the Mahanavami (Paes, l. 7795 to 7935), eyewitness, September 1520
> "This building stands on pillars shaped like elephants and with other figures, and all open in front,
> and they go up to it by staircases of stone ... This house is called the House of Victory, as it was
> made when the king came back from the war against Orya, as I have already told you."
Figueiredo's party sat "On the upper platform, close to the king". The throne: "it is all covered with
silk cloths, and has lions all of gold". The morning rite: eleven horses "with handsome and well-arranged
trappings, and behind them are four beautiful elephants"; a Brahman brings "a basket full of white roses
... and the king, taking three handfuls of these roses, throws them to the horses"; then "the slaughter
of twenty-four buffaloes and a hundred and fifty sheep"; the king "takes the cap from his head, and after
placing it on the ground turns back (to the place) where the idol is; here he lies extended on the
ground"; he throws into a fire "a powder made up of many things, namely, rubies and pearls". "This is
what is done during the morning of each day of these nine days". Afternoons: wrestlers, dancing-women,
elephants. Seated with him: "the king of Syrimgapatao ... called Cumarvirya". Tier: inscription.
LONGHURST 1917 identifies the House of Victory with the stone platform "known now as the Dasara Dibba or
Mahanavami Dibba" (ಮಹಾನವಮಿ ದಿಬ್ಬ). Tier: scholarship. The festival dates (begins 12 September) are
Paes's own (Sewell l. 4319).

### 4g. The review of the army (Paes, l. 8130 to 8195)
Horses "fully caparisoned, and on their foreheads plates, some of silver but most of them gilded, with
fringes of twisted silk"; trappings "of Mecca velvet"; riders in "quilted tunics ... made of layers of
very strong raw leather, and furnished with other iron (plates)"; helmets "with borders covering the
neck, and each has its piece to protect the face"; elephants "with bells so that the earth resounds; and
on their heads are painted faces of giants"; shields "with many flowers of gold and silver ... others with
figures of tigers ... others black and (so polished that) you can see into them as into a mirror";
archers with "bows plated with gold and silver"; "musqueteers with their musquets and blunderbusses and
their thick tunics"; the Moors "with their shields, javelins, and Turkish bows, with many bombs and spears
and fire-missiles". The king "riding ... clothed in the many rich white cloths ... with two umbrellas of
state all gilded and covered with crimson velvet"; "the hills and valleys and all the ground trembled with
the discharges of arms and musquets". Paes: "I went along with my head so often turned from one side to
the other that I was almost falling backwards off my horse with my senses lost." Tier: inscription.

### 4h. The palace women (Paes, l. 7405 to 7412)
"they say that there are twelve thousand women; for you must know that there are women who handle sword
and shield, and others who wrestle, and others who blow trumpets". Queens "carried in litters shut up and
closed". Tier: inscription (hearsay within Paes: "they say").

### 4i. Barbosa (finished 1516; Sewell l. 3955 to 4034, from the Hakluyt translation)
"The king constantly resides in the before-mentioned palaces, and very seldom goes out of them"; "the king
keeps at all times nine hundred elephants and more than twenty thousand horses, all which elephants and
horses are bought with his own money"; "When the king dies four or five hundred women burn themselves
with him." Sewell n. 202: Barbosa "finished his work in 1516". Tier: inscription (traveller; may describe
the years before 1509). **Correction**: the line "The king wears a cap of gold brocade two spans long ...
His horse is worth more than some of our cities" (l. 3725) is **Varthema** (c. 1505, King Narasimha), not
Barbosa (Sewell n. 409, l. 13258: "This was Krishna Deva's predecessor, Narasimha").

## 5. The king as poet and patron

### 5a. Amuktamalyada: the dream at Srikakulam (AMUKTA I vv. 11 to 17)
Prose before v. 12 (our rendering of the Telugu, verbatim Telugu given): "అని యిష్టదేవతావందనంబుఁ జేసి మున్నేఁ
గళింగదేశ విజిగీషామనీషం దండెత్తిపోయి విజయవాటిం గొన్నివాసరంబు లుండి శ్రీకాకుళనికేతనుం డగు నాంధ్రమధుమథను
సేవింపంబోయి హరివాసరోపవాసం బచ్చటఁ గావింప నప్పుణ్యరాత్ర చతుర్థయామంబున." = "Earlier, marching out with
the wish to conquer the Kalinga country, I stayed some days at Vijayavata [Vijayawada], went to serve the
Andhra Vishnu who dwells at Srikakulam, kept the Ekadashi fast there, and in the fourth watch of that holy
night ..."
v. 13 (the god speaks): he lists the king's Sanskrit works (Madalasa-charitra, Satyavadhu-prinanam,
Sakala-kathasara-sangraham, Jnana-chintamani, Rasamanjari) and asks: "అంధ్రభాష యసాధ్యంబె యందు నొక్క కృతి
వినిర్మింపు మిఁక మాకుఁ బ్రియముఁ గాఁగ" ("is the Andhra tongue beyond you? compose one work in it now, to please
us").
v. 14 ends: "నేను దెలుంగురాయఁడ న్గన్నడ రాయ!" (our rendering: "I am the Telugu king, O Kannada king!").
v. 15: "తెలుఁ గ దేల యన్న, దేశంబు దెలుఁ గేను / దెలుఁగు వల్లభుండఁ దెలుఁగొకండ / యెల్ల నృపులు గొలువ నెఱుఁగవే
బాసాడి / దేశభాషలందుఁ దెలుఁగు లెస్స." (our rendering: "If you ask why Telugu: the land is Telugu, I am the
Telugu lord, Telugu is sweet; serving all the kings, have you not known them speak? Among the languages of
the land, Telugu is best.")
v. 17: he wakes, bows to the temple tower, and (v. 18 prose) tells the dream to the assembled scholars.
SEXC 1936 p. 187 on v. 14: "The distinction drawn in the previous verse between Telugu Raya and Kannada
Raya can only mean that Krishnaraya was lord of the Kannada country while the God by tradition was the
lord of the Telugu country." Tier: inscription (the king's own poem; a literary dream, not an event
record). **Kannada note**: the god of the poem calls the king ಕನ್ನಡ ರಾಯ.

### 5b. The king's verses on kingship (AMUKTA IV, raja-niti)
Context: in canto IV the poem's king Yamunacharya, about to renounce, hands the realm to his son and
teaches him (vv. 203 to 285). These are the author's verses, spoken by a character.
- v. 204: "ఏపట్టున నిసువక రక్షాపరుఁడవు గమ్ము ప్రజలచక్కి; విపన్నుల్గూపెట్టిన విని తీర్పుము; కాపురుషులమీఁద
  విడకు కార్యభరంబుల్." (our rendering: "Never weary, be ever the protector of the people; when the
  distressed cry out, hear them and settle it; do not leave the burden of affairs to base men.")
- v. 205: "రాష్ట్రవర్ధన మెదఁ గోరు రాజు మేలు రాష్ట్రమును గోరు" (our rendering: "the king who wishes his
  realm to grow should wish the realm's welfare").
- v. 285 (prose, the closing charge): "పట్టబద్ధుండగు రాజు ధర్మంబునంద దృష్టి యిడి నడవవలయు" (our rendering:
  "a crowned king must walk with his eye fixed on dharma"). This is the source of the brief's line "a
  crowned king should always rule with an eye to dharma"; the wording there is a paraphrase.
Tier: inscription (author's text).

### 5c. His title "emperor of the battlefield of letters"
AMUKTA I v. 44 (the scholars' address): "సాహితీసమరాంగణసార్వభౌమ కృష్ణరాయేంద్ర" (Sahiti-samarangana-
sarvabhauma). Tier: inscription (contemporary poem; panegyric).
EI I p. 370 (coronation record): "(like) a second Bhoja, knew the mysteries of poetry, of the drama and of
rhetoric". ASR 1908-09 p. 175: "earned the title, 'a second Bhoja'". TTD 1930 p. 184 n.: "Prativarsha-
Vasantotsava", an annual spring assembly of poets, from the inscription and *Parijatapaharanamu*.

### 5d. When was Amuktamalyada written?
TTD 1930 p. 186 n. 3: written "about 1519-20 A.C." (inferred from the queens' names). Its canto
colophons celebrate the Kalinga war (TTD). Tier: scholarship.

### 5e. The Ashtadiggajas (ಅಷ್ಟದಿಗ್ಗಜರು): later canon
CHENCHIAH 1928 p. 72: the eight "have become famous. They are: 1. Allasani Peddana. 2. Nandi Timmanna.
3. Ayyala Raju Ramabhadriah. 4. Dhurjati. 5. Madiahgari Mallana. 6. Pingali Suranna. 7. Ramaraja
Bhushana. 8. Tenali Ramakrishna. It is almost certain that the last three poets did not write in the reign
of Krishnadevaraya". Tier: scholarship. Securely at his court: Peddana (*Manucharitramu*) and Timmana
(*Parijatapaharanamu*), both dedicated to him (SEXC 1936 p. 186; TTD 1930 p. 186). Mallana: *Rajasekhara-
charitramu* dedicated to Appa of Nadendla (ASR 1908-09 p. 183). The "eight" as a fixed group: no
contemporary record found: **legend/later canon**.
SEXC 1936 p. 186 also reports verses "ascribed to Peddanarya wherein the king is described as having
lifted the poet's palanquin on his shoulders": **legend** (stray verses).

### 5f. Tenali Rama (ತೆನಾಲಿ ರಾಮ)
CHENCHIAH 1928 p. 83: "An enigmatic figure of the time was Ramakrishna, who was attached to the court of
Venkatapati Raya, one of the Vijayanagar kings, who changed the capital of the empire to Chandragiri ...
He is known to posterity, not so much as a poet, but as a court jester". p. 26: "It was during his [Rama
Raya's] reign that a trio of great poets, Pingali Suranna, Ramaraja Bhushana and Tenali Ramakrishna
existed." Tier: **legend** for any Krishnadevaraya and Tenali story; the poet (author of
*Panduranga Mahatmyam*) is real but dated after 1529 by this history.

### 5g. Vyasatirtha (ವ್ಯಾಸತೀರ್ಥ): dated
TTD 1930 p. 165: record No. 246 T.T., "12th January 1524 A.C." "registers the transference of two houses
and house-sites at Tirumala and Tirupati ... to the Madhva teacher Srimat Vyasatirtha-Sripada-Udaiyar"
and "later on Krishnaraya ordered the issue of a part of the donor's share to Vyasatirtha". ASR 1908-09
p. 186: Vyasatirtha "was a contemporary of Krishnaraya and was the recipient of many rich gifts from that
king". Tier: inscription. The *Vyasavijaya* story that Vyasatirtha sat on the throne during the "Kuhu
yoga" to save the king (SEXC 1936 p. 50): **legend** (hagiography).

### 5h. Purandaradasa (ಪುರಂದರದಾಸ) at his court: UNVERIFIED
SEXC 1936 calls him "A disciple of Vyasaraya, a writer and singer of great repute in the time of
Krishnadevaraya" (p. 238) and says Vyasatirtha's disciples Purandaradasa and Kanakadasa composed in
Kannada (p. 50). No inscription or contemporary record of Purandara at Krishnadevaraya's court was found.
Tier: scholarship (contemporaneity) / **UNVERIFIED** (presence at court). Do not stage a court scene.

### 5i. Kannada at his court
TTD 1930 p. 184: "Under his patronage Timmanna rendered 'Mahabharata' into Kanarese in Shatpadi metre."
SEXC 1936 p. 238: Timmanna, "Karnata Kavi Sarvabhauma", completed the Kannada Mahabharata left unfinished
by Kumara Vyasa "and dedicated it to Krishnadevaraya". Tier: scholarship. His gifts at Tirumala "are
generally recorded in the three South Indian languages Telugu, Tamil and Kanarese" (TTD 1930 p. 150);
the coronation grant at Hampi has a Kannada portion (EI I). Tier: inscription.

### 5j. Music
TTD 1930 p. 184: a music treatise *Sangita-suryodaya* "was written at his instance and guidance by
Lakshmana". Tier: scholarship (manuscript information from M. Ramakrishnakavi).

## 6. Building

### 6a. Virupaksha (ವಿರೂಪಾಕ್ಷ) at the coronation: ranga-mandapa and gopura (EI I; ASR 1908-09 p. 174). See §2b.
Sewell l. 3826 to 3829 (local tradition, not a record): "the present head of the Brahmanical establishment
at the Hampe temple informed me that Krishna Deva Raya celebrated his accession by erecting the great
tower". Tier: inscription (mandapa, gopura) / legend (which tower).

### 6b. Krishna temple (ಕೃಷ್ಣ ದೇವಾಲಯ / ಬಾಲಕೃಷ್ಣ ದೇವಾಲಯ), Hampi: image installed 16 Feb 1515. See §3b.
Commons "Krishna-temple-Hampi.jpg" description says "built ... in 1513": an uploader's statement,
superseded by the records.

### 6c. Hazara Rama (ಹಜಾರ ರಾಮ)
Sewell l. 4957 to 4959: "In the same year he commenced the temple of HAZARA RAMASVAMI at the palace, the
architecture of which leads Mr. Rea to think that it was not finished till a later period." LONGHURST 1917
p. 71: "begun (as the inscriptions within testify) by the great Krishna Deva Raya in 1513". Tier:
scholarship (1900, 1917). `vijayanagara.md` reports a modern attribution to Deva Raya I (Fritz and Michell
2016): **not verified this pass**. Mark **disputed**; say "associated with Krishnadevaraya's reign".

### 6d. Vitthala (ವಿಠ್ಠಲ)
LONGHURST 1917 p. 124: "In and about the temple are no less than 23 inscriptions of dates ranging from A.D.
1513 to 1564. ... those which are still legible show that Krishna Deva Raya ... began the temple in 1513
and endowed it with villages; that his two queens built the gateways and presented golden vessels to the
shrine; and that his two successors Achyuta and Sadasiva ... made gifts". "The temple was never finished
nor consecrated" (Longhurst's view). Tradition (same page): built for the Vithoba of Pandharpur, who
"refused to move, saying that it was too grand for him": **legend**. Sewell l. 5013 to 5014: "An
inscription records a grant to the temple in 1561." Tier: scholarship (Longhurst summarising records).

### 6e. The Narasimha monolith (ಉಗ್ರ ನರಸಿಂಹ / ಲಕ್ಷ್ಮೀ ನರಸಿಂಹ), 1528, and the last dated record
EI I p. 398 to 399 (Krishnapura inscription): "Vira-Krishnaraya-maharaya gave two villages to the image of
Lakshmi-Narasimhadeva, which he had caused to be consecrated in the village of Krishnapura by Arya Krishna
Bhatta". Consecration: "Thursday, the 2nd April, A.D. 1528" (Fleet's calculation). Grant: "Friday, the 23rd
April, A.D. 1529", an eclipse day; "this is the latest known date of Krishnaraya" (in 1892). Tier:
inscription.

### 6f. The great tank and Joao della Ponte (Nuniz, l. 10557 to 10586; Paes l. 7290 to 7320)
Nuniz: "he sent to Goa to ask the Governor to send some Portuguese masons, and the Governor sent him Joao
della Ponte, a great worker in stone ... asked him to have lime prepared, at which the King laughed much,
for in his country when they build a house they do not understand how to use lime." The bank "was a
crossbow-shot in breadth and length"; "he gave the people the lands which are irrigated by this water free
for nine years". Paes: "I saw so many people at work that there must have been fifteen or twenty thousand
men, looking like ants". Sewell (l. 4968 to 4980) identifies it with the dry tank south-west of Hospet.
Tier: inscription (both chroniclers) / scholarship (identification).
**Dignity flag**: both say the dam kept failing and men were beheaded to appease the deity (Paes l. 7313:
"the heads of sixty men"; Nuniz l. 10575: "all the men who were his prisoners, and who deserved death").
Foreign hearsay of a sacrifice; do not depict; mention, if at all, only on the evidence page as what the
Portuguese wrote.

### 6g. Irrigation 1521
Sewell l. 4962 to 4966: "He constructed in 1521 the great dam and channel at Korragal, and the Basavanna
channel, both of which are still in use" (n. 259: Kelsall, *Bellary District Manual* p. 231). Tier:
scholarship (1900); present use not checked.

### 6h. Tirumala gifts: seven visits (TTD 1930 pp. 150 to 166)
- Nearly fifty records of the king and his two queens, "between Saka 1434 (=1513 A.C.) and Saka 1445
  (=1524 A.C.)" (p. 150).
- 1st visit 10 Feb 1513 with both queens: a gold crown set with nine gems; each queen a gold cup.
- 2nd 2 May 1513; 3rd 13 June 1513 (five villages, for the merit of his parents).
- 4th 6 July 1514, after Udayagiri: kanakabhisheka with 30,000 varahas.
- 5th 2 Jan 1517: 30,000 varahas "for gilding the vimanam over the sanctum"; gilding complete by 9 Sept
  1518 (No. 576 T.T.).
- 6th 16 Oct 1518 with Tirumaladevi: land granted "for the merit of (Prince) Tirumalaraya Maharaya"
  (No. 697 of 1922, Kamalapuram), the son born that year.
- 7th 17 Feb 1521: a pitambara set with nine gems and "a cap (kullayi) with pearls, diamonds, emeralds and
  sapphires".
Tier: inscription (as calendared by the TTD epigraphist). Sewell does not count visits; "seven visits"
is TTD's count.

## 7. The son, Timmarasu, the last years, Achyuta, death

### 7a. The prince Tirumalaraya (ತಿರುಮಲರಾಯ): crowned in his father's lifetime
Nuniz ch. 18 (l. 10425 to 10436):
> "he determined to make him King during his lifetime, the boy being six years old ... he abdicated his
> throne and all his power and name, and gave it all to his son, and himself became his minister, and
> Salvatinica who had held that office became his counsellor ... And so far did King Crisnarao go that
> after he had given the kingdom to his son, he himself did obeisance to him. With these changes the King
> made great festivals which lasted eight months, during which time the son of the King fell sick of a
> disease of which he died."
**Inscriptions confirm the crowning**: ASR 1908-09 p. 186 n.: "No. 139 of the Epigraphical collection for
1896, speaks of a son of Krishnaraya, named Tirumalaraya-deva-Maharayar as 'ruling the earth' in Saka 1446
... Tirumalaraya-deva-Maharaya, the son of Krishnaraya, again figures in two records from the Bangalore
district (Ep. Carn., Vol. IX, Ma. 6 and 82) both of which are dated in Saka 1446." Born 1518 (TTD, §6h);
six in 1524. Tier: inscription. **Goosebump**: Nuniz's story of the father bowing to his crowned son is
borne out by stones that call a six-year-old "ruling the earth".

### 7b. The accusation and Timmarasu's fate (Nuniz ch. 18, l. 10437 to 10477; the speech from l. 10445)
> "After his death Crisnarao learned that his son had died by poison given him by the son of
> Sallvatinica ... 'I held thee always as my great friend, and now for these forty years thou hast been
> governor in this kingdom, which thou gavest me ... Thou wert bound, since thy lord the King my brother
> commanded so, to put out mine eyes; yet thou didst not carry out his will nor obey him, but instead thou
> didst cheat him and the eyes of a goat were put out, wherefore, since thou didst not fulfil his command,
> thou wert a traitor, and thy sons with thee for whom I have done so much.'"
He seized them "with the aid of many Portuguese"; they "remained three years in prison"; the son escaped,
was recaptured, and the king "sent them to the place of executions and there had their eyes put out, for
in this country they do not put Brahmans to death". 
**Doubt**: SEXC 1936 p. 185: "Historians have discredited the testimony of Nuniz on various grounds. An
inscription in Tirumalaraya's name in 1524 A.D., one of Saluva Govindaraja in 1529 A.D., paucity of
material to conclude in favour of Krishnaraya's abdication or Saluva Timma's treason and the lack of
tradition in relation to such an important occurrence ... have pointed to a contrary view." Sewell
(l. 4850 to 4862) accepts Nuniz. Tier: Nuniz (sole source, hearsay, written under Achyuta) / **disputed**.
**Dignity**: the only source is a single foreign chronicle, and it shows the king at his harshest. What
can be told with dignity: the prince's death (inscriptions plus Nuniz), the father's grief, a breach with
the old minister reported by Nuniz; the blinding goes on the evidence page as "Nuniz reports; historians
doubt it".

### 7c. Last campaign and illness (Nuniz ch. 19, l. 10482 to 10518)
The Adil Shah marched on Raichur; the king "without even telling any one, ordered to saddle a horse, and
he rode at full speed in the direction of Rachol ... but as soon as his enemy was aware of the coming of
the King he fled." He planned to take Belgaum and asked Goa for help, "He promised him that after taking
Billgao he would give him the mainland". Then "he presently fell sick of the same illness of which all his
ancestors had died, with pains in the groin". Tier: inscription (Nuniz). ASR 1908-09 p. 183: "In the last
days of his reign the king again made a vigorous attempt at securing the Adil Shah but died before he
could actually besiege Belgaum".

### 7d. The will and Achyuta (ಅಚ್ಯುತ ದೇವರಾಯ) (Nuniz ch. 20, l. 10614 to 10624)
> "being sick and already despairing of his life, he made a will, saying that of his three brothers whom
> ... he had sent to be confined in the fortress of Chamdegary with his nephew ... they should make King
> his brother Achetarao who now reigns; for the latter seemed to him to be better fitted for that than any
> of the others, for the reason that he himself had no son of fit age for the throne, but only one of the
> age of eighteen months."
Tier: inscription (Nuniz). **Hostile source on Achyuta**: Nuniz wrote under Achyuta and calls him "a man
of very little honesty" (l. 10629); keep Achyuta's portrait neutral.

### 7e. Death: 1529
ASR 1908-09 p. 186: "A traditionary Telugu verse supposed to be the composition of Alasani-Peddana refers
to the passing away of Krishnaraya in Saka 1447, Parthiva. But evidence from inscriptions clearly makes his
reign extend to the beginning (Vaisakha) of Saka 1452, Virodhin, in which year his half brother
Achyutaraya is also stated to have been crowned king of Vijayanagara." Latest dated record: 23 April 1529
(EI I, §6e). Sewell l. 4869 to 4872 (from Nuniz's age): "died shortly afterwards at the age of from
forty-two to forty-five years. It was then the year 1530 A.D." (Sewell's 1530 is superseded). Achyuta's
earliest record known to Sewell: "Monday, August 15, A.D. 1530" (l. 5061; EI IV p. 3 n. 4). Day of death:
the Honnenahalli record (Commons file, photo 2020, CC BY-SA 4.0) is claimed to give it; **UNVERIFIED**
(no published reading opened). Write "died in 1529".
After his death (Ferishta via Sewell l. 5074 to 5083): "Roijore and Mudkul were taken ... after they had
been in possession of the infidels for seventeen years." Tier: Ferishta (c. 1600).

## 9. Visual bible

### 9a. The Tirumala bronze group: the real face (our reference)
Commons files (metadata via API, 2026-10-15):
- "Bronze statues of King Krishnadevaraya and his queen consorts, Chinna Devi and Tirumala Devi.jpg",
  https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bronze_statues_of_King_Krishnadevaraya_and_his_queen_consorts,_Chinna_Devi_and_Tirumala_Devi.jpg ,
  1180 x 1626, licence **PD-India** ("Public domain"), source "The Tirupati Devasthanam Epigraphical Report
  (1930)", date 1930. The description's "installed ... in 1518, during his fourth visit ... in October"
  is the uploader's; see dating below.
- "Annual Report of the Archaeological Survey of India 1911-12 Plate 76, Fig 1, 2, 3 (page 352 crop).jpg",
  PD-India-URAA, ASI (ed. John Marshall), Calcutta 1915: an older photograph of the same group.
- "Metal images of Krishnaraya and his two queens.jpg", PD, from SEXC 1936 (archive.org
  in.ernet.dli.2015.282482).
- "Chinnadevi, Krishnadevaraya, Tirumaladevi statues at Chandragiri Museum.jpg", CC BY-SA 4.0, 2022
  (a modern copper-coloured replica; useful for the back and sides).
Identification (TTD 1930 p. 185): "Chinnadeviyaru on the right, Krishnaraya Maharayaru in the middle and
Tirumaladeviyaru on the left, with their names incised on their shoulders." (Right and left are the
figures' own; check against the photograph before labelling.) Tier: inscription (names on the images).
Dating: TTD 1930 p. 185 n. 2 infers installation "on or before the 2nd January 1517" (the 5th visit),
from *Rayavachakamu* and the fact that Chinnadevi is absent from records after 1517; the Commons "October
1518" is not supported by TTD (Chinnadevi is missing at the 16 Oct 1518 visit). **Disputed: write
"c. 1517".** TTD: "they must be expected to give a true and genuine representation of the three royal
personages".
What the photograph shows (viewed this session): the king stands frontally on a lotus pedestal, palms
joined in anjali at the chest. Tall conical cap (kullayi) with vertical ribs or pleats, slightly
narrowing to a rounded top, a plain band at the brow. Oval face, broad smooth forehead, long straight
nose, heavy-lidded almond eyes looking down, full lips with a faint calm smile, **clean-shaven, no
moustache**. Long neck; a two-strand necklace with a small pendant. **Bare chest and arms**, slim and
firm torso (not fat), upper-arm bands and wrist bangles. A jewelled waist-belt with a hanging central
ornament and fan-pleated cloth ends at the front; a close-fitting lower garment to the ankles; anklets;
bare feet. **Not a long tunic**: the brief's "long tunic" is the court dress Paes describes (CABAYA), not
what the bronze shows. Queens: high round hair-knot or bun, ear ornaments, necklaces, a cloth over the
breast and shoulder, armlets, jewelled girdle, ankle-length lower garment with a fan of pleats at the
feet, palms joined.
SEXC 1936 p. 79 (chapter on the Lepakshi paintings) on the cap: "the headgear which is typically like that of Krishnadevaraya (bronze statue)
at Tirupati ... this kind of cap was popular about the time ... the cap is a type for the wear of
dignitaries like the Emperor and his nobles." Federici (1567, Sewell l. 6339): "long Hats on their heads
called Colae" (Sewell n. 337: "KULLAYI").

### 9b. Court dress (Paes)
King: white cloths embroidered with golden roses; diamond neck ornament; "a cap of brocade in fashion like
a Galician helmet"; barefoot indoors (§4c). Nobles and captains: tunics (CABAYAS), hands in the sleeves,
eyes down; the salaam with joined hands above the head. Gift robes: brocade tunic and cap. Footwear (Paes
l. 7481): "The shoes have pointed ends, in the ancient manner". Commoners (Varthema, c. 1505): "go quite
naked, with the exception of a piece of cloth about their middle".

### 9c. Horses
Imported Arab and Persian ("horses of Ormuz", Nuniz); caparisons of Mecca velvet, satin, damask, Chinese and
Persian brocade; gilded forehead plates, some "heads of serpents and of other large animals"; silk-fringed
reins; horse-tail standards (Paes §4g). Mahanavami: eleven horses with trappings, white roses thrown on
them.

### 9d. Elephants
Nuniz (l. 9545 to 9550): "The war-elephants go with their howdahs (CASTELLOS) from which four men fight on
each side of them, and the elephants are completely clothed, and on their tusks they have knives fastened,
much ground and sharpened". Paes: velvet and gold caparisons, bells, painted giant faces, three or four
men in quilted tunics with shields and javelins. Varthema (c. 1505): "long swords fastened to their trunks
in battle".

### 9e. Arms and armour, 1520
Quilted tunics of leather with iron plates, some gilded; helmets with neck-guards and face-pieces; gorgets;
swords, small battle-axes, gold-shafted javelins; huge shields ("the shields are so large that there is no
need for armour to protect the body", Nuniz l. 9542); gold- and silver-plated bows. **Firearms**: Paes saw
"musqueteers with their musquets and blunderbusses" in the 1520 review; Figueiredo's twenty Portuguese
musketeers at Raichur; Bijapur artillery "four hundred heavy cannon" with 900 carriages; Raichur's walls
had "two hundred heavy pieces" and thirty catapults; Ferishta (via Sewell l. 4682): "cannon-shot,
musquetry, and rockets". Bijapur's guard: "five hundred Portuguese of the renegades". The king's side used
guns, but the decisive charge was cavalry and elephants. Matchlocks, not flintlocks.

### 9f. The city
Walls of "massive stonework", with an outer belt of "pointed stones of great height ... as high as a man's
breast" (Paes l. 7521); gates with flanking towers; rice fields and orchards inside the outer circuit; two
tanks feeding conduits; palm grove by the palace; streets "lined throughout with rows of houses and shops"
and a tree-shaded road to Nagalapura (Paes l. 7505 to 7509); houses "with many figures and decorations";
the House of Victory on elephant pillars with cloth-hung pavilions and eleven velvet-covered scaffolds for
the festival. **Anachronisms**: the bare stone Mahanavami Dibba (in 1520 it carried a hung pavilion);
modern whitewash on gopuras; the Vitthala chariot as it stands today; any "Vijayanagara flag".

Capitals:
- Vijayanagara (ವಿಜಯನಗರ; early "Vidyanagara"): Bukka I first ruled from Dorasamudra (Saka 1274), then
  Hosapattana, then Vijayanagara, the change "about the end of Saka 1277" (ASR 1907-08 p. 240). EC VI p. 24

  "the name was 'City of Victory,' not 'City of Learning'").

Dating note, 1336 vs 1346:
- Sewell l. 718 to 724: "generally supposed to have been founded in the year 1336, and that that date is
  not far from the truth may be gathered from two facts", an inscription of Harihara of 1340 and Ibn
  Battuta's account of the 1334 raid. Tier: scholarship (1900).
- EC VI Sg 1, dated Saka 1268 (A.D. 1346), the earliest dated grant of the house in that volume:
  "Harihara having conquered the earth from the eastern to the western ocean, in order to celebrate the
  festival of his victory, made an excellent grant. ... the maha-mandalesvara, subduer of hostile kings,
  champion over kings who break their word, vira-Hariyapodeyar, Kampannodeyar, Bukkannodeyar, Marapodeyar,
  Muddapodeyar, son-in-law Ballappa-dannayaka ... made, to Bharati-tirtha-sripada, his disciples and others,
  and the forty Brahmans residing in that tirtha of Sringeri ... a grant". Tier: inscription. Note: in 1346
  Harihara is still a *maha-mandalesvara*, and the grant names Bharati-tirtha, not Vidyaranya (the
  invocation is to "Vidyatirtha-guru").
- Rice's statement (EC VI Intro p. 24) that Vidyaranya "was instrumental in founding the Vijayanagar empire
  in 1336" cites "Sg. 11", which is dated 1652 (EC VI Intro p. 25: "Sg. 11 and 13, of 1652, contain a
  summary of the history of the matha"). Tier: legend (a 17th-century matha record).
- Imperial titles begin with Harihara II (ASR 1907-08; Sewell l. 1623 to 1625: "the first king who gave
  himself imperial titles under the style of MAHARAJADHIRAJA").
- Print on the dynasty page: "c. 1336 (traditional); the brothers' rule is attested from 1339-40, and the
  1346 grant celebrates rule from sea to sea; the first imperial title is Harihara II's (1377)".

## 11. Popular claims to avoid

1. **"Krishnadevaraya never lost a battle."** Nuniz l. 9829 to 9834: the Bijapur guns "compelled the
   King's troops to retire ... all the King's troops began to fly". Golkonda history via Sewell l. 4147 to 4149:
   near Bellamkonda the Sultan "suddenly appeared in rear of the Hindu army. In the battle which ensued he
   was victorious and the siege was raised". Contrary 1912 view: ASR 1908-09 p. 183, "ever successful".
   Say: "he won every great campaign he led" at most.
2. **"736,000 soldiers"** (or 1 million). Nuniz's columns (via Sewell l. 4520): "703,000 foot, 32,600
   horse, and 551 elephants"; Sewell: "It certainly demands a large strain on our credulity." Faria y
   Souza (a century later) gives 733,000 foot and 35,000 horse. Say "a vast army; the chronicler counts
   over seven lakh".
3. **Tenali Rama at his court.** CHENCHIAH 1928 p. 83: Ramakrishna "was attached to the court of
   Venkatapati Raya"; p. 26: he flourished in Rama Raya's time; the last three Ashtadiggajas, Tenali
   included, "did not write in the reign of Krishnadevaraya". Legend only.
4. **Vidyaranya founding the city in 1336.** The 1346 Sringeri grant names Bharati-tirtha; the 1336 plus
   Vidyaranya statement rests on Sg 11 of 1652 (EC VI pp. 24 to 25). Nuniz's hermit-and-hare story
   (l. 8770 to 8790) is recorded c. 1535. Legend tier for both.
5. **The Hakka-Bukka conversion story.** Ibn Battuta (via Sewell l. 752 to 758) says "eleven sons of the
   Rai [of Kampili] were made prisoners and carried to the Sultan, who made them all Mussalmans", without
   naming Hakka or Bukka. The Hakka-Bukka-at-Warangal story is in the *Vidyaranya Sikka* shown to Buchanan
   in 1801 (Sewell n. 29, l. 11740 to 11750); Sewell: "this story entirely leaves out of account the most
   important point". Legend; origin disputed.
6. **"Andhra Bhoja" as a contemporary title.** The coronation-festival record calls him "(like) a second
   Bhoja" (EI I p. 370), so the comparison is contemporary; the label "Andhra Bhoja" appears in CHENCHIAH
   1928 p. 26 ("is known as Andhra Bhoja") with no early source found. His own poem gives
   "Sahiti-samarangana-sarvabhauma" (AMUKTA I v. 44). Use "ಅಭಿನವ ಭೋಜ / a second Bhoja" with the
   inscription; mark "Andhra Bhoja" as a later popular title.
7. **Talikota as a religious war.** Sewell l. 5943 to 5945: before 1565 "Again Ali [Adil Shah] called in
   the aid of Vijayanagar, and again Rama Raya marched to his aid", i.e. Vijayanagara fought as Bijapur's
   ally; Paes l. 7600 to 7602: Muslims "who are paid by the king and belong to his guard"; Krishnadevaraya
   himself set a Bahmani prince on the throne (Nuniz ch. 17). Ferishta does frame grievances in religious
   terms (mosques desecrated, Sewell l. 5953). Modern readings (Eaton, Wagoner) not reopened this pass.
   Say: a war of Deccan powers; religion was part of the rhetoric, not the whole cause.
8. **Krishnadevaraya personally killed the Adil Shah.** Nuniz l. 9895 to 9905: the Adil Shah "had not even
   entered into the fight" and escaped on an elephant with Asad Khan; Sewell l. 5100 to 5101: Ismail Adil
   Shah died "on Thursday, August 13, 1534", five years after Krishnadevaraya. The image of the Adil Khan's
   skull as a scarecrow in AMUKTA I v. 42 ("దృష్టి కే దులఖానోగ్రకపాల") is court panegyric (TTD 1930 p. 183
   n. 1 cites it literally): **poetic hyperbole**, not an event.
9. Also avoid: "crowned on Krishnajayanti 1509" (§2b); "the Krishna temple was built in 1513" (installed
   16 Feb 1515, §3b); "Barbosa: cap two spans long" (it is Varthema, §4i); "the Orissa princess Tukka wrote
   the Tukka Panchakam" (verses traced to anthologies of c. 900 and pre-1400, §3f); "Purandaradasa sang at
   his court" (no record, §5h); "the Tirumala bronzes were installed in October 1518" (TTD: c. 1517, §9a).

## 13. Corrections to `vijayanagara.md`

- Krishna temple: Balakrishna installed Friday Phalguna su. 3, Saka 1436 = 16 Feb 1515 (ASR 1908-09;
  TTD 1930), not 1513.
- Accession 22 to 26 July 1509 (TTD 1930); coronation festival Magha su. 14 Sukla (23/24 Jan 1510).
- The "cap of gold brocade two spans long" is Varthema's, of King Narasimha.
- The Tirumala bronzes: names incised on the shoulders; installed c. 1517 per TTD 1930 (not "October
  1518"); the bronze shows a bare-chested king, not a tunic.
- Sangama dates: Deva Raya II sole rule from Saka 1346 (1424) to c. 1446 (ASR 1907-08); Sewell's 1419 is
  superseded; Harihara I's latest record Saka 1276 (1354-55), so "c. 1343" is wrong.
- Sewell's 1530 death year is superseded by records (1529).
- Prince Tirumalaraya: born 1518 (Kamalapuram record) and "ruling the earth" in 1524 (No. 139 of 1896; EC
  IX Ma 6, 82).

## 14. Goosebump candidates (ranked)

1. Raichur, Nuniz l. 9842 to 9847: "'Who ranges himself with me?' he cried ... the Ydallcao would boast
   that he had slain in it the greatest lord in the world, but that he should never boast that he had
   vanquished him. Then he took a ring from his finger and gave it to one of his pages, so that he might
   show it to his queens in token of his death".
2. Raichur, at the river, Nuniz l. 9874 to 9876: "the King out of compassion commanded the troops to
   retire, saying that numbers had died who did not deserve death nor were at all in fault".
3. AMUKTA I v. 14, the god of Srikakulam to the king in a dream: "నేను దెలుంగురాయఁడ న్గన్నడ రాయ!" ("I am the
   Telugu king, O Kannada king!").
4. The crowned child: Nuniz l. 10435 "he himself did obeisance to him", borne out by records of Saka 1446
   calling "Tirumalaraya-deva-Maharayar" "ruling the earth" (ASR 1908-09 p. 186 n.).
5. Paes l. 7802 to 7803 and 7932 to 7934: the House of Victory "was made when the king came back from the
   war against Orya", and Timmarasu "brought up the king and made him king, and so the king looks on him
   like a father."
Also strong: the Balakrishna image brought "with great care" from Udayagiri (ASR 1908-09 p. 176); the
bronze with the three names "incised on their shoulders" (TTD 1930 p. 185); Paes: "he touched us all and
could not have enough of looking at us"; Nuniz: Figueiredo, "what great things could be effected by one
good man".
