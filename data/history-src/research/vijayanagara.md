# Vijayanagara Empire (ವಿಜಯನಗರ ಸಾಮ್ರಾಜ್ಯ), c. 1336–1646 — research fact sheet

Prepared 2026-10-10 against data/history-src/research/BRIEF.md. English with Kannada names beside.
Every claim carries a source and a tier: `inscription` (inscription, coin, contemporary chronicle,
eyewitness), `scholarship` (historian's conclusion from primary evidence), `legend` (tradition with
no contemporary evidence). "Sewell" = Robert Sewell, *A Forgotten Empire (Vijayanagar)*, London 1900,
which also contains the full English translations of the Portuguese chronicles of Domingo Paes
(c. 1520–22) and Fernão Nuniz (c. 1535–37). Line numbers "Sewell txt l.NNN" refer to the Project
Gutenberg plain-text file (ebook 3310) so the writer can find the exact passage; chapter numbers
are Sewell's own.

Research-method note: web search budget ran out part-way through this pass; the remaining work was
done by downloading and grepping the primary editions (Sewell 1900, Major 1857, the 1957
Madhuravijayam translation) and by reading Wikipedia pages purely as finding aids for citations.
Items that could not be verified are marked **UNVERIFIED** and must not be used in a story.

### Key dated events (only those with a traceable source)

- **1333–1342**: Ibn Battuta (ಇಬ್ನ್ ಬತೂತ) in India; he records Sultan Muhammad Tughluq's attack on
  the "Rai of Kambila" (Kampili, ಕಂಪಿಲಿ), the Rai's death, and that "eleven sons of the Rai were
  made prisoners and carried to the Sultan, who made them all Mussalmans". Sewell ch. 2 (txt l. 720–760),
  quoting Ibn Battuta. Sewell dates the raid to 1334. — `inscription` (contemporary chronicle, eyewitness
  of the court, not of the siege).
- **c. 1336**: traditional founding year of the city. Sewell ch. 2: "The city of Vijayanagar is ...
  generally supposed to have been founded in the year 1336". He supports it with (a) an inscription of
  Harihara I dated A.D. 1340 and (b) the match between Ibn Battuta and Nuniz on the 1334 raid. —
  `scholarship`. **Disputed** — see §5: Goodding (2013) argues 1336 rests on 16th-century Sringeri
  copper plates and proposes c. 1346 for the real start of sovereignty.
- **1340**: earliest inscription Sewell knew of Harihara I (ಹರಿಹರ I, "Hakka"/ಹಕ್ಕ, "Hariyappa Vodeya"),
  styling him *mahamandalesvara*, not king; the same year "a fort was built ... at Badami by permission of
  Harihara". Sewell ch. 3 (txt l. 955–972). — `inscription` (cited via Sewell; EC/SII number not given
  by Sewell).
- **1346**: Harihara's grant to Sringeri matha (ಶೃಂಗೇರಿ), in which he is "ruler of the whole country
  between the eastern and the western seas" with capital "Vidya Nagara"; the Hoysala widow
  Krishnayitayi (ಕೃಷ್ಣಾಯಿತಾಯಿ) of Vira Ballala III is said to have attended. Kamath, *A Concise History
  of Karnataka* (2001) pp. 129, 160–161, as cited in Wikipedia "Origin of the Vijayanagara Empire";
  Goodding 2013 p. 88 treats 1346 as the more secure date. Exact EC volume/number: **to verify** (EC VI,
  Sringeri taluk, is the expected place). — `inscription` via `scholarship`.
- **1343 (Sewell) / 1355 (Wikipedia infobox)**: death of Harihara I. Sewell ch. 3 reasons from Nuniz's
  "seven years" to c. 1343; Wikipedia "Harihara I" gives 20 Nov 1355 citing Lakshmi 1958. **Disputed.**
- **1356–1377**: reign of Bukka I (ಬುಕ್ಕ I / ಬುಕ್ಕರಾಯ). Kumari Jhansi Lakshmi, "The Chronology of the
  Sangama Dynasty", *Proceedings of the Indian History Congress* 21 (1958) pp. 290–297, as cited in
  Wikipedia "Bukka Raya I". Sewell (ch. 3) has Nuniz's "thirty-seven years" and says Bukka "did in fact
  reign thirty-six years". — `scholarship`.
- **1347**: Bahmani Sultanate (ಬಹಮನಿ) founded at Gulbarga; Vijayanagara's northern rival for two
  centuries. Sewell ch. 3 heading. — `scholarship`.
- **c. 1362**: Kumara Kampana (ಕುಮಾರ ಕಂಪಣ) takes the Sambuvaraya country (Rajagambhira-rajya) in
  Tondaimandalam. Kalidos 1976 pp. 214–217 via Wikipedia "Kampana's invasion of Madurai". —
  `scholarship`.
- **c. 1370–1371**: Kampana defeats and kills the Madurai Sultan (Qurbat Hasan Kangu) and ends the
  Madurai Sultanate; "by 1371 his conquests reached the southern ocean" (Sharma 1978 p. 31). The
  Thiruvenkatachari introduction to Madhuravijayam says "the Muslim rule ended for ever in Madhurai in
  1371" and quotes Shams-i-Siraj Afif: "A neighbouring chief named Bukkan at the head of a body of men
  and elephants marched into Ma'bar" (Elliot & Dowson III p. 339). — `inscription` (Afif is a
  contemporary Delhi chronicler) + `scholarship`.
- **1374**: Kampana dies (Sharma 1978 p. 31 via Wikipedia). — `scholarship`.
- **1377–1404**: Harihara II (ಇಮ್ಮಡಿ ಹರಿಹರ), first to take the imperial title *maharajadhiraja*; his
  general Iruga's 1385 inscription on a pillar in a Jain temple near Kamalapura "proves that the king
  was tolerant in religious matters". Sewell ch. 4 (txt l. 1612–1640). — `inscription` via Sewell.
- **1406–1422/23**: Deva Raya I (ಒಂದನೇ ದೇವರಾಯ). Wikipedia infobox gives 5 Nov 1406 – 25 Feb 1423. He
  built a barrage across the Tungabhadra and an aqueduct to the capital c. 1410 (Wikipedia "Deva Raya I",
  citing Kamath 1980 and Chopra et al. 2003). — `scholarship`.
- **c. 1420**: Nicolo de' Conti (ನಿಕೊಲೊ ಕೊಂತಿ), Venetian, sees the city: circumference "sixty miles",
  "90,000 men fit to bear arms", king "more powerful than all the other kings of India". Major 1857
  (Conti section); Sewell ch. 7. — `inscription` (eyewitness).
- **1423/24–1446**: Deva Raya II (ಇಮ್ಮಡಿ ದೇವರಾಯ, "Praudha Deva Raya"); 10 Feb 1423 – 24 May 1446 per
  Lakshmi 1958 via Wikipedia. Title *Gajabetekara* (ಗಜಬೇಟೆಕಾರ, "elephant hunter") per Sastri 1955. —
  `scholarship`.
- **1443**: Abdur Razzaq (ಅಬ್ದುರ್ ರಜ್ಜಾಕ್), envoy of Shah Rukh of Herat, at Vijayanagara from end
  April to 5 Dec 1443 (Sewell ch. 7, txt l. 2795–2810). — `inscription` (eyewitness).
- **1443**: assassination attempt on Deva Raya II by his brother at a banquet (Razzaq); then war with
  Bahmani Ala-ud-din. Sewell ch. 6 (txt l. 2360–2460). — `inscription` (Razzaq heard it in Calicut and
  reached the city months later; near-contemporary).
- **1470s**: Athanasius Nikitin (ಅಫನಾಸಿ ನಿಕಿತಿನ್), Russian of Tver, in the Deccan; calls the capital
  "Bichenegher". Sewell ch. 9 (txt l. 3246–3300). — `inscription` (eyewitness of Bahmani lands; did not
  visit the capital).
- **1485**: Saluva Narasimha (ಸಾಳುವ ನರಸಿಂಹ) seizes the throne. Sewell ch. 9. — `scholarship`.
- **1509**: Krishnadevaraya (ಕೃಷ್ಣದೇವರಾಯ) succeeds his half-brother Vira Narasimha. Wikipedia gives
  accession 26 July 1509 (Srinivasan 1979 p. 200) and coronation 23/24 Jan 1510 from a Hampi inscription
  dated 14 Magha Śaka 1431. Sewell ch. 12 mentions his coronation grants to the Virupaksha temple. —
  `inscription` + `scholarship`.
- **1512–1515**: Orissa (Gajapati) campaign — Udayagiri, Kondavidu; Prataparudra gives a daughter in
  marriage (Paes confirms: "the king of Orya gave him a daughter whom the king of Bisnaga married",
  Sewell txt l. 7372–7377). — `inscription` (Paes) + `scholarship`.
- **1513**: Krishna temple (ಕೃಷ್ಣ ದೇವಾಲಯ) at Hampi built "after his return from the successful
  campaign in the east" (Sewell ch. 12, txt l. 4955–4960); Fritz & Michell 2016 pp. 70–72 (via
  Wikipedia "Hampi") give 1515. ARE 354 of 1934–35 (Virupaksha temple, Śaka 1435 = 1513) records
  Krishnadevaraya's village grants to Virupaksha (whatisindia.com transcription of the Madras Epigraphy
  report). — `inscription`.
- **1518 (October)**: bronze portrait statues of Krishnadevaraya and queens Tirumala Devi
  (ತಿರುಮಲದೇವಿ) and Chinna Devi (ಚಿನ್ನಾದೇವಿ) installed at Tirumala on his fourth visit, per *Tirupati
  Devasthanam Epigraphical Report* 1930 (ed. Sadhu Subrahmanya Sastry), as quoted on the Commons file
  page (see §6). Wikipedia says he visited seven times and that 229 of ~1,250 published Tirumala
  epigraphs are his. — `inscription` (the statues and the TTD inscriptions).
- **1520, Saturday 19 May** (Sewell's deduction): battle of Raichur (ರಾಯಚೂರು) against Ismail Adil
  Shah; Nuniz wrote 1522, Ferishta AH 927 (= May 1521). Sewell ch. 11 argues 1520 from Paes's festival
  dates and the Portuguese seizure of the Goa mainlands. — `inscription` (Nuniz) + `scholarship`
  (Sewell's date). **Year disputed between 1520 and 1521.**
- **1521**: Korragal dam and the Basavanna channel built (Sewell ch. 12, txt l. 4962–4966). —
  `scholarship` (Sewell cites local inscriptions).
- **1524**: son Tirumala Raya (ತಿರುಮಲರಾಯ), aged about six, crowned in the king's lifetime; dies within
  months, "by poison" (Nuniz ch. 18, Sewell txt l. 10419–10445). — `inscription` (Nuniz, near-
  contemporary, not eyewitness).
- **1528**: Ugra/Lakshmi Narasimha monolith (ಉಗ್ರ ನರಸಿಂಹ) hewn from a single boulder near the Krishna
  temple; an inscription dated Friday 23 April 1529 stands in front of it — "the last inscription of the
  reign at present known" to Sewell (ch. 12, txt l. 5016–5040). — `inscription`.
- **1529, 17 October**: death of Krishnadevaraya. Wikipedia infobox; a Honnenahalli (ಹೊನ್ನೇನಹಳ್ಳಿ,
  Tumakuru dt.) inscription dated Śaka 1452, Virodhi, Kartika śu. 15, uploaded to Commons in 2025 with
  an unpublished Academia.edu paper, is claimed to fix the day. **Treat the exact day as UNVERIFIED**
  (unpublished); the year 1529 is secure from Achyuta's accession. — `inscription` (pending).
- **1529, 30 November – 1542**: Achyuta Deva Raya (ಅಚ್ಯುತ ದೇವರಾಯ); earliest inscription Sewell knew =
  Monday 15 Aug 1530 (ch. 13). Raichur and Mudgal lost to Bijapur at once (Ferishta via Sewell ch. 13).
  — `inscription` + `scholarship`.
- **1542–1565**: Sadasiva Raya (ಸದಾಶಿವರಾಯ) nominal king, Rama Raya (ಅಳಿಯ ರಾಮರಾಯ) and brothers Tirumala
  and Venkatadri rule; inscriptions 1542–1568 name Sadasiva as sovereign, and even a 1566–67 record
  (Hultzsch) has Tirumala act "after obtaining sanction at the feet of Sadasiva-deva Maharaya" (Sewell
  ch. 14, txt l. 5451–5480). — `inscription`.
- **1565, Tuesday 23 January**: battle of Rakkasa-Tangadi / Talikota (ರಕ್ಕಸತಂಗಡಿ / ತಾಳಿಕೋಟೆ). Sewell
  ch. 15 (txt l. 6120–6125); his note 327: Ferishta gives "Friday the 20th of Jumad-oos-Sany, A.H. 972"
  (Briggs iii. 414), "but the day of the month given corresponds to Tuesday, not Friday". — `inscription`
  (Ferishta, writing c. 1600 from Bijapur records) + `scholarship`. Some modern pages give 26 Jan;
  Wikipedia notes "debate as to the precise dates" (Sarkar 1960 p. 91).
- **1567**: Cesare Federici (ಸೀಸರ್ ಫ್ರೆಡರಿಕ್), Venetian merchant, in the ruined city for seven months.
  Purchas, *Pilgrimes* 1625 ii. p. 1703 (Sewell note 333). — `inscription` (eyewitness).
- **1565–1572**: Tirumala Deva Raya (ತಿರುಮಲ ದೇವರಾಯ) at Penukonda (ಪೆನುಕೊಂಡ); then Sriranga I
  (1572–86), Venkata II (1586–1614; capital shifted to Chandragiri/ಚಂದ್ರಗಿರಿ c. 1592 and Vellore/ವೆಲ್ಲೂರು
  c. 1604), Sriranga III (1642–1646), last ruler. Wikipedia "List of Vijayanagara emperors",
  "Venkatapati Raya", "Sriranga III" — citations in those pages are thin; **dates after 1572 need
  checking against Heras, *The Aravidu Dynasty of Vijayanagara* (1927)**. — `scholarship` (weak).

## 2. Primary sources: what survives, where to read them, licence

### A. Inscriptions and copper plates
- Roughly 7,000 Vijayanagara inscriptions are known, about half in Kannada (Kamath 2001 p. 157 via
  Wikipedia "Origin of the Vijayanagara Empire"); the rest Telugu, Tamil, Sanskrit.
- **Epigraphia Carnatica** (B. L. Rice, 1886–1905; revised eds 1970s), 12 original volumes. Public domain
  (Rice editions). On archive.org: e.g. `ec-05-1-1902`, `epigraphiacarnat05mysouoft`,
  `epigraphia_carnatica_vol2_mysore` (vol. II = Shravanabelagola), `epigraphia_carnatica_vol12_mysore`,
  `ec-08-1904-b`, `india.history.resource.93146` (vol. IX Bangalore). Full list:
  https://archive.org/advancedsearch.php?q=title%3A%28epigraphia+carnatica%29 — 149 items.
- **Epigraphia Indica** vol. III p. 36 has Sewell's pedigree of the first dynasty (Sewell ch. 2 end).
  Public domain for pre-1930 volumes.
- **South Indian Inscriptions** vols IX, XVI (Vijayanagara volumes) — transcriptions at
  https://www.whatisindia.com/inscriptions/ (the "Indian Analyst" site; useful but check against the
  printed volume). Example: SII IX Vijayanagara section; Annual Report on Epigraphy 1934–35 (Hampi
  Krishnadevaraya 1513 record, ARE 354 of 1934–35).
- **Madras Epigraphy Annual Report to 31 March 1935**: whatisindia.com transcription lists Bukka I's
  Katletala (South Kanara) record of Śaka 1282, Deva Raya II records (Nos 63, 143, 264, 300, 312), and
  a 1395 Jain *chaityalaya* consecration at Kandanavrolu (Kurnool) by "Immadi-Bukkamantrisvara".
- **Tirupati Devasthanam Epigraphical Report** (1930) and *Tirumala Tirupati Devasthanam Inscriptions*
  (6 vols, 1930s) — the 229 Krishnadevaraya records; the 1930 report is public domain in India (Commons
  tags it PD-India).
- **Sringeri copper plates** claiming 1336 and Vidyaranya's role — **argued to be 16th-century
  productions** (Goodding 2013 pp. 88–89; Kulke 1985 "Maharajas, Mahants and Historians: Reflections on
  the Historiography of Early Vijayanagara and Sringeri", in A. L. Dallapiccola ed., *Vijayanagara —
  City and Empire*, 1985, pp. 120–143). Use only as evidence of the legend.

### B. Literary sources (contemporary)
- **Madhuravijayam / Virakamparaya-charitam** (ಮಧುರಾವಿಜಯಂ) by Gangadevi (ಗಂಗಾದೇವಿ), Sanskrit, 9 cantos
  (incomplete). Palm-leaf ms in Grantha script found in a private library at Trivandrum, "first brought
  to light in 1916" by Pandits G. Harihara Sastri and V. Srinivasa Sastri; ed. Trivandrum 1916, 2nd ed.
  1924 (Sridhara Press). 521 extant verses, c. 70 lost, 58 mutilated (Thiruvenkatachari 1957 intro).
  The Sanskrit text is public domain. English translation: S. Thiruvenkatachari, Annamalai University
  1957 — **copyright status uncertain (Indian term: 60 years from publication for a work published by an
  institution would have expired 2018, but the translator's personal term may run longer); quote only
  short passages and check**. Online: https://ambuda.org/proofing/madhuravijayam-with-english-translation
  (plain text: .../download/text). Other mss: NCC lists three more (two Trivandrum, one Lahore) with less
  text (INDOLOGY list, Dec 2012).
- **Amuktamalyada** (ಆಮುಕ್ತಮಾಲ್ಯದ) by Krishnadevaraya, Telugu prabandha on Andal; contains a
  statecraft section. Telugu text public domain; Commons has scans (Category:Works by Krishnadevaraya;
  file "Amuktamalyada by Krishnadevaraya.jpg"). The dream of "Andhra Vishnu" at Srikakulam commanding a
  Telugu poem is in the poem's own prologue (Wikipedia "Amuktamalyada"; IFP open-edition chapter
  https://books.openedition.org/ifp/7916 — not read in this pass; **verify the exact verse before
  quoting**).
- **Rayavachakamu** (ರಾಯವಾಚಕಮು), Telugu prose "report" on Krishnadevaraya's court, written c. 1600 at
  Madurai, not contemporary (Wagoner, *Tidings of the King*, 1993). Commons scan "రాయవాచకము.pdf". Use
  as later tradition only. — `legend`/late chronicle.
- **Krishnadevarayana Dinachari** (ಕೃಷ್ಣದೇವರಾಯನ ದಿನಚರಿ), Kannada daily-record; surviving copy is a
  17th-century transcript of a document claimed to be from the reign; authorship "not yet clear"
  (Karnataka Itihasa Academy page https://karnatakaitihasaacademy.org/?p=1646). **Authenticity
  uncertain — do not treat as the king's diary.**
- Deva Raya II's court: Kannada *Gadugina Bharata* (ಗದುಗಿನ ಭಾರತ, Kumara Vyasa/ಕುಮಾರವ್ಯಾಸ, c. 1430),
  *Prabhulinga-lile* (ಪ್ರಭುಲಿಂಗಲೀಲೆ, Chamarasa/ಚಾಮರಸ, c. 1430), *Shivatattva-chintamani*
  (Lakkanna Dandesa/ಲಕ್ಕಣ್ಣ ದಂಡೇಶ); Telugu Srinatha. Sastri 1955 pp. 244, 363–364; Shiva Prakash 1997
  pp. 208–209 (via Wikipedia "Kannada literature in the Vijayanagara Empire"). All texts PD.

### C. Travellers (eyewitnesses)
- **Ibn Battuta** (1333–42): Kampili siege, "Haraib" (Harihara). English: Gibb's Hakluyt translation
  (vol. III, 1971, copyright) or Lee 1829 (PD). Sewell ch. 2 quotes him.
- **Nicolo de' Conti** (c. 1420): in R. H. Major, *India in the Fifteenth Century*, Hakluyt Society
  1857 — PD. archive.org identifiers: `indiainfifteenth00majo`, `indiainfifteenth00majorich`,
  `indiainfifteent00nikigoog`. Each narrative is separately paginated.
- **Abdur Razzaq** (1443): same volume, "Narrative of the Voyage of Abd-er-Razzak", tr. Quatremère/Major.
  City description p. 23 ("the pupil of the eye has never seen a place like it ... seven citadels and the
  same number of walls"); king's person pp. 30–31; "Mahanadi" festival pp. 35–37; the banquet-massacre
  story c. pp. 32–34 (the betel-dish dagger at p. 33). Also in Elliot & Dowson IV. Sewell ch. 6–7
  reprints most of it. PD.
- **Athanasius Nikitin** (1470s): same Major volume (Russian text tr. Wielhorsky). PD.
- **Duarte Barbosa** (c. 1516): *The Book of Duarte Barbosa*, tr. Dames, Hakluyt 1918–21 — PD in most
  jurisdictions (author d. 1933? — check); Stanley's 1866 translation certainly PD. Sewell ch. 10 quotes:
  "The common people go quite naked ... The king wears a cap of gold brocade two spans long ... His horse
  is worth more than some of our cities" (txt l. 3718–3730).
- **Domingo Paes** (ದೊಮಿಂಗೊ ಪಾಯಿಸ್, c. 1520–22) and **Fernão Nuniz** (ಫೆರ್ನಾವೊ ನೂನಿಜ್, c. 1535–37):
  Portuguese ms (Bibliothèque Nationale, Paris) translated in full in Sewell 1900, pp. 236–290 (Paes)
  and 291–395 (Nuniz). PD. Gutenberg: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3310 ; archive.org:
  `aforgottenempir00paesgoog` (1900), `Vijayanagar_201606`, `a-forgotten-empire`.
- **Cesare Federici** (1567): "Voyage and Travell of M. Caesar Fredericke" in Hakluyt (1599) and
  Purchas (1625, vol. ii p. 1703). EEBO-TCP 1588 edition at https://name.umdl.umich.edu/A00611.0001.001
  (returned 403 in this pass; retry). PD.
- **Ferishta** (Muhammad Qasim Firishta, c. 1600, Bijapur court): *History of the Rise of the Mahomedan
  Power in India*, tr. John Briggs, 4 vols, 1829. PD. archive.org: `in.ernet.dli.2015.102983` (vol. III,
  1829), `historyofriseofm03feri_0` (vol. III, 1908 reprint), `historyofriseofm01feri`,
  `historyofriseofm02feri`, `historyofriseofm04feri`. Talikota at Briggs iii. 414 (per Sewell n. 327);
  Deva Raya II's Muslim troops and the Koran-before-the-throne passage is quoted by Sewell ch. 6 from
  Scott's 1794 translation (txt l. 2336–2360). — `inscription` tier for Bijapur-side events of the 1560s
  (Ferishta used official records); `scholarship`/late for 14th–15th-century events.
- **Diogo do Couto**, *Décadas da Ásia*, Dec. VIII c. 15 (Rama Raya's death; Hussain's words) — PD
  Portuguese; Sewell ch. 15 translates the key line.

## 3. Candidate stories

### Story 1 — "The Queen Who Wrote the War": Gangadevi and Kumara Kampana (c. 1362–1374)
**People**: Kumara Kampana (ಕುಮಾರ ಕಂಪಣ), son of Bukka I; Gangadevi (ಗಂಗಾದೇವಿ), his wife and the poet;
Bukka I (ಬುಕ್ಕರಾಯ); the Sambuvaraya king of Tondaimandalam; the Madurai sultan Qurbat Hasan Kangu
(ಕುರ್ಬತ್ ಹಸನ್); the generals Saluva Mangu (ಸಾಳುವ ಮಂಗು) and Gopana (ಗೋಪಣ).

**Arc (beats)**
1. Vijayanagara, c. 1360: Bukka, in open court, analyses the Tamil country and orders Kampana south to
   crush the Sambuvarayas and rule from Kanchi (Madhuravijayam canto 3 — `inscription`, a court poem by
   a participant's wife).
2. The march: Vijayanagara's army with Chola, Chera and Pandya allies camps at Mulbagal (ಮುಳಬಾಗಿಲು), then
   Virinchipuram; siege of Padaividu; the Sambuvaraya king dies "at the hands of Kampana" (canto 4;
   Wikipedia/Sharma: after a duel at the fortress, c. 1362).
3. Kampana rules justly from Kanchipuram (canto 5); two cantos of palace life (6–7) — the historical
   silence the writer can fill with the marriage and Gangadevi's own voice.
4. A woman (a goddess; the translator reads her as Dharma personified) appears and describes the Tamil
   country under the Sultanate: "The cocoanut trees have all been cut and in their places are to be seen
   rows of iron spikes with human heads sticking at the points"; "The river Kāvēri, uncurbed by proper
   bunds or dams, has become deflected very much from her time-honoured course"; "Those temples which
   were once resonant with the sounds of mridanga drums are now echoing the fearful howls of jackals";
   "the voice of the parrots taught to speak Persian in the houses of the foreigners" (canto 8, 1957 tr.).
5. She gives him a sword sent by the sage Agastya — "which looked as terrible as Yama himself" (canto 8
   and concluding canto v. 33).
6. The battle before Madurai: elephants, arrows, "several hundreds of Tāmraparṇis" of blood (concl. canto
   vv. 1–20).
7. Single combat: the Sultan, "his eyes red with the drink of vīra-pāna", strings his bow; Kampana cuts
   the bow-string, the Sultan draws his sword; Kampana, "the glory of the Karnāṭa race", takes off his
   head in an instant (vv. 21–36).
8. Aftermath: Kampana "guaranteed the safety of the remaining men in his enemy ranks"; the Ranganatha
   image returns to Srirangam; by 1371 Vijayanagara reaches the southern sea (v. 40; Devakunjari 1979
   p. 164; Sharma 1978 p. 31). Kampana dies 1374; the poem breaks off — the manuscript itself is damaged,
   ten leaves missing.

**Goosebump moment**: concluding canto v. 37 — "even after the head had fallen, the trunk on the horse-back
still held the reins, checking the horse's course with one hand, while the other was uplifted to return
the blow." Evidence: Gangadevi's own verse (1957 tr.), written within the generation; the Delhi
chronicler Afif independently confirms that "Bukkan" marched into Ma'bar with elephants (Elliot &
Dowson III p. 339).

**Dramatised vs recorded**: Recorded — the campaign sequence, allies, places, the sultan's death, the
devastation description, the sword gift (as a poetic episode). Dramatised — any claim that Gangadevi
rode with the army (Thiruvenkatachari: "only a guess"); the duel itself (he doubts a duel happened; the
rival poem Ramabhyudayam gives it to Saluva Mangu). The goddess is convention (cf. Harsha's Nagananda).
The sultan's name comes from Afif, not from Gangadevi, who never names him.

**Why it grips adults**: a 14th-century woman's war poem, found by accident in a heap of worn palm leaves
in 1916; a queen narrating her husband's killing of a king; and the dramatic irony that the only copy is
mutilated — the story has to end where the leaves end.

### Story 2 — "The Banquet of Drums": Deva Raya II and the brother's knife (1443)
**People**: Deva Raya II (ಇಮ್ಮಡಿ ದೇವರಾಯ); his unnamed brother; the minister "Danaik" (dandanayaka,
probably Lakkanna Dandesa/ಲಕ್ಕಣ್ಣ ದಂಡೇಶ — Sewell n. 114 suggests the identification; treat as probable);
Abdur Razzaq, the Persian envoy; Sultan Ala-ud-din Bahmani.

**Arc**
1. Razzaq, sea-sick and homesick, is summoned from Calicut by a letter from "the king of Bidjanagar, who
   holds a powerful empire" (Major p. 22; Sewell txt l. 2803–2815).
2. The road: Mangalore, a bronze temple, "a mountain whose summit reached the skies", Belur ("the houses
   of which were like palaces") (Major pp. 22–23).
3. The city: "the pupil of the eye has never seen a place like it ... seven citadels and the same number
   of walls enclose each other" (Major p. 23); roses sold everywhere; jewellers selling pearls and
   diamonds openly; "cells, like basins, filled with bullion" (Sewell txt l. 2870–2925).
4. Flashback (as Razzaq heard it in Calicut): the king's brother builds a new house, invites every
   noble; with "all the drums, kettledrums, trumpets, and flutes that could be found in the city" playing,
   each guest is called in alone and cut to pieces; the brother then comes to the king "holding in his hand
   a dish covered with betel-nut, under which was concealed a brilliant poignard" (Major p. 33; Sewell
   txt l. 2368–2420).
5. The king says "I am not in good health to-day"; is stabbed, falls behind the throne; the brother
   proclaims from the portico "I have slain the king ... now I am king"; the king, seizing the seat, fells
   the man sent to behead him, and cries out from the harem side, "I am alive. I am well and safe. Seize
   that wretch."
6. Retribution: "Men in great numbers were slain, flayed, burnt alive". Danaik, away at the Ceylon
   frontier, is recalled.
7. The Bahmani sultan, smelling weakness, demands "seven lakhs of varahas"; war on the frontier
   (Sewell txt l. 2449–2520).
8. Razzaq's audience: the king "dressed in a robe of green satin ... olive complexion, his frame was
   thin ... rather tall ... no beard on his chin ... extremely pleasing" (Major pp. 30–31); the three-day
   "Mahanadi" festival with revolving pavilions and a thousand armoured elephants "like the waves of the
   sea" (Major pp. 35–37); the gold-plated audience hall "each of these plates was as thick as the blade of
   a sword" (Sewell txt l. 2990–3000). Razzaq leaves 5 Dec 1443.

**Goosebump moment**: the wounded king rising behind his own throne while his brother is already
announcing his death to the crowd — "I am alive." Evidence: Razzaq's narrative, written by an envoy who
reached the city weeks later and was presented to the same king (Major 1857; Sewell says Razzaq's version
"is without doubt the more reliable" against Nuniz's "nephew").

**Dramatised vs recorded**: Recorded — all of the above in Razzaq's words. Dramatised — the brother's
name and motive (unrecorded); whether the festival Razzaq saw was Mahanavami or New Year (Sewell doubts
the month — txt l. 2963–2985); Lakkanna = Danaik.

**Why it grips adults**: a locked-room massacre staged under cover of music, in the most splendid city
a Persian courtier had ever seen; told by an outsider who had no reason to flatter.

### Story 3 — "The Eyes of a She-Goat": Krishnadevaraya, Timmarasu and Raichur (1509–1529) — STRONGEST
**People**: Krishnadevaraya (ಕೃಷ್ಣದೇವರಾಯ); the dying king Vira Narasimha ("Busbalrao" in Nuniz); the
minister Saluva Timmarasu (ಸಾಳುವ ತಿಮ್ಮರಸು, "Salvatinica"/"Temersea"); queens Tirumala Devi and Chinna
Devi; Ismail Adil Shah of Bijapur; his general Salabat Khan; Asad Khan; the Portuguese horse-dealer
Cristóvão de Figueiredo; the chroniclers Paes and Nuniz; the boy Tirumala Raya.

**Arc**
1. Deathbed, 1509: the dying king orders Timmarasu to "put out the eyes of" his half-brother Krishna
   "and must bring them to show him". Krishna says he "did not seek to be king ... his desire was to pass
   through this world as a JOGI". Timmarasu "commanded to bring a she-goat, and he put out its eyes, and
   took them to show the King" (Nuniz ch. 4, Sewell txt l. 9195–9214). — `inscription` (Nuniz, c. 1535,
   writing within living memory; not an eyewitness).
2. The new king shuts himself in the city "a year and a half ... looking at the testaments of past
   kings", finds Narasimha's will ordering the capture of Raichur, Mudgal and Udayagiri (Nuniz ch. 5).
3. The morning regime (eyewitness Paes): drinks "three-quarter pint of oil of gingelly before daylight",
   lifts earthenware weights, sword exercise "till he has sweated out all the oil", wrestles, then
   "gallops about the plain ... till dawn" (Sewell txt l. 7428–7436). Paes's portrait: "of medium height,
   and of fair complexion and good figure, rather fat than thin, he has on his face signs of small-pox. He
   is the most feared and perfect king that could possibly be, cheerful of disposition and very merry ...
   a great ruler and a man of much justice, but subject to sudden fits of rage" (txt l. 7357–7363). The
   courtesan-wife for whom "he built this new city" (Nagalapura/Hospet) (txt l. 7390–7395).
4. Orissa 1512–15; the Gajapati's daughter given in marriage (Paes); Krishna temple 1513 and the
   Tirupati bronzes 1518.
5. Raichur 1520: the host "736,000 [fighting men], with 550 elephants" in eleven divisions (Nuniz via
   Sewell ch. 11); the Friday postponed as unlucky; Saturday new moon; the first line routed by Bijapur's
   guns; the king, "beginning to despair of victory", mounts and charges with the second line; the
   sultan's army driven into the Krishna; Salabat Khan with "five hundred Portuguese of the renegades"
   cuts almost to the king's bodyguard, "seemed like nothing but a furious wolf amongst sheep", is taken
   (Nuniz ch. 9–10, txt l. 9925–9960). Spoil: "four thousand horses of Ormuz, and a hundred elephants, and
   four hundred heavy cannon"; the king's dead "sixteen thousand and odd"; he burns them and gives alms.
6. The siege: Figueiredo with twenty musketeers picks off defenders "careless and free from fear, as men
   who up to then had never seen men killed with firearms"; the fort captain "reached over with his body
   in front one of the embrasures and was killed with a musquet-shot that struck him in the middle of his
   forehead"; the city falls (txt l. 9965–10035). The king demands that the Adil Shah come and "kiss his
   foot" as the price of peace (Faria y Souza and Nuniz via Sewell txt l. 4455–4462).
7. Mahanavami at the House of Victory (Paes eyewitness, autumn 1520): the king throws white roses to the
   eleven horses and four elephants, watches "the slaughter of twenty-four buffaloes and a hundred and
   fifty sheep" done "at one blow", and burns rubies and pearls on the fire; Figueiredo seated on the
   platform "close to the king" (txt l. 7820–7900).
8. 1524–29: the six-year-old son crowned, the father doing obeisance to him; eight months of festival;
   the boy sickens and dies "by poison"; in open durbar the king turns on Timmarasu: "I held thee always
   as my great friend, and now for these forty years thou hast been governor in this kingdom ... Thou wert
   bound ... to put out mine eyes; yet thou didst not ... the eyes of a goat were put out, wherefore ...
   thou wert a traitor" — arrests him with the help of Portuguese present, and later has Timma, his
   brother and son blinded (Nuniz ch. 18; Sewell ch. 10, txt l. 4850–4860). The king, "sorely ill",
   still marches when the Adil Shah stirs; dies 1529, naming Achyuta (Nuniz).

**Goosebump moment**: the durbar speech — the king citing the she-goat's eyes, the very mercy that made
him king, as the proof of Timmarasu's treason. Evidence: Nuniz, who was in Vijayanagara trading horses
within a decade, and Sewell's note that "some Portuguese ... happened to be present at the Durbar". The
blinding itself is also in Telugu tradition (Timmarasu Charitramu — later, Commons scan) — `legend` for
details, `inscription` for the fact of imprisonment/blinding (Nuniz).

**Dramatised vs recorded**: Recorded — everything above with a Paes/Nuniz line reference; the coins,
temples, 1518 bronzes, Honnenahalli/other inscriptions. Dramatised — Krishna's inner life; whether
Timmarasu's son really poisoned the prince (Nuniz reports the king's belief); the king's face-to-face
exchange with Figueiredo beyond what Paes records (Paes: the king "took much pleasure in his company").
**Avoid**: Tenali Rama (legend only, see §5), "Ashtadiggaja" court scenes as fact (tradition; three of the
eight probably wrote after 1529), any Mughal-style durbar imagery.

**Why it grips adults**: a saint-by-temperament who becomes the most feared king in India; a mentor
whose one lie made the reign possible and whose end is the reign's tragedy; two eyewitness Europeans
who leave us the sweat, the roses, the buffalo blood and the musket-shot to the forehead.

### Story 4 — "The Old Man in the Litter": Rama Raya and the fall of the city (1542–1567)
**People**: Aliya Rama Raya (ಅಳಿಯ ರಾಮರಾಯ), son-in-law of Krishnadevaraya; his brothers Tirumala
(ತಿರುಮಲ) and Venkatadri (ವೆಂಕಟಾದ್ರಿ); the prisoner-king Sadasiva; Hussain Nizam Shah of Ahmadnagar,
Ali Adil Shah of Bijapur, Ibrahim Qutb Shah of Golconda, Ali Barid of Bidar; the merchant Federici.

**Arc**
1. Rama Raya rules in the name of a king kept "under constraint"; inscriptions still name Sadasiva
   (Sewell ch. 14).
2. He plays the sultans against each other — for Bijapur against Ahmadnagar (siege of Ahmadnagar, his
   troops "putting up their horses in the mosques"), later for Ahmadnagar against Bijapur; he makes the
   sultans' envoys stand and walk on foot in his train (Ferishta via Sewell txt l. 5950–5990). —
   `inscription` (Ferishta, Bijapur records).
3. The "league of the five kings" (four fight; Berar absent) marries and allies; Ferishta frames it as
   insult avenged; modern historians (Eaton, Stein, Wagoner) read it as Deccan power politics in a shared
   Persianate court culture, not a religious war (Wikipedia "Battle of Talikota" historiography
   section). — `scholarship`, disputed framing.
4. January 1565: the sultans feint along the Krishna for three days, cross the abandoned ford at night
   (Sewell txt l. 6100–6118).
5. Tuesday 23 January: Rama Raya "a very old man — Couto says 'he was ninety-six years old, but as brave
   as a man of thirty'" commands from a litter; he has ordered his men "to bring him the head of Hussain
   Nizam, but to capture the Adil Shah and Ibrahim of Golkonda alive, that he might keep them the rest of
   their lives in iron cages"; sits on a jewelled throne under "a canopy of crimson velvet" with heaps of
   money round him to reward soldiers (Ferishta via Sewell txt l. 6150–6175).
6. The Nizam's front guns "charged with bags of copper money" fired at close range; 5,000 dead before the
   batteries; an elephant "wild with the excitement of the battle" dashes at the litter, the bearers drop
   him; he is taken to Hussain Nizam Shah, "who immediately ordered his captive to be decapitated, and
   the head to be elevated on a long spear" (txt l. 6180–6200). Couto: Hussain "cut off his enemy's head
   with his own hand, exclaiming, 'Now I am avenged of thee! Let God do what he will to me!'" (Dec. VIII
   c. 15). "The river ... was dyed red with their blood"; "above one hundred thousand infidels were
   slain" (Ferishta).
7. The city: the news arrives; Tirumala carries off King Sadasiva and the treasure (Sewell: "Five hundred
   and fifty elephants, laden with treasure" — **Sewell's figure; source not given, treat as his
   reconstruction**); the hill tribes loot first ("Brinjaris, Lambadis, Kurubas"; Couto counts six
   attacks in a day); on the third day the allied army arrives and stays "five months"; the Narasimha
   monolith's limbs broken, fires lit in the Vitthala temple (Sewell txt l. 6225–6270). Modern view:
   destruction concentrated on royal and some religious sites; the "six months" figure contested (Wikipedia,
   citing Vijaykumar 2016 and Eaton). — `scholarship`, disputed extent.
8. 1567: Federici arrives; "The Citie of BEZENEGER is not altogether destroyed, yet the houses stand still,
   but emptie, and there is dwelling in them nothing, as is reported, but Tygres and other wild beasts"
   (Purchas 1625 ii. 1703). Tirumala orders Portuguese horses and "licensed the Merchants to depart,
   without giving them anything for their Horses, which when the poore Men saw, they were desperate, and,
   as it were, mad with sorrow and griefe"; Federici must wait seven months "until the wayes were clear
   of Theeves" (Sewell txt l. 6300–6325).

**Goosebump moment**: the litter-bearers dropping the ninety-year-old regent in the path of a maddened
elephant, and his head going up on a spear in sight of his own army. Evidence: Ferishta (Briggs iii.
414 ff., writing c. 1600 from Bijapur sources) and Couto (Portuguese, contemporary). Two independent
traditions agree on capture and beheading by/for Hussain Nizam Shah; they differ on who swung the sword.

**Dramatised vs recorded**: Recorded — the battle sequence (Ferishta), Rama Raya's age (Couto; Wikipedia
gives b. 1484 → 80–81, so his age is **disputed**), Hussain's words (Couto), the ruined city (Federici).
Dramatised — the "treachery of the Gilani brothers" (Muslim officers changing sides mid-battle) rests on
Federici's hearsay and later nationalist retellings; "many scholars reject" it (Wikipedia). Avoid it or
flag it as rumour in the story. The hundred-million-sterling treasure and 550 elephants are Sewell's
colour.

**Why it grips adults**: an octogenarian statesman who out-schemed four sultans for twenty years and lost
everything in an afternoon; the city's death seen through a Venetian merchant who was not paid for his
horses.

### Story 5 — "Two Brothers and a Hermit": Hakka, Bukka and the founding (1334–1346) — weakest base
**People**: Harihara/Hakka (ಹಕ್ಕ), Bukka (ಬುಕ್ಕ), the Kampili Raya, Muhammad bin Tughluq, Baha-ud-din
Gushtasp (the rebel nephew), Vidyaranya (ವಿದ್ಯಾರಣ್ಯ)/Madhava, Vira Ballala III of the Hoysalas.

**Arc (what is actually recorded)**
1. 1334: the Kampili Raya shelters Tughluq's rebel nephew; the Sultan comes; the Raya's women immolate
   themselves; the Raya dies fighting; "eleven sons of the Rai were made prisoners ... made ... Mussalmans";
   the nephew is handed over by his next host (Hoysala?) and skinned alive (Ibn Battuta via Sewell ch. 2).
   — `inscription` (contemporary).
2. Nuniz (c. 1535) tells the same siege, then: the Sultan leaves a governor ("Mileque neby"), the country
   rises, the Sultan restores it to a Hindu "Deorao", who hunting south of the river sees a hare turn on
   his dogs, meets the hermit on the river-bank, founds a city and names it after him — "Vydiajuna"
   (Vidyanagara) (Sewell txt l. 785–815). — `legend` as recorded c. 1535.
3. 1340: Harihara is *mahamandalesvara* with authority as far north as Badami (Sewell ch. 3). —
   `inscription`.
4. 1343: Ballala III killed by the Madurai sultan; Harihara absorbs the Hoysala lands (Kamath 2001
   p. 130). — `scholarship`.
5. 1346: the Sringeri grant with the Hoysala widow present; Harihara now "ruler of the whole country
   between the eastern and the western seas". — `inscription`.
6. 1347: Bahmani kingdom founded at Gulbarga — the rivalry begins.

**Goosebump moment**: the hare that turns on the hounds — but it is **legend** (first recorded by Nuniz
c. 1535, two centuries after the event). The recorded goosebump is Ibn Battuta's eleven converted sons —
and the silence of every Hindu account about them.

**Why weakest**: no contemporary source names Vidyaranya at the founding (Wagoner 2000 pp. 300–301;
Goodding 2013 p. 87); the Telugu/Kannada origin dispute is unresolved (§5). A story here must be
openly "the legend and what the stones say".

## 4. Little-known facts (one line + source + tier)

1. Vijayanagara's first two rulers never called themselves kings: Harihara I is "Hariyappa Vodeya,
   mahamandalesvara" in 1340 and Bukka the same in 1353; Harihara II is the first *rajadhiraja*. —
   Sewell ch. 3 (txt l. 962–975). `inscription`.
2. Ibn Battuta says eleven sons of the Kampili Raya were converted and "we were companions and friends"
   with one of them — the possible kernel of the Hakka-Bukka conversion legend. — Sewell ch. 2. `inscription`.
3. Deva Raya II, asking why the Bahmanis beat him, was told it was their stronger horses and archers; he
   enrolled Muslims, gave them *jagirs*, built them a mosque in the capital and "ordered a koraun to be
   placed before his throne, on a rich desk, that the mussulmauns might perform the ceremony of obeisance"
   without bowing to an idol. — Ferishta (Scott tr.) via Sewell ch. 6 (txt l. 2336–2350). `inscription`
   (Ferishta, c. 1600; 150 years after the event — `scholarship` tier in strictness).
4. Abdur Razzaq: "In the king's palace are several cells, like basins, filled with bullion, forming one
   mass." — Major 1857 Razzak section; Sewell txt l. 2905–2907. `inscription`.
5. Razzaq's festival pavilions "could turn rapidly round and present a new face: at each moment a new
   chamber or a new hall presented itself to the view" — revolving stages in 1443. — Major pp. 35–37; Sewell
   txt l. 2975–2982. `inscription`.
6. Paes climbed a hill and judged the city "as large as Rome, and very beautiful to the sight". — Sewell
   txt l. 7604–7607. `inscription`.
7. Paes counted "twelve thousand women" in the palace, including "women who handle sword and shield, and
   others who wrestle, and others who blow trumpets". — Sewell txt l. 7405–7412. `inscription`.
8. Krishnadevaraya kept a Portuguese engineer, João de la Ponte, lent by Goa, to build a great tank
   c. 1520; Paes saw it under construction. — Sewell ch. 12 (txt l. 4968–4975). `inscription` + `scholarship`.
9. The Virupaksha temple at Hampi is the only great shrine of the capital where worship never stopped
   after 1565. — Sewell ch. 3 (txt l. 983–987). `scholarship`.
10. A Jain *chaityalaya* at Kandanavrolu (Kurnool) and a Tirthankara image were consecrated in 1395 by a
    Vijayanagara minister "Immadi-Bukkamantrisvara"; the pedestal inscription is now in the Hampi open-air
    museum. — Madras Epigraphy Report to 31 Mar 1935 (whatisindia.com). `inscription`.
11. Federici lists the trade: "from Goa to Bezeneger were Arabian Horses, Velvets, Damaskes, and Sattens
    ... and from Bezeneger they had ... Jewels and Pagodas, which be Ducats of Gold"; the people wore "long
    Hats on their heads called Colae" (the *kullayi*). — Purchas 1625 via Sewell txt l. 6333–6340.
    `inscription`. (Wagoner 1996 argues the *kullayi* and *kabayi* are Islamicate court dress adopted by
    Vijayanagara kings. `scholarship`.)
12. The Vitthala temple was still being built in 1561 (a grant is recorded that year) when the city fell
    four years later; the stone chariot's tower was removed in the 1940s. — Sewell ch. 12 (txt l. 5005–5015);
    S. Shettar via Wikipedia "Hampi" (Fritz & Michell 2016 pp. 98–103). `inscription` + `scholarship`.
13. Rama Raya's brother Tirumala still issued orders "after obtaining sanction at the feet of
    Sadasiva-deva Maharaya" in 1566–67 — a year after the catastrophe, the fiction of Sadasiva's kingship
    was kept up. — Hultzsch via Sewell ch. 14. `inscription`.

## 5. Disputed or uncertain points

- **Origin of Hakka and Bukka**: (a) Telugu/Kakatiya treasury officers captured at Warangal, converted,
  sent back by Tughluq, reconverted by Vidyaranya (Sewell 1900 p. 23; Venkataramanayya 1938; Nilakanta
  Sastri & Venkataramanayya 1946; sources are Sanskrit/Telugu texts c. 200 years later — Vidyaranya
  Kalajnana, Vidyaranya Vrittanta, Rajakalanirnaya); (b) Kannada/Hoysala officers of Vira Ballala III
  (Rice, Saletore, Heras 1927, Desai, Kamath 2001 pp. 157–161; argued from Kannada titles, the 1346
  Sringeri grant with the Hoysala widow, Ballappa Dandanayaka's marriage to Harihara's daughter); (c)
  Kuruba shepherds (Couto's and Barbosa's tradition; Dhere 2011 on Vitthala and pastoralist cults).
  Sewell himself offers a blend (Kuruba treasury officers at Warangal → Anegundi). **Unresolved.** Source:
  Wikipedia "Origin of the Vijayanagara Empire" (used as citation index only).
- **Vidyaranya's role and the 1336 date**: no contemporary inscription or Muslim chronicle mentions him at
  the founding; earliest texts are c. 200 years later; the Sringeri copper plates with the 1336 date are
  argued to be 16th-century fabrications by the matha (Kulke 1985; Goodding 2013 pp. 87–89; Wagoner 2000
  pp. 300–301). Vidyaranya's earliest epigraphic mention is 25 Oct 1375 (Clark 2006 p. 205); he was
  jagadguru c. 1374/80–1386 (Slaje 1998 p. 115). Even his identity with Madhava (Sayana's brother) is
  debated (Dalal 2010; Clark 2006 p. 208). **Legend.**
- **Bukka's letter to the Chinese emperor (1374)**: repeated on Wikipedia ("It is said that Bukka also
  sent a mission to China") without a citation; Sewell does not mention it; the NUS Ming Shi-lu database
  covers Southeast Asia only and could not be searched in this pass. The usual secondary citation is
  Nilakanta Sastri's *History of South India* (not checked). **UNVERIFIED — do not use until the Ming
  Shi/Ming Shilu entry (Hongwu 7 = 1374) is located and the exact wording of any "letter" is known.**
- **Harihara I's death**: c. 1343 (Sewell, from Nuniz's seven-year reign) vs 1355 (Lakshmi 1958).
- **Bukka I's 1368 Shravanabelagola decree** reconciling Jains and Vaishnavas ("no difference between the
  Vaishnava and Jaina darshanas") — widely cited to EC II (Shravanabelagola) but **not verified in this
  pass**; locate the SB number before use.
- **Deva Raya II's reign dates**: 1423 vs 1424 start (Wikipedia infobox vs text).
- **Which festival Razzaq saw**: he says Mahanavami in Rajab (= Karttika 1443), lasting three days from
  the full moon; Sewell argues it was the New Year feast or a slip of the pen (txt l. 2963–2985).
- **Raichur year**: 1520 (Sewell, from Paes's calendar and Portuguese governors' movements) vs 1522
  (Nuniz) vs AH 927 = 1521 (Ferishta). Sewell's numbers for the army (736,000 + 550 elephants; Faria y
  Souza: 733,000 foot, 35,000 horse, 586 elephants, 20,000 courtesans) are chroniclers' figures, not
  counts.
- **Who poisoned Tirumala Raya**: Nuniz reports the king's conviction that Timmarasu's son did; no
  independent evidence.
- **Krishnadevaraya's death-day**: 17 Oct 1529 from an unpublished 2025 inscription reading; year secure,
  day pending.
- **Tenali Rama (ತೆನಾಲಿ ರಾಮ / Tenali Ramakrishna)**: a real Telugu poet (author of *Panduranga
  Mahatmyam*), but the jester tales and the friendship with Krishnadevaraya are folk tradition with no
  16th-century source; several Telugu literary historians (Sitapati; Chenchiah & Bhujanga Rao, *A History
  of Telugu Literature*, 1928) date his main work after 1529, to Rama Raya's court — i.e. he may not have
  been at Krishnadevaraya's court at all. The English Wikipedia article carries a banner warning of
  unverified/AI-generated text. **Legend only.**
- **Ashtadiggajas** (ಅಷ್ಟದಿಗ್ಗಜರು): the "eight elephants" list (Peddana, Timmana, Mallana, Dhurjati,
  Ramabhadra, Surana, Bhattumurti, Tenali Ramakrishna) is a later canon (Chenchiah 1928 p. 69 via
  Wikipedia); Peddana and Timmana are securely Krishnadevaraya's; the rest are traditional.
- **Hazara Rama temple date**: Sewell (following Rea) says Krishnadevaraya "commenced" it in 1513; Fritz &
  Michell 2016 pp. 84–86 attribute it to Deva Raya I, early 15th century, with a Kannada inscription of his
  there. Prefer the modern attribution.
- **Krishna temple date**: 1513 (Sewell) vs 1515 (Fritz & Michell via Wikipedia).
- **Talikota**: name (the fight was near Rakkasagi and Tangadigi villages south of the Krishna, c. 25 miles
  from Talikota; some say Bannihatti), date (23 vs 26 Jan 1565), numbers (Ferishta's 900,000 foot / 45,000
  horse / 2,000 elephants / 100,000 slain are not counts), the "treacherous Muslim generals" story
  (rejected by many scholars), the extent of the sack (Sewell's "never ... in the history of the world"
  vs modern view of targeted destruction), Rama Raya's age (96 per Couto; c. 80 per modern dating), and
  the religious framing (Sewell's "Hindu bulwark" vs Eaton/Stein/Wagoner's Deccan-politics reading).
- **Catalan Atlas 1375 "King of Vijayanagar"**: the Commons crop so titled is the uploader's
  identification; the atlas caption was not checked. **UNVERIFIED.**
- **Post-1572 Aravidu dates**: Wikipedia-only in this pass.

## 6. Visual references for accurate AI images

### Dress and body (by period)
- **1440s king**: "robe of green satin, around his neck ... a collar, composed of pearls of beautiful
  water and other splendid gems ... olive complexion ... thin ... rather tall ... slight down [on the
  cheeks], but there was no beard on his chin" — Razzaq, Major 1857 pp. 30–31. `inscription`.
- **c. 1516 king**: "a cap of gold brocade two spans long" — Barbosa via Sewell txt l. 3725. `inscription`.
- **1520 Krishnadevaraya**: "medium height ... fair complexion ... rather fat than thin ... signs of
  small-pox" (Paes); rides out "clothed in the many rich white cloths ... with two umbrellas of state all
  gilded and covered with crimson velvet" (Paes, Sewell txt l. 8179–8183); officials wear tunics
  (*cabayas*) and "place their hands in the sleeves of their tunics" (txt l. 7453). `inscription`.
- **Tall conical cap (kullayi/ಕುಳ್ಳಾಯಿ)**: Federici's "long Hats ... called Colae"; see the 1518 bronzes
  at Tirumala and the Chandragiri museum copies (king and queens in *anjali*, king in tall cap). Wagoner
  1996 for the Islamicate origin of *kabayi* + *kullayi*. `inscription` (bronzes).
- **Commoners**: "go quite naked, with the exception of a piece of cloth about their middle" (Barbosa);
  Nikitin: "all naked and bare-footed. They carry a shield in one hand and a sword in the other" (Sewell
  txt l. 3275–3277). `inscription`.
- **Queens**: unseen in public, "carried in litters shut up and closed", eunuch guards "three or four
  hundred" (Paes, txt l. 7400–7404).

### Armour and weapons
- Infantry "wearing quilted tunics" (*laudes/laudeis* — Paes, txt l. 7921, 8138, 8156, Sewell notes
  443/451); shields "with many flowers of gold and silver ... figures of tigers ... others black and (so
  polished that) you can see into them as into a mirror"; archers with "bows plated with gold and silver";
  "daggers at their waists and battle-axes"; "musqueteers with their musquets and blunderbusses and their
  thick tunics"; the Moors "with their shields, javelins, and Turkish bows, with many bombs and spears and
  fire-missiles" (Paes, txt l. 8160–8178). `inscription`.
- Bahmani side 1470s (Nikitin): elephants "clad in ornamental plates of steel", "large scythes ...
  attached to the trunks and tusks", carrying "a citadel, and in the citadel twelve men in armour with
  guns and arrows" (txt l. 3277–3283). Barbosa likewise: elephants "have long swords fastened to their
  trunks in battle". `inscription`.
- Artillery: Bijapur 1520 lost "four hundred heavy cannon" with "nine hundred" carriages (Nuniz); 1565
  the Nizam's "six hundred pieces of ordnance ... in three lines" and guns fired "charged with bags of
  copper money" (Ferishta). Matchlocks are Portuguese-introduced c. 1510–20 (Figueiredo's twenty
  musketeers); Raichur's defenders "had never seen men killed with firearms" (Nuniz). `inscription`.
- Madurai 1370: bows, arrows, javelins, maces, swords, elephants, horses; "coat of mail" on elephant
  riders (Madhuravijayam concl. canto v. 13). `inscription`.

### Horses and elephants
- War horses imported from Ormuz/Arabia via Goa; "four thousand horses of Ormuz" in the Raichur spoil
  (Nuniz); Federici's horse trade. The king's eleven ceremonial horses and four elephants at Mahanavami
  (Paes). Razzaq: "more than a thousand elephants ... resembling mountains". Elephant stables at Hampi
  (eleven domed chambers; Fritz & Michell 2016 pp. 82–83). `inscription`.

### Ships
- Razzaq sails Hormuz → Calicut → Mangalore; Paes/Nuniz come via Goa. No Vijayanagara navy is described
  by these sources; use Arab/Indian Ocean dhows and Portuguese naus for coast scenes. Razzaq says the king
  claims "three hundred ports, each of which is equal to Calicut" (Major p. 22). `inscription`.

### Architecture (Hampi/ಹಂಪಿ; UNESCO 1986)
- Virupaksha temple (ವಿರೂಪಾಕ್ಷ): pre-Vijayanagara core; 1510 small gopuram; Krishnadevaraya's
  mandapa extension (Fritz & Michell 2016 pp. 58–65). Paes's description of the main temple with gilded
  copper roof animals "that look like tigers", 2,500–3,000 oil lamps, and a six-armed white alabaster
  goddess with a buffalo under her feet (txt l. 7575–7600).
- Krishna temple (1513/15); Narasimha monolith (1528, 6.7 m); Sasivekalu/Kadalekalu Ganesha; Hazara
  Rama (ಹಜಾರ ರಾಮ, early 15th c., Ramayana friezes inside, Mahanavami procession friezes outside);
  Mahanavami Dibba (ಮಹಾನವಮಿ ದಿಬ್ಬ) = Paes's "House of Victory" platform (the wooden superstructure
  burnt 1565 — Dallapiccola 1998 p. 27); Lotus Mahal and Elephant Stables (undated, Indo-Islamic
  cusped arches); Queen's Bath (probably a public bath); Vitthala (ವಿಠ್ಠಲ) with stone chariot (Garuda
  shrine; tower removed 1940s) and 56 "musical" pillars (local tradition only); Pattabhirama (early
  16th c.). Fritz & Michell 2016; Verghese 2002 (page refs in Wikipedia "Hampi").
- City plan per Razzaq: seven concentric walls, upright stones "of the height of a man" outside the
  first; four bazaars by the palace; stone water channels (Major p. 23–25). Paes: "three fortresses" plus
  the palace, triumphal cars, markets every evening, "the Moorish quarter ... at the very end of the
  city" (txt l. 7560–7600).
- Hampi image sets on Commons: Category:Hampi (12 subcategories incl. "Group of monuments at Hampi",
  "Hemakuta Hill", "Aerial photographs of Hampi", "Stereoscopic images of Hampi"); Category:Architecture
  of the Vijayanagara Empire.

### Anachronisms to avoid
- Firearms before c. 1500 at Vijayanagara (Bahmani guns are attested from the 15th c.; Vijayanagara
  matchlocks only with the Portuguese); no flintlocks, no Mughal-style *jezails*.
- Mughal dress, *jama* and Rajput turbans; Peshwa-era Maratha turbans; British-era Mysore *peta*.
- The whitewashed, plastered modern look of the Virupaksha gopuram for a 1520 scene (it was painted/gilded:
  Paes says gate and roof "covered with copper and gilded").
- The stone chariot without its tower (removed 1940s) for any pre-1940 scene; the Vitthala complex is
  unfinished in every period scene (work stopped 1565).
- Vidyaranya as a saffron-clad Sringeri pontiff at the 1336 founding (unattested).
- Modern reconstructed flags; saffron banners with "Hindu" iconography.
- Tenali Rama in Krishnadevaraya's court.
- Mahanavami Dibba as a bare stone platform: in Paes's day it carried painted/cloth pavilions and a
  jewelled throne-dais (txt l. 7830–7860).
- Horses as ponies: the war horses were imported Arabs/Persians; Ferishta says the Carnatic's own were
  "weak, lean animals".

## 7. Key people beyond the kings (one line each)

- **Vidyaranya** (ವಿದ್ಯಾರಣ್ಯ, d. 1386): Advaita teacher, Sringeri jagadguru c. 1374/80–86; the founding
  legend attached to him from c. 200 years later (Goodding 2013; Slaje 1998). `legend` for founding,
  `inscription` for 1375 mention.
- **Sayana** (ಸಾಯಣ): Vedic commentator, minister under Bukka I and Harihara II (Sewell ch. 4). `scholarship`.
- **Kumara Kampana** (ಕುಮಾರ ಕಂಪಣ, d. 1374): Bukka's son, conqueror of Tondaimandalam and Madurai
  (Madhuravijayam; Afif). `inscription`.
- **Gangadevi** (ಗಂಗಾದೇವಿ): Kampana's wife, Sanskrit poet of Madhuravijayam, the one near-eyewitness
  woman's voice of the whole period. `inscription`.
- **Saluva Mangu** and **Gopana** (ಗೋಪಣ): Kampana's generals; Ramabhyudayam gives Mangu the Sultan's
  death; Gopana is credited in Srirangam tradition with the Ranganatha restoration. `scholarship`/`legend`.
- **Lakkanna Dandesa** (ಲಕ್ಕಣ್ಣ ದಂಡೇಶ): Deva Raya II's minister, Virasaiva author of
  Shivatattva-chintamani; styled himself the king's "intimate friend" (Indian Antiquary 1928); led an
  expedition to Ceylon (Farooqui 2011 via Wikipedia). `inscription`/`scholarship`.
- **Kumara Vyasa** (ಕುಮಾರವ್ಯಾಸ): Gadugina Bharata, c. 1430, "Emperor of the land of Metaphors" (Sastri
  1955 p. 364). `scholarship`.
- **Chamarasa** (ಚಾಮರಸ): Prabhulingalile, c. 1430, translated into Telugu and Tamil at the king's request
  (Sahitya Akademi 1987 p. 617). `scholarship`.
- **Srinatha** (ಶ್ರೀನಾಥ): Telugu "Kavisarvabhauma" at Deva Raya II's court. `scholarship`.
- **Abdur Razzaq** (1413–1482): Timurid envoy, eyewitness of 1443. `inscription`.
- **Nicolo de' Conti**: Venetian, c. 1420. `inscription`.
- **Athanasius Nikitin**: Russian merchant in the Bahmani Deccan, 1470s. `inscription`.
- **Saluva Narasimha** (ಸಾಳುವ ನರಸಿಂಹ): general who took the throne 1485; Nuniz's "Narsymga". `scholarship`.
- **Tuluva Narasa Nayaka** (ನರಸ ನಾಯಕ): Saluva's general, regent, father of Krishnadevaraya. `scholarship`.
- **Saluva Timmarasu** (ಸಾಳುವ ತಿಮ್ಮರಸು): the minister who blinded a goat instead of the prince, later
  blinded himself (Nuniz). `inscription` (Nuniz).
- **Tirumala Devi** (ತಿರುಮಲದೇವಿ) and **Chinna Devi** (ಚಿನ್ನಾದೇವಿ): the two queens of the 1518 bronzes;
  Paes's "courtesan whom in his youth he had for mistress" who "made him promise that if he came to be
  king he would take her to wife" is traditionally identified with Chinna Devi (Rama Sharma via
  Wikipedia: "a royal dancer from Kodagu"). `inscription` (Paes, unnamed) / `scholarship` (identification).
- **Allasani Peddana** (ಅಲ್ಲಸಾನಿ ಪೆದ್ದನ): Telugu "Andhra-kavita-pitamaha", Manucharitramu; securely
  Krishnadevaraya's court. `scholarship`.
- **Vyasatirtha** (ವ್ಯಾಸತೀರ್ಥ, d. 1539): Madhva pontiff, guru of Purandara and Kanaka; Madhva sources
  make him Krishnadevaraya's guru, Srivaishnava sources name Venkata Tathacharya (Wikipedia; Stoker 2016).
  `scholarship`, disputed.
- **Purandara Dasa** (ಪುರಂದರದಾಸ, 1484–1564): Haridasa composer, "Karnataka Sangita Pitamaha", at
  Vijayanagara under Achyuta (Moorthy 2001; Iyer 2006). `scholarship`.
- **Kanaka Dasa** (ಕನಕದಾಸ, trad. 1509–1609): Mohanatarangini (1550) dedicated to Krishnadevaraya;
  Ramadhanya Charitre. `scholarship`.
- **Cristóvão de Figueiredo**: Portuguese factor/horse-dealer whose twenty musketeers decided the Raichur
  siege; honoured at the 1520 Mahanavami (Paes, Nuniz). `inscription`.
- **Domingo Paes** and **Fernão Nuniz**: the chroniclers. `inscription`.
- **Ismail Adil Shah** (d. 1534), **Asad Khan** of Belgaum, **Salabat Khan**: the Bijapur side at Raichur
  (Nuniz, Ferishta). `inscription`.
- **Tirumalamba** (ತಿರುಮಲಾಂಬ): Achyuta's second queen, author of the Sanskrit Varadambika-parinaya-champu
  — a second woman poet of the dynasty (Wikipedia "Achyuta Deva Raya"; **verify authorship in a scholarly
  edition**). `scholarship`.
- **Aliya Rama Raya**, **Tirumala**, **Venkatadri**: the Aravidu brothers. `inscription`.
- **Hussain Nizam Shah I** of Ahmadnagar: beheaded Rama Raya (Ferishta; Couto). `inscription`.
- **Cesare Federici**: Venetian merchant, 1567 eyewitness of the ruins. `inscription`.
- **Ferishta** (Muhammad Qasim Hindu Shah Astarabadi, c. 1570–1620): Bijapur court historian. `inscription`
  for 1560s, later for earlier.

## 8. Sources to avoid

- **Exam-prep and coaching sites** (prepp.in, drishtiias, vajiramandravi, collegedunia, indianetzone,
  anantamias, telangana360): recycle unsourced dates (e.g. "26 Jan 1565", "1374 China letter",
  "Tenali Rama the court jester") without evidence.
- **Wikipedia "Tenali Rama"**: flagged by Wikipedia itself as possibly AI-generated/unverified.
- **Wikipedia "Harihara I"** and **"Sriranga III"**: tagged for lacking inline citations; precise
  day-dates in their infoboxes (18 Apr 1336, 20 Nov 1355) are not traceable here.
- **Wikipedia "Battle of Talikota"** older revisions / mirrors (dharmapedia, bharatpedia, kiwix mirrors,
  "grokipedia"): the "treacherous Gilani brothers" story and inflated casualty numbers.
- **Popular retellings of the founding** (Substack "The founding of Hampi", Pragyata "Harihara, Bukka,
  and the Birth of Vijayanagara"): present the Vidyaranya legend and the conversion story as fact.
- **Sewell's framing** ("Hindu bulwark", "Never perhaps in the history of the world has such havoc been
  wrought"): quote him for the primary texts he translates, not for his judgements; modern scholarship
  (Eaton, Stein, Wagoner, Subrahmanyam) has rejected the clash-of-civilisations reading.
- **Rayavachakamu** and **Krishnadevarayana Dinachari** as contemporary records: the first is c. 1600
  Madurai, the second a 17th-century copy of unknown authorship.
- **Modern "Vijayanagara flag" images on Commons**: reconstructions.
- **Dealer/auction coin pages** for dating: Baldwin's "possibly posthumous" vs Sarmaya's reign-dating
  disagree; use Sarmaya/museum pages for images, not for attributions.
- **Any page giving Rama Raya's age as both 96 and 80 without flagging the conflict** (Couto vs modern).
