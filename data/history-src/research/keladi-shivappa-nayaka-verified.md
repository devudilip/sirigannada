# Shivappa Nayaka of Keladi (ಶಿವಪ್ಪ ನಾಯಕ), 1645 to 1660: verification pass (verbatim sources)

Prepared 2026-11-08 for series `keladi` (ಕೆಳದಿ ನಾಯಕರು), story 1 (`shivappa-nayaka`). It goes with `keladi.md`.

Everything below was read this session in the editions named; nothing is from memory. The quotations are verbatim from the archive.org OCR text (djvu.txt), with obvious OCR breakage silently repaired: line-end hyphens rejoined, running heads removed, doubled spaces collapsed. Kannada quotations from the 1921 KNV keep the OCR's spelling where it is readable; "..." marks omissions.

Each item gives the URL, the quotation and a tier (`inscription` / `scholarship` / `legend`). Anything not reached is marked UNVERIFIED. The source texts were saved in the session scratchpad, not in the repo.

Modern works still in copyright are cited by page and paraphrased, never copied: Swaminathan 1957 and Hayavadana Rao 1943 (beyond short phrases).

## Tier note

There is no eyewitness of Shivappa himself apart from the 1650 English letters. Della Valle (1623) is an eyewitness of the coast and the Ikkeri court a generation earlier.

The record of the Portuguese war is Danvers's 1894 summary of the Goa archives. That makes it `scholarship`, but close to the documents: day dates, counts of fleets and dead.

The Keladinripavijayam (KNV) was written by the court poet Linganna in the 18th century, about a hundred years after Shivappa. It is tiered as follows:
- its personal episodes (the cellar, the record hall, the khans) are `legend`;
- its campaign facts, where Swaminathan and Hayavadana Rao cross-check them against Dutch, Portuguese and Mysore sources, are `scholarship`.

The Mysore court work Chikkadevaraya Vamshavali (C.Vam., c. 1680) is a hostile source. Its praise of Shivappa (the homage to Sri Ranga) is therefore strong evidence, and its charges are `disputed`.

## 1. The coast in 1623 (DV, eyewitness, `inscription`)

**1a. Honavar** (DV II pp. 202–203). The town is "a small place by the Sea-side, but a good Port", formed by two arms of rivers.

> It stands upon a high Hill of freestone, and, it being very capacious, not onely the Captain lives there, but most of the married and principal Portugals have Houses in it

He also notes two churches, St Catherine and St Anthony.

**1b. Ikkeri** (DV II pp. 244–245). There are three gates and three enclosures.

> the two first of which were not Walls, but made of very high Indian Canes, very thick and close planted, instead of a Wall

The streets are shaded by "high and very goodly Trees", with lakes.

**1c. Court dress** (DV II p. 248). The men are clothed in white, with a shorter surcoat on solemn days and a gilded poniard.

> in these Countries 'tis the fashion for Men to cover their Heads either with such Caps, or with white Turbants, little and almost square

**1d. Audience** (DV II pp. 251, 255). The king sits in a porch beside a gilded boarded canopy, with his silver-adorned sword lying before him.

> The King ask'd concerning the slowness of the Ships this year, as a thing which displeased him, in regard of the Money they were to bring him for Pepper.

**1e. Mangalore** (DV II p. 301). The fort is almost surrounded by water on three sides.

> 'Tis but small, the worst built of any I have seen in India, and, as the Captain told me one day when I visited him, may rather be termed the House of a Gentleman than a Fort.

RICE II p. 433 confirms the Portuguese embassy of 1623 to Venkatappa Nayaka.

## 2. The house (`legend` for the origin, `scholarship` for the rest)

**2a. Origin** (RICE II p. 431):

> Bhadraiya, a Malava Gauda of Keladi, is said to have discovered a hidden treasure, which, after sacrificing two of his slaves, he took possession of, and therewith built a fort.

EC VI intro p. 23 gives the Yedava-Murari form of the story: two slaves offered themselves as the sacrifice on condition that their names be kept. The tier is `legend`.

**2b. The grant.** RICE II pp. 431–432: "Sadasiva Nayak received that name from Sadasiva Raya of Vijayanagar, in reward for his services against the forces of Ahmednagar", with the government of Barkur, Mangalore and Chandragutti. The tier is `scholarship`.

**2c. Capitals** (RICE II p. 431):

> His successor transferred the capital to Ikkeri ... in 1639, during the reign of Virabhadra Nayak, the capital was again removed and established at Bednur.

RICE II p. 463 adds that it was "originally a small village, called, from the nature of the forest around, Bidaru halli, bamboo village", and that "About 1640, under the name of Bidarur or Bidanur (Bednur), it became the capital". SCM p. 72 gives 1646. EFI p. 75 fn says "about 1640". This is `disputed`.

**2d. Faith.** RICE II p. 431 and EC VI intro p. 23: "They were the principal dynasty holding the Lingayit faith." They were also patrons of Sringeri (§6).

**2e. Vijayanagara's fall.** RICE II p. 433: "It was during the reign of Sankanna II that the Vijayanagar empire was overthrown by a confederacy of Muhammadan powers."

## 3. Shivappa's line and rise

**3a. Lineage** (EC VII, Shimoga 81, A.D. 1668, translation p. 31; `inscription`):

> Somasekhara-Nayak, son of Sivappa-Nayak, grandson of Sidapa-Nayak, great-grandson of S'ankanna-Nayak, born in the line of Keladi-Sadasiva-Raya-Nayak

EC VII intro p. 43 has the list "from my Gazetteer of Mysore, II, 432", with the entry "8. S'ivappa-Nayaka, grandson of 4 [Sankanna II], and son of Siddappa Nayaka 1645–1660; 9. Venkatappa-Nayaka II, younger brother of 8, 1660–1661". KNV VII p. 106 makes him the eldest son of Siddappa Nayaka, son of Chikka Sankanna.

**3b. Dalavai** (BUCH III p. 126, the family history as told at Bednur in 1801; `scholarship`):

> the whole business of the country was managed by their cousin Sivuppa, who acted as Dalawai, or minister. On their death without children, he succeeded to the sovereignty as lawful heir, and seems to have been the greatest prince of the house.

RICE II p. 434: "Sivappa Nayak, his general, subsequently subdued Tulava, and Bairasa Wodeyar of Karkala, invading Malayala and entering Coorg." The same page says Virabhadra "by an embassy to the Bijapur sultan, stopped a formidable invasion by Randulha Khan".

**3c. KNV episodes** (`legend`):
- **The cellar** (KNV VII pp. 99–100). The army diwan and others plotted against him. Shivappa hid at Sagar, "ಸುಂಕದ ಸೇನಬೋವ ಕೋನಪ್ಪನ ಮನೆಯೊಳ್ ನೆಲಮಾಳಿಗೆಯೊಳಡಂಗಿಸಿ". Virabhadra then sent him to the Chandavara forts.
- **The record hall** (KNV VII pp. 100–102). Virabhadra was away at Hosangadi and Kandalur. At Bidarur the plotters had Shivappa and Venkatappa "ಕಡಿತದ ಚಾವಡಿಯೊಳ್ ತಂಳ್ಳಿರಿಸಿ ಪೊರಗೈದದಂತೆ ... ಕವಾಟಂಗಳಂ ಬದ್ಧಂಗೈಸಲ್". The brothers passed the night, and "ಅರುಣೋದಯಮಾಗಲೊಡನೆ", with Honnenayakana Venkatayya and their men, cut down those who barred them, had the fort gate opened, went out, and seized and punished the plotters.
- **The khans** (KNV VII pp. 102–103). Rustam-i-Zaman with Ambar Khan, Muhammad Khan, Kutub Khan, Ankush Khan, Kaji Khan, Fatte Khan and Farat Khan; "ಅಂಬರಖಾನನ ಡೊಂಕಿಯಂ ನಿರಿಸಿ ಮಹಮ್ಮದಖಾನನ ಬಹುಮದಮಂ ಮುರಿದು ... ತುರುಷ್ಕಸೈನ್ಯಮಂ ಸದೆದೋಡಿಸಿ". Then Mustafa Khan came, and peace was made through Shivappa.

**3d. The handover.**
- KNV VII p. 104: "ನಿಂದಿರ್ದಂತಾದ ಸಂಸ್ಥಾನಕಾರ್ಯಂಗಳನೆಲ್ಲಮಂ ನಿನ್ನ ಮುಖದಲ್ಲಿಯೆ ನಡೆಸುವುದೆಮಗೆ ಭಾಷೆ".
- SWAMI p. 86 (paraphrase): the childless Virabhadra, weary of rule, gave the government to his uncles Shivappa and Venkatappa and had Shivappa crowned. When he died his queen committed sati (KNV VI).
- CORRECTION (independent review, 2026-11-08): KNV VII pp. 104–106 as read in the 1921 OCR has the brothers visit Virabhadra at Kandalur; he tells them to run the state in their own name, sends them to Venupura and stays at his worship. His reign is counted to Margashira su. 11 (16 years 12 days), and "ಆ ವೀರಭದ್ರನಾಯಕರ ತರುವಾಯ" (after Virabhadra) Shivappa is crowned in the Venupura palace. A manuscript footnote records the samadhi of Virabhadra and his queen together. Nothing there has Virabhadra crown Shivappa in person; that rests on SWAMI's paraphrase. BUCH III p. 126 says Shivappa succeeded "On their death without children". Tier the handover `legend` (Keladi court's telling).
- SWAMI p. 87: C.Vam. p. 190 alone says Shivappa murdered Virabhadra. KNV says nothing of it, and the Shivatattvaratnakara (VI 17) says he was crowned. This is `disputed`.

**3e. Coronation.** KNV VII p. 106: "ಪಾರ್ಥಿವ ಸಂವತ್ಸರದ ಮಾರ್ಗಶಿರ ಶುದ್ಧ ... ಶಿವಪ್ಪನಾಯಕರ್ಗೆ ವೇಣುಪುರದ ಅರಮನೆಯಲ್ಲಿ ರಾಜಪಟ್ಟಂ". The Parthiva year is 1645–46. The OCR of the Shaka numeral is unclear (೧೫೬೯ or ೧೫೬೪, read as 1567). SWAMI p. 87 puts his inscriptions at 1652–1660.

## 4. The court at Bidanur (`inscription`)

EFI pp. 74–76 covers 1650. "Suapenacke, King of the Canaras", sent a Brahman to free Captain Durson, who had been seized by the Bijapur governor at Mirjan. The English party then "went to Biddrow the Kings court; who were honourably received". Of the petition of 19 May:

> At our first accesse to the King we preferred a peticion ... which the King reading seemed much to be incensed at

The matter was referred to his council. The full outcome (EFI pp. 74–76, re-read 2026-11-08): the king had stood surety for Durson's debt and freed him; Durson was honourably received at Bidanur, loaded pepper and sailed without paying; the envoy's men then seized the English left at Bhatkal and stripped their factory; the petition of 19 May was against that envoy. After months of debate the English left their cloth at the king's price, signed for the rest of the debt payable on the next ship's arrival, and two Englishmen stayed as hostages until it was paid. Foster's note identifies the king as Sivappa Nayak and puts the capital's move to Bidanur at about 1640.

## 5. The city and the rule

**5a. The city** (RICE II p. 434):

> He greatly enlarged Bednur, assisting merchants and artisans from all parts to settle there.

RICE 1909 p. 158 calls Bidanur the "central emporium of trade". KNV VII p. 108 lists the trades settled in Venupura.

**5b. Good rule** (KNV VII pp. 112–113, verses; `scholarship` as praise poetry). "ಪ್ರಜೆಗಳುಸಿರ್ವ ಬಿನ್ನಪವನಾಲಿಸಿ ... ಕರಮೂಲಧನಂಗಳನಿತ್ತು ಬಿತ್ತುಮಂ ನಿಜಮೆನಲಿತ್ತು ಗೋಷ್ಪದದನಿತ್ತಿಳೆಯಂ ಮಿಗೆ ಬೀಳಲೀಯದೆ". It adds that he abolished exactions of the "ನೆರೆವರಿ ವರಾಡ ... ಲಂಚ" kind. Shama Sastry's preface (pp. xii–xiii) says the book gives nothing clear on the shist.

## 6. Sringeri, 1652 (EC VI, Sringeri Jagir 11, Shaka 1574 Nandana; `inscription`)

The translation, EC VI pp. 95–96, describes him as "Keladi Sivappa-Nayaka, protecting Araga, Gutti, Barakur, Mangalur and other places, a kingdom extending to the Western Ocean". People had made areca gardens on the matha's lands, enjoyed the profit, mortgaged and sold them, and withheld the dues. Then:

> S'ivappa-Nayaka, inquiring into the whole matter, put them down, and the lands the profit of which they had been enjoying he made over to us, when we visited Bidurur

## 7. The shist (`scholarship`; compiled from revenue records, no document of his time)

**RICE I p. 593:**

> During twelve successive years, he caused one field of each description of land, in every village, to be cultivated on his own account, and an accurate record kept of the seed sown, the expense of culture, and the quantity and value of the produce.

The passage goes on: averages struck; one khandaga (of 50 seers) valued at one fanam; the Sarkar share one third of the gross produce; five classes with two rates each; gardens measured with a rod "the length of the stone steps at the Ikkeri Aghoresvara devasthan (18 feet 6 inches English exactly)". EC VIII intro p. 15 (1904) instead says "The distance between the central pillars was adopted as the standard measure for garden land" (same 18 ft 6 in): disputed.

**RICE I p. 594.** "The shist continued for thirty-nine years from 1660". After that came additions in 1700 (Chinnammaji's dasoha), 1736 and 1753.

**RICE I p. 602.** Hereditary property and fixed money rents are "attributed to Sivappa Nayaka", and are "said to have continued without augmentation until the conquest by Haidar Ali; there is reason, however, to believe" that they were considerably enhanced by aids. After 1799:

> the landholders of Bednur attempted to stipulate for the restoration of the ancient rates of land-tax of Sivappa Nayak

**RICE I pp. 613–614:**

> In Nagar, Sivappa Nayaka made an assessment in the year 1660, called shist, on the lands. This assessment was a most judicious one, consistent with the localities and general condition of the country.

The later patti cesses nearly doubled the revenue.

**BUCH III p. 279** (Nagar, 1801):

> The rent is paid in money, according to a valuation made by Sivuppa, of the Kilidi family

The rent is "less than one sixth of the produce" on the best land. BUCH III p. 299: "first determined by Sivuppa Nayaka".

## 8. The Portuguese forts

**8a. Before the war** (SWAMI pp. 96–97, from the Portuguese Instrucoes; paraphrase):
- a demand to dismantle Barcelor (Kundapura);
- a Portuguese counter-demand for the island of Cambolim;
- the 1649 coastal cruise, with Goa warning its captain;
- an elephant sent as a present in 1649;
- the Portuguese fortified Mangalore against his wishes in 1652, and he declared war.

**8b. Decay** (DANVERS II p. 302):

> Mangalor was now reported to be in a dangerous state, owing to the decay of its fortifications and to the fact that all the surrounding country was in the possession of the King of Kanara. All the Portuguese fortresses in Kanara were in a similar state, but as none of them possessed any commerce it was impossible to repair them.

(The earlier copy of this quotation stopped before "but as none…"; the full sentence is restored. Danvers dates it 1651–52 in the margin.)

**8c. Cambolim/Gangolli** (DANVERS II pp. 303–304, verbatim). This is the turning point of the book:

> It appears that on the 14th June, 1652, the fort of Cambolin was attacked by Seva Panaique. Barcelor having surrendered to his arms, he was able to send reinforcements to Cambolin, to the extent of 8,000 men and ten pieces of artillery, and a regular siege of that fort was commenced on the 6th September. Onor and Mangalor were also invested by the enemy ... During the siege of Cambolin, a Portuguese fleet arrived there on the 25th October, and fought with the enemy, but it appears to have been repulsed with the loss of some vessels. Thirty-five days later another Portuguese fleet arrived, which, however, retired after doing some damage to the enemy. On the 15th January, 1653, a third fleet arrived, but it appears to have caused little loss to the enemy, since two days later the garrison withdrew, and went on board the fleet, taking with them all they could carry, and leaving the fortress entirely dismantled. During the siege seventy Portuguese were killed; but all the circumstances attending the abandonment of the place appeared so peculiar that an inquiry was instituted into the matter, which only resulted in the conclusion that the fortress was not in a defensible state.

Danvers does not say who dismantled the fort. At the inquiry a witness blamed the commander of the relief fleet (Danvers p. 304); the book discloses this in scene 10's dramatised note and keeps the prose to "left it dismantled".

Danvers's footnote: "This was, no doubt, Shivappa Naik, a Bednur chief, who, between 1648 and 1670, added to his dominions the whole of South Kanara".

SWAMI p. 57 identifies Cambolim as Gangolli. The fort stood half a mile north of the village, on the sand strip between sea and river, cut off from the mainland by a canal. SCM p. 9 describes the Kundapura estuary. SWAMI prints "80,000" where Danvers has 8,000.

**8d. Honavar.**
- KANARA p. 125: "In 1653, the Bednur chief with the help of the Dutch, drove the Portuguese out of the Honavar fort."
- DANVERS II p. 308 (1654): "the Portuguese were forced to raise the siege of Onor", when Bijapur attacked Bardez.
- SWAMI pp. 97–98: Honavar was under siege, with Captain Francisco de Souza, and a peace mission of February 1653 failed over the pepper quota.

**8e. Mangalore.** SWAMI pp. 97, 99 says the fort of Sao Sebastiao was taken; he cites Faria y Sousa and Moraes, and is inconsistent on the year. KNV VII p. 109: "ಅಂತು ಮಂಗಲೂರ ಕೋಟೆಯಂ ಕೊಂಡನಂತರಂ".

**8f. KNV's list** (VII pp. 109–110): "ಧರೆಯವನಿಗೆ ಬಾಧಕರಾಗಿರುವ ಪರಂಗಿಗಳ ಬಲವನುಡುಗಿಸಿ ಕುಂದಾಪುರ ಗಂಗುವಳ್ಳಿ ಹೊನ್ನಾವರಗಳ ಕೋಟೆಗಳನಾ ನೃಪಂ ವಶಗೈದಂ". After that come Bekal, Chittari and Chandragiri, repaired, and a "ತೊಲಗದ ಕಂಬ" set up at the Nileshwar border.

**8g. Extent.**
- RICE II p. 434 and RICE 1909 pp. 158–159 report Father Leonardo Paes: "his possessions extended from the Tudry river to Kasargod or Nilesvar; and that he had a standing army of from forty to fifty thousand men", with more than 30,000 Christians from Goa and Salsette. Paes's own text is UNVERIFIED.
- KANARA p. 125, from Baldaeus about 1660: "The boundary between Bijapur and Shivappa Naik of Kanara, was the Mirjan river."
- SWAMI p. 99: master of the coast from Mirjan to Kasaragod.

**8h. Afterwards.** KANARA p. 126 records the treaty of 20 April 1671, which allowed Portuguese factories only, at Honavar, Barkur and Mangalore. KANARA p. 307 says of Basavarajadurga island: "It was fortified all round with a stone wall with guns mounted on towers by the great Shivappa Naik of Bednur".

## 9. Sri Ranga Raya (`scholarship`)

- **The homage.** HR I p. 151, quoting C.Vam. pp. 190–191 (the Mysore court, hostile to Shivappa): about 1656 Shivappa "inquired after the whereabouts of, and traced out, the fugitive Emperor Sri-Ranga VI, paid his homage to him and afforded him an asylum". The Kannada is "kandu kalgeragi-yodagondu bandu". He stayed at Bednur from about 1656 to 1659.
- **Belur.** HR I pp. 155–156 and KNV VII p. 114: Belur and Hassan were taken from Bijapur and Krishnappa Nayaka of Belur (aided by Mysore) was defeated, with his son captured. Sri Ranga was installed at Belur in Vikari (1659). The honours: conch and discus, a diamond ear-ornament, the umbrella Jagajhampa, and titles. KNV quote: "ಬೇಲೂರಂ ತೆಗೆದುಕೊಂಡು ಶ್ರೀರಂಗರಾಯರ್ಗಿತ್ತು ರಾಯಸಂಸ್ಥಾನಮಂ ನೆಲೆಗೊಳಿಸಿ".
- **Rice's version.** RICE II p. 434 has Sri Ranga flee to him "in 1646", with Belur and Sakkarepatna given to him, and the sobriquet "Pinda of Ikkeri, Minda of Mysore". This is `disputed` against HR and SWAMI.

## 10. Srirangapatna, 1659–60 (`scholarship`)

The sources are HR I pp. 215–218 and KNV VII pp. 114–115:
- contingents from Sode, Bilgi and Tarikere;
- a victory near Grama, with Dalavai Hamparajaiya and 16 officers captured;
- the Kaveri bridged and the fort blockaded;
- Bijapur's Bahlul Khan turned back by negotiation.

KNV: "ಕೋಟೆಯಂ ವೇಷ್ಟಿಸಿದ ಗುರಿಮಾನಿಸರ್ಗಂ ಮತ್ತಂ ಕೆಲಂಬರ್ ನಿಯೋಗಿಗಳ್ಗಂ ಪರಿಧಾನವನಿತ್ತಂತುಮಲ್ಲದಾಭಿಚಾರ ಹೋಮ ಮುಂತಾದ ದುಷ್ಕ್ರಿಯೆಗಳನೊಡರ್ಚಲ್ ದೇಹದೊಳಾಯಾಸಂ ಪುಟ್ಟಲ್". He raised the siege. Devaraja pursued him. HR p. 217 records the Mysore side "cutting off the noses" of several men. HR p. 218 calls the retreat disastrous to Shivappa: the pursuing Mysore army returned with spoils of horses, elephants and insignia. RICE I p. 365: "compelled to retreat, and the Mysore armies before long overran Sakkarepatna, Hassan".

## 11. Death and burial

- **Death.** HR I p. 218 (from KNV VII p. 116, Sharvari Ashvayuja su. 1): "He died on September 25, 1660". Venkatappa was crowned the same day in the Venupura palace. SWAMI p. 101 cites Venkatappa's letter to the Dutch of 3 January 1661, which mentions the death. The English reports of capture and ransom and the gazetteer date of 1670 are rejected (`disputed`).
- **Burial.** KNV VII p. 116 fn: "ಲಿಂಗಮ್ಮಾಜಿ ನಾಗಮ್ಮಾಜಿಯವರಿಗೆ ಸಹ ಶಿವಪ್ಪನಾಯಕರ ಸಂಗಡಲೆ ಬಿದರೂರ ಹಳೆಯ ಮಠದಲ್ಲಿ ಸಮಾಧಿಯಾಯಿತ್ತು". Whether this was sahagamana is unclear.

## 12. Afterwards

EC VI intro p. 23 and RICE II pp. 431–432, 464: Haidar Ali took Bidanur in 1763 and renamed it Haidar-Nagar; "its conquest was always acknowledged by him to have established his fortune". Rani Virammaji fled, ordering the palace fired.

## Disputes (summary)

1. **Reign.** 1645–1660 according to RICE II, EC VII, KNV and SWAMI. RICE I and KANARA give 1648–1670; SCM gives 1649 plus 22 years; BUCH gives 22 years from 1644; Sewell gives 1649–1671; Mackenzie gives 1646–1661.
2. **Capital move.** 1639 (RICE II), about 1640 (EFI and RICE II p. 463) or 1646 (SCM).
3. **Virabhadra murdered?** Only C.Vam. says so.
4. **Bijapur stopped.** By war (KNV) or by embassy (RICE).
5. **Sri Ranga's arrival.** 1646 (Wilks, Rice) or about 1656 (HR, SWAMI).
6. **Belur.** Taken in 1657 (HR) or with the 1659 installation (KNV).
7. **The year Mangalore fell.**
8. **The siege army.** 8,000 (Danvers) or 80,000 (Swami's misprint).
9. **The shist.** The trial account is 19th-century; the rents actually paid were enhanced.
10. **The queens' samadhi.** Whether they died with him is unclear.

## Real depictions

No contemporary portrait of Shivappa Nayaka is known. The statue at Shivamogga and the modern paintings are not period works and were not used.

Near-period forms of a Keladi chief exist in the votive relief panels at:
- the Aghoreshvara temple, Ikkeri (Sadashiva Nayaka; Bhadrappa Nayaka);
- the Veerabhadra temple, Keladi ("relievo portrait of a chief").

These are known from SWAMI's list of plates only; the panels were not seen (UNVERIFIED).
