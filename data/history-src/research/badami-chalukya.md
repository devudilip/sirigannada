Inscriptions, Indian Antiquary, Fleet 1896, Beal 1884) plus modern scholarship cited as reference.

## 1. Timeline and rulers (dates with source)

"Chalikya/Chalukya", nourished by the Seven Mothers, protected by Karttikeya, with the boar (varaha)
emblem obtained by the favour of Narayana. — `inscription` (e.g., Chiplun plates of Pulakeshin II,
Fleet EI III pp. 50–53, digital edition https://dharmalekha.info/texts/INSBadamiCalukya00006; Mahakuta
pillar, Fleet IA XIX (1890) pp. 7–20, https://jainqq.org/pagetext/Indian_Antiquary_Vol_19/032511/15).

Notes on the table:
- Fleet's 1896 regnal arithmetic for Kirtivarman I / Mangalesha (Saka 489 as the hand-over year) is
  superseded; it rested on reading the Mahakuta pillar's "fifth year" against the Badami cave "twelfth
  year", which later editors assign to Kirtivarman I (EI XXVII no. 2 calls it "Badami cave inscription of
  Kirttivarman I (Saka 500)"). Use Fleet for the *inscriptions' own* dates, not his derived reign-lengths.
- Pulakeshin II's death has no inscription of his own; it is inferred from the Pallava record at Badami and
  silence afterwards (Fleet 1896 p. 26: "not likely that he continued to reign much longer after Saka 556";
  Dikshit 1980 p. 99 "probably killed" c. 642–43). — `scholarship`

## 2. Primary sources: what survives, where to read them, licence

### 2.1 Stone inscriptions

1. **Badami cliff inscription of Pulakeshin I (Vallabhesvara), Saka 465 = 543 CE.** Five lines (one prose
   line + two Anushtubh verses) cut on a hill-rock at Vatapi. Says the king, "performer of sacrifices such
   as the Asvamedha", Hiranyagarbha, made the great hill of Vatapi an invincible citadel "unapproachable
   from the top or bottom". Earliest authentic inscriptional use of the Saka era. Ed. EI XXVII no. 2
   (1947–48), editorial notes signed C. R. K. (C. R. Krishnamacharlu).
   Read: https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol27_1947-1948/panchamukhi2.html
   Licence: EI XXVII is a 1947–48 Government of India publication; treat as usable for quotation of the
   text/translation but check before copying long passages (Indian crown-copyright term has expired for
   pre-1960 government works; mark as "verify").  — `inscription`
2. **Badami Cave III pillar inscription, Saka 500 (Karttika full moon = 31 Oct 578 per Dikshit 1980 p. 38).**
   Sanskrit; on a pilaster in the verandah of the Vaishnava cave. Records the completion/excavation of the
   "Maha-Vishnu-griha" by Mangalesha, described as "well-proportioned and of wonderful workmanship", and a
   village gift for maintaining worship and relief of sixteen Brahmanas (ASI guide *Bas-reliefs of Badami*
   via https://www.exoticindiaart.com/book/details/NAM206/; Dikshit 1980 pp. 38, 47, 60 via Wikipedia
   "Mangalesha"). First ed. J. Eggeling IA III (1874) p. 305; re-ed. Fleet IA VI; better facsimile IA X.
   Fleet 1896 p. 21 n. 5. A short Kannada rock inscription outside the cave names Mangalesha "Prithvivallabha"
   and protects "the gift … to the garland makers who work for the god Lanjigesara" (ASI guide). The
   exact text of Fleet's IA VI edition was not retrieved in this pass — flag for the writer. — `inscription`
   Licence: IA volumes (1872–1933) public domain; scans at https://www.jainqq.org (Indian_Antiquary_Vol_06).
3. **Mahakuta pillar inscription of Mangalesha, 5th regnal year** (12 Apr 602 Fleet; 601 Dikshit). Sanskrit,
   Old-Kannada script, 16 lines, on a fallen column at the Makutesvaranatha temple near Badami. Saiva record:
   Durlabhadevi, "wife of his own father" (sva-guru-patni), with Mangalesha's authority, adds ten villages
   to the god's endowment. Preamble lists Kirtivarman I's conquests and Mangalesha's defeat of Buddharaja
   (Kalachuri) and his wish to plant a pillar of victory on the Bhagirathi. Ed. Fleet IA XIX (1890) pp. 7–20
   with facsimile; date discussed again IA XXXII (1903). Scan: https://jainqq.org/pagetext/Indian_Antiquary_Vol_19/032511/15.
   Ink impression image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:602_CE_Mahakuta_pillar_inscription,_Sanskrit,_old_Kannada_script,_1890_ink_impression_inverted.jpg
   Licence: public domain (1890). — `inscription`
4. **Aihole (Meguti) prashasti of Ravikirti, Saka 556 / Kaliyuga 3735 = 634–35 CE.** 19 lines Sanskrit,
   37 verses, on the east side-wall of the Meguti Jaina temple, Aihole (Hungund taluka). Lines 18–19 are a
   later addition listing six villages assigned to the temple. First ed. Fleet IA V (1876) p. 67 ff. with
   photo-lithograph; revised IA VIII p. 237 ff.; re-ed. Kielhorn EI VI (1900–01) no. 1 from Fleet's
   estampage. Read: whatisindia.com pages kielhorn.html → kielhorn12.html (translation on pages 7–12).
   Photos: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:634_CE_Aihole_inscription_by_Ravikirti,_Meguti_Jain_temple.jpg ;
   1879 photolithograph https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:634_CE_Aihole_inscription_Meguti_Jain_temple,_1879_photolithography_print.jpg
   Licence: Kielhorn 1900 and Fleet 1876/79 public domain; quote freely. — `inscription`
5. **Badami rock inscription of Narasimhavarman I "Mahamalla", 13th regnal year** (Pallava-Grantha
   script, "much obliterated"). On a rock behind the Mallikarjunadeva temple, Badami; names the place
   "Vatapi". First noticed IA IX p. 99; revised text SII XI pt. 1 no. 1 (B.K. 100 of 1928–29).
   Fleet assigned it "to the end of the reign of Pulakesin II".
   Read: https://www.whatisindia.com/inscriptions/south_indian_inscriptions/volume_11/pallavas_of_kanchi.html
   and https://dharmalekha.info/texts/INSSIIv11p0i0001 — `inscription`
6. **Kanchi, Kailasanatha (Rajasimhesvara) temple, Kannada pillar inscription of Vikramaditya (II).** Old-
   Kannada prose on the back of a pillar of the mandapa in front of the shrine; undated. After conquering
   Kanchi, Vikramaditya-Satyasraya "did not confiscate the property of the Rajasimhesvara temple, but
   returned it to the god"; ends with an imprecation and the names of the writer and the supervising
   official. Ed. Hultzsch EI III no. 48 pp. 359–360, assigned to Vikramaditya II because the Vakkaleri
   plates say he made gifts to Rajasimhesvara after taking Kanchi.
   Read: https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol3/epigraphiaindica359.html — `inscription`
   Licence: EI III (1894–95) public domain.
7. **Pattadakal Virupaksha (Lokesvara) temple inscriptions of Vikramaditya II and Lokamahadevi** — two on
   pillars of the eastern gateway, two in the east porch, one on a pillar in the house of Parappa Pujari
   (Fleet IA X (1881) pp. 162–168; Fleet 1896 p. 29 nn. 6–8). State that Lokamahadevi, of the Haihaya family,
   had the Lokesvara temple built to commemorate her husband "having three times conquered the Pallavas of
   Kanchi" (Fleet 1896 p. 29). Kannada phrase as quoted by K. V. Ramesh (1984, p. 156, via Wikipedia):
   "Sri Vikramaditya-bhatarar mume Kanchiyan mume parajisidor". The architect's name and titles (Gunda
   Anivaritachari; "Tenkanadesiya sutradhari", "Anikapuravastu-pitamaha"; honour "perjjerepu patta")
   circulate in secondary sources (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gundan_Anivaritachari, article flagged
   as under-cited) — verify against IA X p. 164 before use. — `inscription` (Fleet), `scholarship` (names)
8. **Pattadakal pillar inscription of Kirtivarman II** (trident-marked "Lakshmi-kambha" north of the
   Virupaksha enclosure). Records that Lokamahadevi built Lokesvara (now Virupaksha), her "uterine younger
   sister" Trailokyamahadevi, mother of Kirtivarman II, built Trailokyesvara (Fleet thought it lost; now
   identified with the Mallikarjuna temple); pillar set up by the sculptor Subhadeva for the acharya
   Jnanasiva from the Ganges' north bank; grant on a total solar eclipse, 25 June 754. Ed. Fleet EI III no. 1.
   Read: https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol3/epigraphiaindica3.html — `inscription`
9. Lakshmeshwar stone inscriptions (Vinayaditya Saka 608; Vikramaditya II Saka 656), Aihole Durga temple
   gateway inscription of Vikramaditya II, Adur Old-Kannada inscription of Kirtivarman I's time ("earliest
   known specimen of the Old-Kanarese language to which a definite period can be allotted", Fleet 1896 p. 20
   n. 6, IA XI p. 68). — `inscription`

### 2.2 Copper plates

- **Hyderabad plates of Pulakeshin II, Saka 534 (612–13), 3rd year** — Fleet IA VI p. 72 (Fleet 1896
  p. 23). Modern discussion in EI XXVII (G. H. Khare) which reads a related grant as Saka 552.
  https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol27_1947-1948/Khare3.html — `inscription`
- **Chiplun plates of Pulakeshin II** (undated; found by Fleet in the BBRAS library 1889; Fleet EI III
  pp. 50–53): grant of Amravatavaka village; donor's maternal uncle Srivallabha-Senanandaraja, a Sendraka;
  Kirtivarman "first maker of Vatapi". https://dharmalekha.info/texts/INSBadamiCalukya00006 — `inscription`
- **Lohaner plates of Pulakeshin II, 630 CE** — silent on Harsha; used to date the war 630–634
  (EI XXVII, Khare; see §5). — `inscription`
- **Bijapur-Mumbai plates of Pulakeshin II, dated 4 April 619** — P. S. Sohoni & S. L. Bapat, "The
  Bijapur-Mumbai copperplate grant of Calukya ruler Pulakesin II, dated April 04, 619 CE, mentioning his
  triumph over emperor Harsavardhana", *Annals BORI* XCIII (2017) pp. 205–209,
  https://www.academia.edu/34011338. Modern edition, reference only. — `inscription` (content) /
  `scholarship` (dating argument)
- **Kandalgaon plates of Pulakeshin II** — regarded as spurious (Sohoni & Bapat 2017 p. 207; EI XXVII).
- **Nerur plates of Mangalesha** (IA VII p. 161): Vaishnava (parama-bhagavata) record; conquest of
  Svamiraja of the Chalukya lineage "who had won 18 battles" (Fleet 1896 p. 21; Dikshit 1980 p. 58). — `inscription`
- **Goa plates of Satyasraya Dhruvaraja Indravarman, Saka 532** — Fleet 1896 p. 21 reads "twentieth
  year … of his conquest of Revatidvipa"; Dikshit 1980 pp. 48–53 treats it as Mangalesha's 20th regnal
  year (accession 591–92). Disputed. — `inscription`
- **Kuram plates of Paramesvaravarman I (Pallava)** — 7 plates, Sanskrit + Tamil, seal with seated bull,
  moon and linga. Narasimhavarman "repeatedly defeated the Cholas, Keralas, Kalabhras and Pandyas", "wrote
  the three syllables of (the word) vijaya on Pulakesin's back" at Pariyala, Manimangala and Suramara, and
  "destroyed (the city of) Vatapi". Paramesvaravarman "made Vikramaditya, whose army consisted of several
  lakshas, take to flight, covered only by a rag". Ed. Hultzsch SII I no. 151, pp. 144–155 (1890, public
  domain). Read: https://www.whatisindia.com/inscriptions/south_indian_inscriptions/volume_1/kuram.html — `inscription`
- **Udayendiram plates of Nandivarman Pallavamalla** (Foulkes, IA VIII p. 273): Paramesvaravarman "defeated
  the army of Vallabha in the battle of Peruvalanallur"; compares Narasimhavarman to the sage Agastya,
  "the destroyer of the demon Vatapi" (Fleet 1896 p. 26). — `inscription`
- **Kasakudi plates** (Pallava): Narasimhavarman "captured Vatapi" and "conquered Lanka" (Hultzsch SII II;
  Wikipedia "Pulakeshin II" fn. 66 notes K. V. Ramesh's critique that the Kasakudi claims are vague). — `inscription`
- **Gadval plates of Vikramaditya I, Saka 596 (674), 20th regnal year**, issued at Uragapura on the Kaveri
  after "entering the territory of the Cholas"; four stanzas on his victories over "three adversaries of the
  Pallava royal family of Kanchi" (EI XXVII Khare, pp. cited above; Hultzsch EI X). — `inscription`
- **Honnur plates of Vikramaditya I, 16th year** (670–71): camp at Malliyur on the way to Kanchi; help of
  the Gangas (Mahalingam 1977 p. 73 via Wikipedia). — `inscription` (reference)
- **Karnul plates of Adityavarman (1st year), Nerur/Kochre plates of Vijayabhattarika** (wife of
  Chandraditya, elder brother of Vikramaditya I) — Fleet 1896 p. 26. — `inscription`
- **Navsari plates of Avanijanasraya Pulakeshin (Gujarat Chalukya), Kalachuri year 490 = 739/40 CE**:
  victory over the Tajikas (Arabs) who had already overrun Saindhava, Kachchhela, Saurashtra, Chavotaka,
  Maurya and Gurjara; suzerain (Vikramaditya II) confers titles Dakshinapatha-sadhara, Chalukkikulalankara,
  Prithivivallabha, Anivartaka-nivartayitri. Ed. EI XVIII; CII IV (Mirashi). Read:
  https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/kalachuri-chedi/kalachuri-chedi-part1/earlychalukyasofgujarat5.html — `inscription`
- **Vakkaleri (Wokkaleri) plates of Kirtivarman II, Saka 679** — Rice IA VIII p. 23; Kielhorn EI V no. 22
  p. 200 ff. Vikramaditya II "entered Kanchi without destroying it" (Kanchim avinasya pravisya), defeated
  the Pallava Nandipotavarman, "grievously distressed the Pandya, Chola, Kerala, Kalabhra and other kings",
  set up "his pillar of victory on the shores of the southern ocean", gave gold to Rajasimhesvara (Fleet
  1896 p. 29; Hultzsch EI III p. 359). Scan: https://www.jainqq.org/pagetext/Epigraphia_Indica_Vol_05/032559/249 — `inscription`
- **Samangadh plates of Dantidurga, 753 CE** (Rashtrakuta): defeat of "the Karnatic army" (Chalukyas).
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dantidurga (finding aid; primary ed. IA XI p. 108). — `inscription`

### 2.3 Travellers and foreign chronicles

- **Xuanzang (Hiuen Tsiang) ಹ್ಯುಯೆನ್ ತ್ಸಾಂಗ್, *Si-yu-ki*, Book XI, "Mo-ho-la-ch'a (Maharashtra)"**, written
  646 CE from travels 629–645; visit to the Deccan c. 641–42 (Sastri 1960 p. 218; Fleet 1896 p. 24 gives
  629–645 for his stay in India). Beal 1884 vol. 2 pp. 255–257 (full quotation §3.1 and §4). Public domain.
  https://archive.org/details/siyukibuddhistre02hsuoft — `inscription` (contemporary eyewitness/chronicle)
  Note: Beal's note 43 and Fleet 1896 p. 24 n. 4 discuss whether the "capital near a great river, 30 li
  round" is Badami (Burgess) or elsewhere (Paithan, Nasik etc.) — unresolved.
- **Tabari (839–923), *History*, Sasanian section (Khusrau II)**: Khusrau, quarrelling with his son, says
  "Two years ago, Pulakesi, King of India, sent to us, in the thirty-sixth year of our reign, ambassadors
  carrying a letter imparting to us various news, and presents for us, for you, and our other sons"; the
  letter was marked "private" in the Indian language and read by an Indian scribe; it predicted the son's
  accession in the 38th year; Khusrau sealed it and gave it to Shirin (Sastri FN n. 21, paraphrasing
  Nöldeke). Nöldeke, *Geschichte der Perser und Araber zur Zeit der Sasaniden* (1879) p. 371 n., restored
  the garbled name "Purumesa" as Pulukesa; Ettinghausen preferred "Paramesvara" (= Pulakeshin, the
  Paramesvara of the South); R. C. Majumdar related the passage to Harsha instead (Sastri FN n. 21).
  Nöldeke's German Tabari is public domain (1879); the digitised copy is at Halle:
  https://menadoc.bibliothek.uni-halle.de/publicdomain/content/titleinfo/418497. The Bosworth English
  translation (SUNY 1999, vol. 5) is in copyright. The Tabari passage itself was NOT read in this pass —
  it is reported from Sastri's summary; flag for verification before quoting. — `inscription` (chronicle,
  but c. 300 years later and name reading disputed)
- **Periyapuranam (Sekkizhar, 12th c.)**, life of Siruttondar (Paranjoti): his northern campaign "razed to
  the ground the hoary city of Vatapi" (EI article on Narasimhavarman I, noted in search; Wikipedia
  "Paranjothi"). — `legend` (hagiography five centuries later; but consistent with the Kuram plates)

### 2.4 Coins
- No securely attributed coin of Pulakeshin II or any Badami Chalukya king was located in this pass.
  Numismatic sale listings describe "Chalukya" gold coins with a caparisoned boar facing left with sun
  and crescent, but attributions vary; Commons has only "File:Eastern Chalukya coin.jpg". Treat the coinage
  as UNKNOWN for visual purposes; use the sculpted varaha emblem instead (§6). — unknown

### 2.5 Later Chalukya tradition
- **Miraj plates / Kalyani Chalukya genealogies** (11th c.) say Mangalesha ruled as regent during
  Pulakeshin II's minority and "peaceably resigned the throne" — contradicted by Aihole v. 14–16
  (Fleet 1896 p. 21; Sastri 1960 p. 211 calls it a gloss). — `legend` (later dynastic rewriting)
- Bharangi inscription of Vikramaditya VI (Saka 1039) compares a burning of Kanchi by Pulakeshin I to
  later events — "must be accepted with some caution" (Fleet 1896 p. 20). — `legend`

## 3. Candidate stories (3–5)

### Story A — "The Exile Who Became Lord of the South" (ಇಮ್ಮಡಿ ಪುಲಿಕೇಶಿ, 610–634)
People: Pulakeshin II; his uncle Mangalesha; Mangalesha's unnamed son; the poet Ravikirti ರವಿಕೀರ್ತಿ;
Harsha (Siladitya) of Kanauj; the "two" Appayika and Govinda; the Pallava king (Mahendravarman I, not
named in the text); Kubja Vishnuvardhana (brother); the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang.

Arc (8 beats, each tied to a verse or line):
1. **Jealousy.** The young prince, "sought by Fortune", draws the envy of his uncle Mangalesha, who means
   to give the throne to his own son; Pulakeshin "resolved to live abroad as an exile" (Aihole v. 14,
   Kielhorn fn. 2: aparudh = banish; he went "to neighbouring princes to seek help"). — `inscription`
2. **Return.** By "counsel and energy" he strips Mangalesha of his strength "on all sides"; Mangalesha
   gives up "both his effort to secure the kingdom for his own son and his life" (v. 15). — `inscription`
3. **Darkness before dawn.** "After the rule of Mangalesa collapsed, surrounded by enemies, the world was
   brightened again by Polekesin's splendour" (v. 16). Two invaders, Appayika and Govinda, come with
   elephants to seize the land north of the Bhima; one "learned the taste of fear", the other "received
   the reward for his prior services" (v. 17 — Pulakeshin split the alliance: Dikshit 1980 pp. 72–74). — `inscription`
4. **The south secured.** Siege of Vanavasi "whose girdle is the rows of swans on the Varada" (v. 18);
   Gangas and Alupas "intoxicated by close attendance upon him" (v. 19); the Mauryas' "rising pools" swept
   from the Konkan (v. 20); **Puri, "the Fortune of the western sea", besieged "with hundreds of ships"**
   so that "sky and sea seemed to mirror each other" (v. 21). — `inscription`
5. **The north submits.** Latas, Malavas, Gurjaras "subdued by his splendour" become "teachers of proper
   conduct" to other feudatories (v. 22, Kielhorn: they submitted voluntarily). — `inscription`
6. **GOOSEBUMP: Harsha at the Narmada.** "Harsha, whose lotus-feet were arrayed with the rays of the jewels
   of the diadems of hosts of feudatories … through Him had his mirth (harsha) melted away by fear, having
   become loathsome through his rows of lordly elephants fallen in battle" (v. 23, Kielhorn's rendering,
   quoted in short phrases above; pun on Harsha's name). Xuanzang, writing of the same king a few years
   later, from the other side: "Siladitya Maharaja has conquered the nations from east to west … but the
   people of this country alone have not submitted to him. He has gathered troops from the five Indies …
   and himself gone at the head of his army to punish and subdue these people, but he has not yet
   conquered their troops." (Beal 1884 ii. 256–257). — `inscription` twice, independent witnesses
7. **Lord of three Maharashtras.** "Almost equal to Indra", he rules "the three Maharashtrakas with their
   nine and ninety thousand villages" (v. 25); Kalingas and Kosalas "made to evince signs of fear" (v. 26);
   Pishtapura falls (v. 27); the Kunala (Kolleru) lake "reddened with blood" (v. 28); the Pallava "vanish[es]
   behind the walls of Kanchipura" (v. 29); the Kaveri "had her current obstructed by the causeway formed by
   his elephants" (v. 30); Cholas, Keralas, Pandyas "brought prosperity" (v. 31). — `inscription`
8. **Home.** "Having entered the city of Vatapi" he rules the earth "as if it were one city" (v. 32); the
   Jaina poet signs off — the stone house of Jinendra is his, and so is "the fame of Kalidasa and Bharavi"
   (v. 35–37). — `inscription`

Evidence base: excellent for the arc (a dated contemporary prashasti + an independent foreign eyewitness),
but the prashasti is court poetry: no numbers, no battle site, no date for the Harsha war (§5). Dramatised:
every dialogue, the exile's whereabouts, the battlefield itself (the Narmada is Fleet's/Xuanzang's framing,
not Ravikirti's; v. 24 names the Vindhya). Why it grips adults: a dispossessed heir returns, unites a
fractured kingdom, and stops the most powerful emperor of northern India — and we have both sides' words.

### Story B — "The Three Syllables on the King's Back" (the fall of Vatapi, c. 642, and the recovery, c. 655–674)
People: Pulakeshin II (old, c. 60+ — Fleet 1896 p. 26 notes he must have been at least three in Saka 489);
Narasimhavarman I "Mahamalla" ನರಸಿಂಹವರ್ಮ of Kanchi; his general Paranjoti (Siruttondar); Pulakeshin's sons
Adityavarman, Chandraditya, Vikramaditya I; Chandraditya's widow Vijayabhattarika; Vikramaditya's Ganga
grandfather Durvinita; the Pallava Paramesvaravarman I.

Arc:
1. The Pallava, humiliated in Pulakeshin's youth ("vanish[ed] behind the walls of Kanchipura", Aihole v. 29),
   raises a new generation. — `inscription`
2. Narasimhavarman "wrote the three syllables of vijaya on Pulakesin's back" in three battles — Pariyala,
   Manimangala, Suramara — and "destroyed (the city of) Vatapi" (Kuram plates, Hultzsch SII I no. 151). — `inscription`
3. **GOOSEBUMP:** a Pallava-Grantha inscription scratched on a rock behind the Mallikarjuna temple at Badami,
   "much obliterated", dated in Mahamalla's 13th regnal year and naming the place "Vatapi" — the conqueror's
   graffito inside the fallen capital (SII XI no. 1; Fleet: end of Pulakeshin II's reign). — `inscription`
4. Silence: no record of Pulakeshin's death; the Pallavas hold Vatapi "thirteen years" (Dikshit 1980
   pp. 128–129). The Pallava court poets compare Narasimhavarman to Agastya "the destroyer of the demon
   Vatapi" (Udayendiram plates, Fleet 1896 p. 26). — `scholarship` / `inscription`
5. The family splinters: Adityavarman's one-year plates; Vijayabhattarika reigning as regent for a child
   (Fleet 1896 p. 26). — `inscription`
6. Vikramaditya I, riding "his horse of the breed called Chitrakantha", seizes Kanchi "after defeating the
   leader of the Pallavas who had been the cause of the humiliation and temporary destruction of his family",
   restores "the regal splendour of his father, which had been obscured by a confederacy of three kings"
   (Karnul and Hyderabad grants as summarised in Fleet 1896 p. 27). — `inscription`
7. The Pallava answer: Paramesvaravarman made Vikramaditya "take to flight, covered only by a rag" at
   Peruvalanallur (Kuram, Udayendiram plates). Both sides claim the same war. — `inscription`
8. 674: Vikramaditya issues the Gadval plates from his camp at Uragapura on the Kaveri, deep in Chola country
   (EI XXVII). — `inscription`

Dramatised: the sack itself (no description survives), Pulakeshin's end, Paranjoti's role (Periyapuranam,
`legend`), the Vatapi Ganapati image carried to Tiruchengattankudi (`legend`, "no written records
substantiate the oral tradition" per https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paranjothi). Why it grips: the greatest
king of the South dies unrecorded; his enemy's boast and his son's revenge survive on copper, and each side
insists the other ran.

### Story C — "Lokamahadevi's Temple" (Vikramaditya II, Kanchi, and Pattadakal, c. 733–754)
People: Vikramaditya II ಇಮ್ಮಡಿ ವಿಕ್ರಮಾದಿತ್ಯ; queen Lokamahadevi ಲೋಕಮಹಾದೇವಿ of the Haihaya (Kalachuri) family;
her uterine younger sister and co-queen Trailokyamahadevi ತ್ರೈಲೋಕ್ಯಮಹಾದೇವಿ; their son Kirtivarman II; the Pallava
Nandipotavarman (Nandivarman Pallavamalla); the sculptor Subhadeva and the Saiva acharya Jnanasiva "from the
north bank of the Ganges"; the architect Gunda (name needs verification, §2.1 item 7).

Arc:
1. "Determined to uproot the Pallavas, who had obscured the splendour of former kings of his lineage", the
   king makes "a sudden and expeditious incursion into the district of Tundaka" (Vakkaleri plates, Fleet
   1896 p. 29). — `inscription`
2. He defeats Nandipotavarman and "entered Kanchi, which, however, he refrained from destroying"
   (Vakkaleri: Kanchim avinasya pravisya). — `inscription`
3. **GOOSEBUMP:** in the Pallava's own dynastic temple, Rajasimhesvara (Kailasanatha), a Kannada line is cut
   on the back of a pillar: Vikramaditya-Satyasraya, having conquered Kanchi, "did not confiscate the
   property of the Rajasimhesvara temple, but returned it to the god" — with a curse on whoever touches it,
   and the names of the clerk and his supervisor (Hultzsch EI III no. 48). The Chalukya who could have
   burned the city that burned Vatapi signs a receipt instead. — `inscription`
4. He "grievously distressed" Pandya, Chola, Kerala, Kalabhra and set up "his pillar of victory on the shores
   of the southern ocean" (Vakkaleri). — `inscription`
5. Three times Kanchi: the Virupaksha inscriptions say Lokamahadevi built Lokesvara because her husband had
   "three times conquered the Pallavas of Kanchi" (Fleet 1896 p. 29; Ramesh 1984 p. 156: as prince, as king,
   and under his son). — `inscription`
6. Her sister answers with Trailokyesvara (now Mallikarjuna) beside it (Pattadakal pillar, EI III no. 1). — `inscription`
7. Meanwhile, on the Gujarat frontier, the Chalukya viceroy Avanijanasraya Pulakeshin stops the Arab (Tajika)
   army that had rolled over Sindh's neighbours, and Vikramaditya II names him "Pillar of the South" and
   "Repeller of the unrepellable" (Navsari plates, 739/40). — `inscription`
8. 25 June 754: a total solar eclipse "visible right across India"; Kirtivarman II's pillar at Pattadakal is
   dated by it; within a year Dantidurga's plates (753) boast of beating "the Karnatic army"; by 757 the last
   Chalukya charter is issued from a camp on the Bhima (Fleet 1896 pp. 29–30). — `inscription`

Dramatised: the queens' motives, the architect's voice, any Pallava sculptors brought north (secondary
sources assert it; no primary citation found — flag). Why it grips: a revenge story that ends in restraint,
told through two sisters who build the finest temples of the age, with the dynasty's own eclipse as the
closing image.

### Story D — "The Uncle" (Mangalesha ಮಂಗಳೇಶ, 578–610)
People: Mangalesha; his brother Kirtivarman I; Durlabhadevi "wife of his own father"; the Kalachuri Buddharaja;
Svamiraja of the Konkan "who had won 18 battles"; the boy Pulakeshin.

Arc: (1) a younger brother builds the great Vishnu cave at Badami, Saka 500, "of wonderful workmanship", and
endows sixteen Brahmanas (§2.1 item 2); (2) takes the throne on Kirtivarman's death (Aihole v. 11); (3) marries
into or defeats the Kalachuris ("Katachchuri" princess/campaign, v. 12) and takes the island of Revati by sea
(v. 13; Nerur plates); (4) at Mahakuta, in his 5th year, plants a pillar as "a preparatory step" towards a
victory column on the Ganges (Mahakuta pillar; Dikshit 1980 p. 56); (5) GOOSEBUMP: the same dynasty's own poet,
25 years later, records that the uncle "gave up both his effort to secure the kingdom for his own son and his
life" (v. 15) — the usurper's fate written by the nephew's court; (6) three centuries later the Miraj plates
rewrite him as a loyal regent who "peaceably resigned" (Fleet 1896 p. 21). Evidence: strong on the monuments,
thin on the civil war (one allusive verse; Peddavaduguru's "Ranavikrama" may or may not be him — Ramesh 1984
pp. 76–77 vs Dikshit 1980 p. 70). Dramatised: everything personal. Grips because the villain built the most
beautiful thing in the story.

### Story E — "Ravikirti's Stone" (the poet, 634)
Short frame story: a Jaina poet builds a stone temple of Jinendra on the Meguti hill, cuts 37 Sanskrit verses
on its wall, dates them twice (Bharata war + Saka), and claims "the fame of Kalidasa and Bharavi" (v. 37).
Kielhorn: "surely an exaggeration", yet "in the very front rank of court-poets" (EI VI p. 2). Modern note: the
Aihole text is the earliest dated literary use of Kalidasa's Raghuvamsa as a model (Kielhorn's footnotes trace
v. 7, 14, 17, 18, 28–32 to Raghuvamsa and Kiratarjuniya). Use as the narrator's voice for Story A. — `inscription`

## 4. Little-known facts (one line + source + tier)

1. The Aihole prashasti dates itself twice: "3,735 years since the Bharata war" and Saka 556 — the
   earliest dated inscriptional use of the Kaliyuga reckoning tied to the Saka era. Kielhorn EI VI v. 33–34
   (kielhorn11–12.html). — `inscription`
2. The Chalukya who beat Harsha also commanded a navy: Puri "the Fortune of the western sea" was attacked
   "with hundreds of ships" (Aihole v. 21), and Mangalesha before him had taken Revatidvipa (Redi) by sea
   (v. 13; Nerur plates). — `inscription`
3. Xuanzang says Maharashtra's defeated generals were not punished but "present[ed] … with woman's clothes,
   and so he is driven to seek death for himself". Beal 1884 ii. 256. — `inscription`
4. Xuanzang: before battle "they inebriate many hundred heads of elephants", the champions "first drink
   their wine", and "no enemy can stand before them". Beal 1884 ii. 256. — `inscription`
5. Xuanzang gives the king's name phonetically as Pu-lo-ki-she and calls him a Kshatriya whose "plans and
   undertakings are wide-spread". Beal 1884 ii. 256. — `inscription`
6. Pulakeshin I's 543 CE Badami inscription is the earliest authentic inscription in India to name the Saka
   era. EI XXVII no. 2 (panchamukhi2.html). — `inscription`
7. Mangalesha calls Durlabhadevi "the wife of his own father" (sva-guru-patni), not "mother" — Fleet took
   this to mean Kirtivarman and Mangalesha were half-brothers. Fleet 1896 p. 21 n. 1; Mahakuta pillar l. 13.
   — `inscription` / `scholarship`
8. Pulakeshin II's brother was called Kubja ("hump-backed") Vishnuvardhana; he took the eastern (Vengi) share
   and founded the Eastern Chalukya line. Fleet 1896 p. 23; Sastri 1960 pp. 216–217. — `inscription`
9. The Pallava Kuram plates say Narasimhavarman "wrote the three syllables of vijaya" on Pulakeshin's back —
   i.e., marked him as a runaway three times. Hultzsch SII I no. 151. — `inscription`
10. The only Pallava inscription inside Badami is "much obliterated", survives only in the epithet
    "Mahamalla", and still names the city Vatapi. SII XI no. 1. — `inscription`
11. Vikramaditya I's charters name his warhorse's breed: Chitrakantha. Fleet 1896 p. 27. — `inscription`
12. Vikramaditya II gave back the temple treasury of Kanchi and had the act recorded in Kannada on a Pallava
    pillar, with the clerk's and supervisor's names. Hultzsch EI III no. 48. — `inscription`
13. Two sisters, both queens of Vikramaditya II, built the two great Pattadakal temples side by side; the
    younger was the next king's mother. Fleet EI III no. 1. — `inscription`
14. A Chalukya viceroy's victory over the Arabs (739/40) is the earliest Deccan record of the word "Tajika".
    Navsari plates, EI XVIII / CII IV. — `inscription`
15. The last dated Chalukya record (Vakkaleri, 757–8) was issued from a camp on the north bank of the Bhima —
    the dynasty had already lost its heartland. Fleet 1896 p. 29. — `scholarship`
16. Tabari has Khusrau II mention a letter from "Pulakesi, King of India" sealed and entrusted to queen
    Shirin — the reading of the name is a scholar's restoration (Nöldeke 1879 p. 371 n.). Sastri FN n. 21.
    — `inscription` (chronicle; name disputed)

## 5. Disputed or uncertain points

1. **Date of the Harsha–Pulakeshin war.** (a) c. 612: Sastri 1960 pp. 213–214, Ramesh 1984 (via Wikipedia
   "Pulakeshin II" fn. 33–38). (b) **Winter 618–19**: Sohoni & Bapat 2017 on the Bijapur-Mumbai plates dated
   4 Apr 619 that "mention his triumph over Harsha" and his assumption of "Paramesvara" (academia.edu/34011338;
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Narmada). (c) **630–634**: EI XXVII argument that the Lohaner plates
   of 630 are "altogether silent" while Aihole (634) records it, so "the war seems … to have been fought
   between 630 and 634" (https://whatisindia.com/inscriptions/epigraphica_indica/vol27_1947-1948/Khare3.html,
   and the Kalachuri-Chedi volume page earlygurjaras3.html). Also: Aihole itself names no site; "Narmada" is
   the modern inference from Xuanzang's geography and v. 24's Vindhya. Xuanzang gives no date.
   The Bijapur-Mumbai plates are a 2017 publication; their authenticity has not been independently checked
   in this pass — treat (b) as "strong but recent".
2. **Where and whether Harsha was "defeated".** Ravikirti says Harsha's "mirth melted away by fear" and his
   elephants fell (v. 23); Xuanzang says Harsha "has not yet conquered their troops" — a stalemate reading is
   possible (Beal 1884 ii. 257). The Rashtrakuta claim that they beat "the army of Vallabha … which had defeated
   Harsha and Vajrata" is reported by Dikshit 1980 p. 98 (via Wikipedia) — the primary plate was not located
   in this pass; do not cite a specific plate.
3. **Accession of Pulakeshin II.** Fleet: early Saka 532 (610–11); Hyderabad plates year 3 = Saka 534 (612–13)
   → 610–11; Maruturu eclipse of 21 May 616 in year 8 → 609 (Dikshit 1980 p. 71). Use "c. 609–611".
4. **Whose regnal year is Saka 500 at Badami Cave III?** Fleet 1896: Mangalesha's 12th year; EI XXVII (1947–48)
   and Dikshit 1980 p. 38: issued under Kirtivarman I. Consequence: Kirtivarman alive in 578 and Mangalesha's
   accession c. 591–92 (Dikshit pp. 53–54), not 567.
5. **Mangalesha's accession and Mahakuta date.** 12 Apr 602 (Fleet IA XIX; Wikipedia "Mahakuta Pillar") vs
   12 Apr 601 (Dikshit 1980 pp. 47, 53 via Wikipedia "Mangalesha"). Goa plates Saka 532 "20th year" — of the
   Revatidvipa conquest (Fleet) or of the reign (Dikshit).
6. **The Persian embassy.** Tabari's name is a restoration ("Purumesa" → Pulukesa, Nöldeke; "Paramesvara",
   Ettinghausen; Harsha, Majumdar). Khusrau II's 36th year ≈ 625–26 CE if his accession is 590 (the arithmetic
   is this file's, not a source's). The passage was not read in Nöldeke/Bosworth in this pass.
7. **Ajanta Cave 1 "Persian embassy".** Fergusson, "On the identification of the portrait of Chosroes II among
   the paintings in the caves at Ajanta", JRAS 11.2 (Apr 1879) pp. 155–170 — identified the scene with the
   Sasanian embassy to Pulakeshin II. Rejected by: Foucher ("no historical scenes … anywhere in Ajanta",
   Sastri FN n. 21); Schlingloff (scene = Mahasudarsana Jataka, Livehistoryindia on Spink); Spink 2005
   pp. 181–183 ("The Persian Embassy") — Vakataka-period painting c. 462–480, "no longer considered correct".
   The Commons file itself says "proposed to be the Persian embassy group … largely rejected". Do NOT depict
   Pulakeshin receiving Persians as fact; if used, caption as a 19th-century reading.
8. **Xuanzang's Maharashtra capital.** "Borders on the west on a great river … about 30 li round" — Badami
   (Burgess, Fleet 1896 p. 24 n. 4), Nasik, Paithan, Kalyan etc. (Beal note 43). Unresolved; Fleet notes no
   trace of "the hundred convents" at Badami.
9. **Did Xuanzang meet Pulakeshin?** The Si-yu-ki describes the kingdom and the king; it does not state a
   personal audience. Fleet 1896 p. 24: he "describes, and apparently visited a capital of" Maharashtra.
   Do not stage a meeting as recorded fact.
10. **Death of Pulakeshin II.** No inscription. "Presumed to have lost his life" (Wikipedia "Battle of Vatapi"),
    "probably killed" (Dikshit 1980 p. 99). Date c. 642–43 rests on Narasimhavarman's 13th regnal year.
11. **Succession 642–655.** Adityavarman, Chandraditya (via widow Vijayabhattarika), Vikramaditya I — order and
    whether the first two reigned is unclear (Fleet 1896 p. 26; Dikshit 1980 pp. 114–119). "No evidence that
    the kingdom was partitioned among the three brothers" (Dikshit p. 119).
12. **Vikramaditya I's "three kings".** Chalukya charters: a "confederacy of three kings" obscured his
    father's splendour. Fleet/Hultzsch: three Pallavas (Narasimhavarman, Mahendravarman II, Paramesvaravarman I);
    EI XXVII/EI XXXII: the Pandya, Chola, Kerala confederacy. Both appear in the records.
13. **Vikramaditya II and Nandipotavarman.** Fleet 1896 p. 29 renders the Vakkaleri verb as "slew"; Kielhorn's
    EI V edition and Hultzsch EI III p. 359 say "defeated"; Nandivarman Pallavamalla reigned for decades after.
    Use "defeated".
14. **Did Vikramaditya II "destroy" Kanchi?** The Pattadakal pillar says he "bruised (vimardana) the town of
    Kanchi"; Fleet's footnote: vimardana may mean "bruising or destroying", but Vakkaleri says he entered it
    "without destroying" (EI III no. 1 fn.). Use the Vakkaleri reading.
15. **Pattadakal architect.** "Gunda Anivaritachari", titles and the honour "perjjerepu patta" — reported in
    Wikipedia and blogs; the Fleet IA X text was not retrieved. Verify before naming him.
16. **Trailokyesvara = Mallikarjuna.** Fleet (1891) thought Trailokyesvara no longer existed; the modern
    identification with the Mallikarjuna temple is standard (UNESCO nomination
    https://whc.unesco.org/document/162837) but is an identification, not an inscription.
17. **Lokamahadevi's origin.** "Of the Haihaya family" (Fleet 1896 p. 29; EI III no. 1) = Kalachuri; no
    inscription names her father.
18. **Xuanzang's date in the Deccan.** c. 641–42 (Sastri 1960 p. 218) — Beal's own text has no year.
19. **Xuanzang's figures for Harsha's army** (5,000 elephants, 20,000 cavalry, 50,000 foot; later 60,000
    elephants and 100,000 cavalry; "after six years he had subdued the Five Indies"; "after thirty years his
    arms reposed") — Beal 1884 i. 213; conventional, not verifiable.

### 6.2 Dynastic emblem
- Boar (varaha) "obtained by the favour of Narayana", with sun and moon; conch, chakra and mirror appear
  with it on the Ladkhan lintel (file above). Seal of the Chiplun plates: described in Fleet EI III (not
  retrieved). Pallava seal for contrast: seated bull with moon and linga (Kuram plates, Hultzsch).
  Varaha imagery in stone: Cave III Varaha panel (file above). — `inscription`

### 6.3 Dress, armour, weapons
- Xuanzang on the Maharashtra fighters: "each being armed, they attack each other with lances (spears)";
  Fleet's quoted Julien version: "each puts on his cuirass and grasps his spear" (Beal 1884 ii. 256; Fleet 1896
  p. 24). Champions "drink wine" before battle, "lance in hand"; drums beaten on the march. — `inscription`
- Chalukya sculpture of the period (Cave III brackets; Aihole Ravanaphadi; Pattadakal reliefs) shows: men
  bare-chested with dhoti and sash, heavy earrings, necklaces, armlets, tall crowns (kirita) for royalty,
  jata/loose curls for others; women with heavy jewellery, breast-band or bare, hip girdles. Use the Commons
  files above as reference. (General art-historical reading — treat as `scholarship`; verify details per image.)
- Swords: Chiplun plates praise "the sword that is held in his hand" splitting elephants' tusks (dharmalekha
  INSBadamiCalukya00006) — a long straight sword; depictions of curved talwars are later.
- Bows, spears, shields and elephant-goads appear on Badami/Pattadakal reliefs (search Commons "Pattadakal
  battle relief"). No Chalukya mail or plate armour is attested; Xuanzang's "cuirass" (Julien) vs "armed"
  (Beal) is a translation difference.

### 6.4 Elephants, horses, ships
- Elephants dominate every war description: Appayika and Govinda "with elephant troops" (v. 17); Harsha's
  "rows of lordly elephants fallen" (v. 23); elephants "had to avoid [the Vindhya] because of their great size"
  (v. 24); Kunala lake "surrounded by elephant formations" (v. 28); the Kaveri dammed "by the causeway formed by
  his elephants" (v. 30); Xuanzang's intoxicated elephant charges. — `inscription`
- Horses: Vikramaditya I's "Chitrakantha" breed (Fleet 1896 p. 27). Stirrups: not attested in Chalukya
  sculpture seen in this pass — mark unknown; avoid showing them prominently.
- Ships: "hundreds of ships" at Puri (v. 21). No Chalukya ship image survives; the nearest painted Indian
  ship of the era is Ajanta Cave 2 (5th c.): https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:INDIAN_SHIP_AJANTA_CAVE_2.jpg
  — use as the model (sewn-plank, square sail, steering oars), caption as Vakataka-period.

### 6.5 Portraits
- **No portrait of any Badami Chalukya king survives.** The "Pulakeshin receiving the Persian embassy" in
  Ajanta Cave 1 (https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ajanta_Cave_1_Persian_embassy_group.jpg, public
  domain, Spink plate) is a 5th-century Jataka scene — usable for Deccan court dress and furniture a century
  and a half earlier, NOT as Pulakeshin. The 2021 Deccan Herald note on activists' "Pulikeshi II" statues
  (Wikipedia fn. 101) describes modern imaginings.
- Script for any inscription prop: Early Chalukya southern Brahmi / proto-Kannada (see the Mahakuta impression
  and Aihole photolithograph) — not modern Kannada glyphs.

### 6.6 Anachronisms to avoid
- Pattadakal temples, Durga temple, Bhutanatha group in scenes set before 700 CE (Pulakeshin II's Badami had
  the caves, the fort, and whatever Pulakeshin I built — nothing of Pattadakal yet).
- Vijayanagara/Hampi, Hoysala, Islamic domes and arches, Bijapur-style gateways at Badami (the fort's later
  walls are Adil Shahi).
- Gunpowder, cannon, matchlocks; curved talwars; Maratha/Mysore turbans; Rajput-style chain mail.
- Modern Kannada script on stone; printed-paper letters (Tabari: palm-leaf or similar; Xuanzang: "the leaves of
  this tree [tala] are everywhere used for writing on", Beal 1884 ii. 255).
- Pulakeshin II meeting Xuanzang in person, or receiving Persians in Ajanta Cave 1 — neither is recorded.
- Coins with the king's face — no such Badami Chalukya coin is known (§2.4).
- Rice-white Dravida gopurams at Kanchi: the Kailasanatha is a low sandstone complex; the great gopurams are
  Vijayanagara.

## 7. Key people beyond the kings (one line each)

- **Ravikirti ರವಿಕೀರ್ತಿ** — Jaina poet, builder of the Meguti Jinendra temple, author of the Aihole prashasti; claims
  the fame of Kalidasa and Bharavi (Aihole v. 35–37; Kielhorn EI VI). — `inscription`
- **Durlabhadevi** — queen of Pulakeshin I, "wife of his own father" to Mangalesha; endowed ten villages to
  Makutesvaranatha (Mahakuta pillar, Fleet IA XIX). — `inscription`
- **Mangalesha's son (unnamed)** — the intended heir whose claim died with his father (Aihole v. 15); the Mudhol
  inscription's Pugavarman may be him (Dikshit 1980 p. 59). — `inscription` / `scholarship`
- **Appayika and Govinda** — two chiefs (Fleet: Rashtrakutas) who invaded from the north with elephants; one
  frightened off, one won over (Aihole v. 17). — `inscription`
- **Svamiraja** — Chalukya-lineage prince of the Konkan "who had won 18 battles", slain by Mangalesha (Nerur
  plates; Fleet 1896 p. 21). — `inscription`
- **Buddharaja (Kalachuri)** — defeated by Mangalesha (Mahakuta pillar). — `inscription`
- **Kubja Vishnuvardhana** — hump-backed brother of Pulakeshin II, viceroy then founder of the Eastern
  Chalukyas of Vengi (Fleet 1896 p. 23). — `inscription`
- **Jayasimha II / Dharasraya** — youngest brother, northern share; ancestor of the Gujarat Chalukyas (Navsari
  grant 671; Fleet 1896 p. 23). — `inscription`
- **Srivallabha-Senanandaraja** — Sendraka, maternal uncle of Pulakeshin II (Chiplun plates). — `inscription`
- **Durvinita (Western Ganga)** — maternal grandfather of Vikramaditya I, who helped him recover the kingdom
  (Dikshit 1980 pp. 128–129; Wikipedia "Vikramaditya I" infobox). — `scholarship`
- **Vijayabhattarika (Vijayamahadevi)** — widow of Chandraditya, issued the Nerur and Kochre grants, "reigned
  after his death, probably as regent" (Fleet 1896 p. 26). — `inscription`
- **Adityavarman** — eldest(?) son of Pulakeshin II, one Karnul grant of his first year (Fleet 1896 p. 26). — `inscription`
- **Harsha / Siladitya ಹರ್ಷವರ್ಧನ** — emperor of Kanauj; "has not yet conquered their troops" (Beal ii. 257);
  "his mirth melted away by fear" (Aihole v. 23). — `inscription`
- **Xuanzang (Hiuen Tsiang) ಹ್ಯುಯೆನ್ ತ್ಸಾಂಗ್** — Chinese pilgrim, in India 629–645, author of the Si-yu-ki (646). — `inscription`
- **Khusrau II (Parviz) and Shirin** — Sasanian king (590–628) and queen; recipients/keepers of "Pulakesi's"
  letter in Tabari (Sastri FN n. 21). — `inscription` (chronicle, disputed)
- **Narasimhavarman I "Mahamalla" ನರಸಿಂಹವರ್ಮ** — Pallava of Kanchi; "destroyed Vatapi" (Kuram plates); left a
  record on a Badami rock (SII XI no. 1). — `inscription`
- **Paranjoti / Siruttondar** — Pallava general in the Periyapuranam who "razed … Vatapi"; later a Saiva saint;
  the Vatapi Ganapati tradition attaches to him. — `legend`
- **Paramesvaravarman I** — Pallava; made Vikramaditya I "take to flight, covered only by a rag" (Kuram). — `inscription`
- **Nandipotavarman (Nandivarman Pallavamalla)** — Pallava defeated by Vikramaditya II (Vakkaleri). — `inscription`
- **Lokamahadevi ಲೋಕಮಹಾದೇವಿ** — chief queen of Vikramaditya II, Haihaya family; built Lokesvara (Virupaksha),
  Pattadakal (Fleet IA X; EI III no. 1). — `inscription`
- **Trailokyamahadevi ತ್ರೈಲೋಕ್ಯಮಹಾದೇವಿ** — her uterine younger sister, co-queen, mother of Kirtivarman II; built
  Trailokyesvara (Mallikarjuna) (EI III no. 1). — `inscription`
- **Subhadeva** (sculptor) and **Jnanasiva** (Saiva acharya from the Ganges' north bank) — Pattadakal pillar
  (EI III no. 1). — `inscription`
- **Gunda Anivaritachari** — reputed architect of Virupaksha (Wikipedia; verify IA X p. 164). — `scholarship` (unverified)
- **Avanijanasraya Pulakeshin** — Gujarat Chalukya who beat the Arabs 739/40; titled "Pillar of the South"
  (Navsari plates). — `inscription`
- **Dantidurga** — Rashtrakuta who defeated "the Karnatic army" (Samangadh 753; Ellora Cave 15 inscription). — `inscription`
- **Jayasena** — Mahasandhivigrahika who drafted the Gadval plates (EI XXVII). — `inscription`
- **Devasakti, king of the Sendrakas** — vassal named in Vikramaditya I's second grant (Fleet 1896 p. 27). — `inscription`

## 8. Sources to avoid (or use only as finding aids)

- **UPSC/exam-prep sites** (vajiramandravi.com, anantamias.com, prepp.in, iasgyan.in, gktoday.in, civilsdaily.com,
  nextias.com, testbook.com, drishtiias PDFs): repeat "Pulakeshin met Harsha on the Narmada in 618/630/634" as
  settled, present the Ajanta "Persian embassy" as fact, give Pulakeshin I's reign as 540–566 and Mangalesha's as
  597–609 without sources, and sometimes say Pulakeshin I received the Persian embassy. Uncited.
- **livehistoryindia.com** (Aihole, Badami, Chalukyas, Pattadakal pieces) — readable, but no citations; mixes Fleet's
  superseded dates with modern ones; states Pallava sculptors built Virupaksha without a source.
- **pragyata.com**, **swarajyamag.com**, **storyofkannada.blogspot.com**, **ithihas.wordpress.com**, **telangana360.com**,
  **magikindia.com**, **myoksha.com**, **writermanam.blogspot.com** — blog retellings; the Persian-embassy and
  "Dakshinapatheshvara" claims are repeated without primary citation (the title Dakshinapathesvara is not in the
  Wikipedia article's cited sources either — unverified).
- **newworldencyclopedia.org / wikipedia2007 mirrors / bharatpedia / grokipedia / everybodywiki** — stale Wikipedia forks.
- **Wikipedia** "Battle of Vatapi", "Battle of Narmada", "Gundan Anivaritachari" — thin citations (Battle of Narmada
  leans entirely on Sohoni & Bapat 2017 + Dikshit; Gundan article flagged under-cited). "Pulakeshin II", "Mangalesha",
  "Vikramaditya I/II" are better footnoted (Dikshit 1980, Sastri 1960, Ramesh 1984, Mahalingam 1977) and were used
  here only as finding aids to those books.
- **Fleet 1896 reign-lengths for Kirtivarman I / Mangalesha** (Saka 489 hand-over; 597–98 accession) — superseded;
  use Fleet for inscription dates and translations only.
- **Fergusson JRAS 1879** — historically important, conclusion rejected.
- **Miraj plates (11th c.)** on Mangalesha as a peaceful regent, and on an extra generation (Nadamari) between
  Pulakeshin II and Vikramaditya I — "a pure mistake, based on imperfect tradition" (Fleet 1896 p. 26 n. 3).
- **Kurtakoti plates (Saka 532), Kandalgaon plates, Aminbhavi stone (Saka 488)** — forgeries/spurious (Fleet 1896
  pp. 23, 27; Sohoni & Bapat 2017 p. 207).
- Any page claiming a Pulakeshin II coin portrait, a surviving Pulakeshin statue, or that Xuanzang "met" him.

## Appendix B — Beal 1884 vol. 2 pp. 255–257, the Maharashtra passage (public domain; quote in full)

"This country is about 5000 li in circuit. The capital borders on the west on a great river. It is about 30 li
round. The soil is rich and fertile; it is regularly cultivated and very productive. The climate is hot; the
disposition of the people is honest and simple; they are tall of stature, and of a stern, vindictive character.
To their benefactors they are grateful; to their enemies relentless. If they are insulted, they will risk their
life to avenge themselves. If they are asked to help one in distress, they will forget themselves in their haste
to render assistance. If they are going to seek revenge, they first give their enemy warning; then, each being
armed, they attack each other with lances (spears). When one turns to flee, the other pursues him, but they do not
kill a man down (a person who submits). If a general loses a battle, they do not inflict punishment, but present
him with woman's clothes, and so he is driven to seek death for himself. The country provides for a band of
champions to the number of several hundred. Each time they are about to engage in conflict they intoxicate
themselves with wine, and then one man with lance in hand will meet ten thousand and challenge them in fight. If
one of these champions meets a man and kills him, the laws of the country do not punish him. Every time they go
forth they beat drums before them. Moreover, they inebriate many hundred heads of elephants, and, taking them out
to fight, they themselves first drink their wine, and then rushing forward in mass, they trample everything down,
so that no enemy can stand before them.

The king, in consequence of his possessing these men and elephants, treats his neighbours with contempt. He is of
the Kshattriya caste, and his name is Pulakesi (Pu-lo-ki-she). His plans and undertakings are wide-spread, and
his beneficent actions are felt over a great distance. His subjects obey him with perfect submission. At the
present time Siladitya Maharaja has conquered the nations from east to west, and carried his arms to remote
districts, but the people of this country alone have not submitted to him. He has gathered troops from the five
Indies, and summoned the best leaders from all countries, and himself gone at the head of his army to punish and
subdue these people, but he has not yet conquered their troops.

So much for their habits. The men are fond of learning, and study both heretical and orthodox (books). There are
about 100 sangharamas, with 5000 or so priests. They practise both the Great and Small Vehicle. There are about
100 Deva temples, in which very many heretics of different persuasions dwell."

(Beal's n. 44: "That is, Siladitya of Kanauj (vol. i. p. 210 ss.)". The chapter continues with the Ajanta
monastery description, pp. 257–258.)
